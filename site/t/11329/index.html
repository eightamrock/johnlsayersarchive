Lou21 October 2008#2
Hi Razor – Great question, nice, concise, and to the point! :wink: I’m only speaking from personal experience of course, but will happily share this with you. The way I see it is basically as follows: Yes there are three main concerns at first, namely isolation (from the outside world), absorption (control of the sound within) and you are clearly concerned with the third, and equally vital, subject of separation between your rooms. Nice one! :) My studio is now finished (finished-ish! – proper update tomorrow) and I marvel at how many people get totally the wrong handle on this bit. :roll: The separation you absolutely have to achieve is such that when you have a gorilla in your drum room playing the heaviest groove you’ve ever heard, whilst you may be able to faintly hear him through the glass, the awesome collection of mics that you’ve placed around his even more awesome kit are not picking up what’s coming out of your monitoring. In short, dude, if you’re worried that you can still (just ever so slightly) hear what’s going on next door – then DON’T. If, however, your drum room mics are picking up noise from your control room monitoring…then you may have a problem! :shock: Then of course there’s the psychological annoyance of seeing a gorilla through a window banging seven bells out of a kit, cymbals flying everywhere, you being able to barely hear him, your brain telling you it must be louder than it actually is, and then being worried about separation. Set your control room levels loud enough to be able to properly hear your mix (without the gorilla next door) and your monitoring in the control room quiet enough not to be picked up by your mics! If it works - You know your rooms are the ‘nuts’ (From a separation perspective at least). Hope this helps my friend, :wink: Regards, Lou. 8)

AVare27 October 2008#7
razor wrote:
The accoustician is quoting 45db flanking noise through the common cement slab so we will design around that a 50- 55db wall.
Without frequencies, those numbers are meaningless. The lower the frequency, the less the isolation. Greater than ~50 dB isolation from 125 Hz up is quite achievable with a common floor. If by a 50 -55 dB wall you mean one with that much isolation from 125 Hz up, that amount of isolation is quite satisfactory. What is important with music isolation is the low frequencies. A wall with that much isolation at 125 Hz will have greater than 30 dB of isolation at 63 Hz. A very respectable figure. Andre
