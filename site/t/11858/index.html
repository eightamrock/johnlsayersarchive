What changes would you recommend to the plan? I have contractors in my house!
That's not a good time to be making design decisions! Anyway, Ian pretty much said it all. the only think I'll add is: think "room within a room" for the overall construction. Think "Mass-Spring-Mass" (or "Mass-Air-Mass" if you prefer) for the wall and ceiling construction. You need to build your actual room as an entirely separate structure, within the outer shell of the building, and there can be no mechanical connections at all between the "inner" structure and the "outer" structure, except for the floor. The floor is the only part that is common between the inner "leaf" and the outer "leaf". So your outer leaf will be a single stud frame with a couple of layers of 5/8 drywall on the outside of the studs, and NOTHING on the inside of the studs, except an empty space filled with insulation, such as mineral wool or fiberglass. Then your inner leaf will be a separate single stud frame with a couple of layers of 5/8 drywall on the inside of the studs, and NOTHING on the outside of the studs, once again except for insulation. That is the standard "Mass-Air-Mass" or "Mass-Spring-Mass" construction for studios here. It is relatively cheap, it is relatively easy to build, and it gives you excellent isolation. Do not build anything that has more than one "Air" gap in it, which implies that it has more than two leaves. If you build a three-leaf wall, the isolation will be WORSE than if you build a two leaf wall in the same space with the same materials. Build ONLY a two leaf wall, never three, never one. Forget the MLV: It is just mass. One sheet of 5/8 drywall will give you better isolation than the same size piece of MLV, and for a fraction of the cost. Forget any fancy commercial magical acoustic panels that are promoted as defying the laws of physics: they cannot. The laws of physics cannot be violated, no matter how hard you try. There is no magical material that will totally block all sound at all frequencies, that you can just hang inside your wall, at any price. (The very best you could do is lead sheeting, which is extremely dense and extremely expensive. You'd need at least an inch thickness of it, an even then it would not block everything. Second best would be a two foot thick reinforced high-density concrete wall.) Don't get me wrong: There are some good products on the market, yes, and they do work to one degree or another, but they always end up being much more expensive than a simple drywall MSM wall. Go with the tried and true, well-proven isolation that simple cheap building materials give you.
I thought the walls sounded pretty stout with staggered studs, ...
Well, yeah, it would be "stout", but "stout" is not necessarily the same as "excellent acoustical isolator. As Ian mentioned, staggered studs are still coupled at the top and bottom plates, so they will still flank, to a certain extent. They won't be as bad a single stud wall, yes, but they won't be as good as two separate, independent stud frames, with two layers of drywall on only one side of each frame. Nothing beats that.
... and cotton batting. (mind you the acoustic panels and bass baffles aren't shown in these because they aren't part of construction)
I'm not sure that I understand you: You say the the one inch of cotton batting plus acoustic cloth that your diagram shows covering 100% of the wall surface, is NOT part of the acoustic treatment? An inch of cotton on the entire interior will most definitely have a serious acoustic effect on your room! It will most likely make it sound dull and lifeless in the high end, but still boomy and muddy in the low end. Unpleasant. Yes, you will need plenty of bass trapping, since it seems to be a small room, but an inch of cotton batting does nothing to bass. That batting will be killing most of your highs, selectively, so I can't imagine that you'll need very much in the way of acoustic panels (except maybe at first reflection points). On the contrary, you'll probably need to introduce some hard surfaces to liven up the room a little.

Much less expensive, looks reasonably good, and helps to keep your room live. Let's start here - what do you guys recommend I do to FIX the room as it is now
First, do an accurate, detailed plan of your room as it is right now, in SketchUp. Yeah, SketchUp is a pain to learn, and makes no sense at all when you first start, but once you get used to it, it's a very powerful tool, and it will help you do a clear, detailed design: Best of all, it's free! Download some of the tutorials to get you started. The post your model here for the experts to look at, and make suggestions on how to improve it. You are lucky: it seems like lots of god people have taken an interest in your thread, so make the most of that! It doesn't happen very often. Next, take out that staggered stud frame up against the concrete wall. You have about four times as much wood in there as you need! Is that porous concrete? If so, then seal it with some kind of masonry sealant paint, to make it airtight. Then build that frame again, but start with a 2x4 sole plate and 2x4 studs 24 inches OC, four inches away from the wall. Make sure that you run three beads of flexible caulk underneath the full length of that plate, one down the center line, and one close to each edge. You must seal that well, if you want good isolation. the same applies to all of the sole plates for all of the walls: they must be caulked and sealed airtight. Because you need the extra space so desperately, you should build this wall "inside out" to gain space. That means that you will put two layers of drywall on only ONE side of the studs, and it will be the side that faces the concrete, not the side that faces the room. So you will have to build the wall lying down on the floor, then raise it into place. It will be VERY heavy, because it will have two layers of 5/8 drywall on it when you lift it, so you will need to plan carefully how to get it up and into place. The drywall joints should be staggered, so that no joints line up. All joints should be filled with backer rod, then caulked. You will also need to hang insulation in the cavity between the drywall and the concrete, as thick as you can afford, before you raise the wall. So you will end up with this: Concrete wall -- air gap filled with insulation -- 2 layers of drywall -- stud frame. Why should you build it "inside out"? Because you need the space! You are probably thinking that putting a 2x4 stud four inches from the wall places it even further into the room that you have with your 2x6. True. But... The interior of the stud frame is now facing the room, so you are looking at the "inside of the wall. That space is where you will install your acoustic treatment: Built this way, the edge of the studs is the start of your actual room space, and your acoustic treatment does not stick out from the wall, since it is in between the studs. If you built the wall the normal way, then you could move it an inch or two closer to the concrete, but you would have drywall facing the room, and you'd still need to build your treatment on top of that, which would take up extra space in the room. You can still do it that way if you prefer, since building a wall inside out is extra work, and hard to lift. Figure out your design carefully, to see if you need extra space on that wall. Next, your inner-leaf ceiling is currently attached to your outer leaf framing. That cannot be. Remove all the existing top plates from all the walls, and drop them a couple of inches, in order to make room for your inner leaf ceiling to be clear, free, and decoupled form the outer leaf. Remove anything that connects the inner walls to the outer walls or to the roof above, and anything that connects the inner leaf ceiling to the outer leaf walls or ceiling. There can be NO connections at all between your iner leaf and outer leaf. The inner leaf must be totally isolated from the outer: this is the concept of "room within a room". Install sway braces if you need to, for stability, but they must be proper acoustic isolation sway braces. Your inner leaf ceilings (yes, plural: you need one separate independent ceiling for each room, so if you have a live room and a control room, that is two separate ceilings) will be attached ONLY to the inner leaf walls. Put 2x6 joists across the tops of the walls. Once again, you can build your ceiling inside out to gain HEIGHT: That means that two layers of drywall goes on TOP of the joists, not underneath it, and the space in between the joists is for acoustic treatment. You gain several inches of height like this. Ceilings must be totally sealed to the walls, and the walls must be sealed all around to each other. The inner leaf must be airtight, and the outer leaf must also be airtight. There must be no connections at all between the inner leaf and the outer leaf. They must be absolutely decoupled. Then make all of your framing 24" OC. That brings down the resonant frequency of your walls a bit, which is good. You want it as low as possible.
