... then mineral fiber insulation inside that wall, a layer of 5/8 sheet rock, a layer of "Green Glue", a layer of 5/8" sheet rock, the cotton batting, the burlap
I'm not 100% sure here, but I think I'd skip the cotton batting and burlap for now. That's acoustic treatment, not isolation, so I think I'd leave that until the very end, after the room is actually finished, then measure how it is behaving acoustically in order to determine how to treat it. Cotton/burlap might turn out to be a good treatment, but it might not. You won't know for sure until you have the actual structure finished and sealed. So I think I'd be inclined to tell him that you are going to leave that for "stage two", which is the treatment of the finished room.

Attach layers of drywall in between the studs as described in Rod's book. Your inner leaf bottom plate should not touch the outer leaf bottom plate but very close is OK. The "air gap" is the space between leafs, not framing. You probably won't be able to get the drywall in there without removing some or all of your inner leaf framing. Also, beefing up your outer leaf might not even be worth it if you don't plan to replace or eliminate that existing window. Now that I look at the photo closer, it looks like that inner leaf wall is attached to the floor joists above. Not good for isolation. What are you planning for your ceiling?

xpesrx wrote:
"Can you offer any suggestions as to where we are now?"
I can only offer based on what I am familiar with as it relates to framing of this sort. If I were personally involved in this build, everything that is framed up would be demoed back down. Everything. And there is a simple reason why. You will find it difficult to continue working with either so much directly in your way physically or points in the existing framing that cannot connect properly and require a "bastard" fit to gain access to any continuity. And then the matter of your 2X4 ceiling framing that bridges directly into the existing framing of the building. Anywhere you move, you will either have to hack a piece of wood out of your way or pound another to shore up a poor construction. There is nothing worth putting that much headache into. I wish I could give you a better answer, but there is not one.

I have to second what Brien just said. I framed my studio twice before getting the actual frames where they are now, the first with a partial triple leaf, and the second with a raised wooden floor. Brien convinced me to bite the bullet and pull everything apart, and boy was I glad I listened to him. I managed to salvage almost all my wood, apart from some sills, and sister studs but, it was well worth it. It is so much easier to build and demo anything that needs it. I think it would be more time consuming too try and cut corners oddly enough. It may be a pain in the ass at first, but if your build comes out wrong, you'll realize the extra effort you could have put in, might have saved your studio.
