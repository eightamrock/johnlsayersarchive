Hello, and thank you for the wonderful forum. I'm preparing to do some tracking of acoustic instruments (voice, guitar, upright bass, possibly some woodwinds/brass) and electric guitar/bass (mic'd cabs and DI). Nothing will be terribly loud. Drums have already been tracked elsewhere. I'll be doing no mixing here. I'm hiring a top-tier mixer in a top-tier room to mix. Based on some feedback from Ethan W and others my current strategy is to put some absorption on the ceiling and back wall and put down a sheet of 4x8 plywood to get some nice reflection. For the absorption my original plan was to purchase two sheets of 2" 4x6 wedge foam (one each for the back wall and ceiling) put the plywood down and get to it. However, the place that sells the foam (foambymail.com) says it'll take 1-1.5 weeks to get here and I'd really rather not wait that long. My plan is to mic most of the sources using two mics: a close mic right on the source and a more distant one with a reflexion filter to provide some breathing room for the source. Thanks for reading this, and TIA for any and all suggestions/feedback. --David

Eric_Desart18 February 2008#7
703 and 705 are both used. Since 703 is A LOT cheaper than 705 you're better off with more 703 than less 705, for the same price (making absorbers where needed thicker, as in e.g. corners and back-wall). You can also alternative boards if you can find them. Some of them are significant cheaper: http://www.bobgolds.com/AbsorptionCoefficients.htm Putting them in bags is OK, whichever, as long as you can blow through the fabric. Fire properties are possibly a factor to concider (your decision). Covering the carpet with hardboard or plywood can be a good thing to better reflect the whole spectrum (carpet only absorbs highs). But there are different visions about that. You could easily try the effect with the cheapest hardboard you could find.

I'm no expert on how web search engines work, but I know a little bit - from experience - about "do's and don'ts" when it comes to optimizing search hits for products. I am not commenting on any of the above since I don't know (and don't want to know) the specific details. I offer this only as general advice from one who has "been there." Many search engines use what are called "meta tags" to generate hits. Put simply, these are keywords buried in the HTML (or equivalent) code of a page. Using competitors' trademarks in your page's meta tags is a BIG no-no. Any P&TM attorney will tell you so. In the U.S., it is generally illegal in the context of situations that either (a) cause confusion in the market place, or (b) redirect potential customers of a competitor's product(s) to your page. (Even if that is not your intention.) And do keep in mind that a trademark does not need to be registered to be considered a trademark. If I create a product called the SuperduperhyperübermegaTrap™ along with a website and the rest, I am protected regardless of whether I register the name. If I do, that offers me more protection. However, I am protected without registration - in the US, at least - by the very fact that I've established the brand and the name in the market. ™ = trademark ® = registered trademark (see www.uspto.gov in the US)
