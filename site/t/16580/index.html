Hi John, and welcome! First, to answer your questions straight-up:
Is it an agle really necessary between the 2 sets of sliding glass doors on the isolated tracking room?
No, not necessary. (But you might want to consider angling one or both of those walls, along their full length, so that you don't have parallel walls in the LR. Helps a bit with flutter echo.
Is the space between leaves on the isolated tracking room too much? I have heard 12 in max…..
Is it possible to have too much money? :) In other words, the bigger the gap, the better. The amount of isolation you get depends mainly on the MSM resonant frequency of your wall. That is controlled mainly by two parameters: mass of each leaf, and depth of the air gap between them. More mass is better. More air gap is better. So if you can afford 12" of air gap, that is excellent. Minimum is 4", good is 8", great is 12". This is one case where size DOES matter...
The inspector would like me a lot better if I went ahead and dry walled the entire interior of the outer shell. Am I correct in assuming that would create a 3 leaf wall or can I go ahead and drywall it all?
Yup! Put drywall on there, and you have a third leaf. Not good. Why does he want you to drywall it? What is the reason? If he can explain that, then maybe we can figure a work around.
Suggestions for diffusion in isolated tracking room? Are slat walls good enough?
Slat walls are more tuned absorber than diffuser, although they will diffuse as well to a certain extent. And yes, they should be good enough, if designed and built properly.
Suggestions for materials for audio conduit?
What do you mean by "audio conduit"? Comments:
I hope to max out in the 100 -110 range but I am sure those will be extreme cases.

Predictability is fine, as far as it goes, but reality will not match your best predictions! I wrote a long post about that to someone else yesterday: you can find it here: viewtopic.php?f=1&t=16515&p=116450&hilit=modal+ratios+for+rooms+#p116450 Long story short? Room ratios and predictions are only useful to a certain extent. You'll have to measure and treat anyway.
I had intended for absorption panels to cut down my flutter echoes. Do you think it would be just as effective as splaying walls
Both will work fine for flutter, and there is certainly nothing wrong with building a rectangular room if you want: there are lots of great rectangular rooms! But don't build it rectangular just because you think it will make it easier to predict modal behavior. Build it rectangular because you like the aesthetics of rectangles, or because you want to maximize use of a small space, or for structural reasons, or for other valid reasons, but it's a common fallacy on the internet that rectangular rooms are somehow better just because you can easily predict the expected modal behavior. In other words, if you want to angle a couple of walls in there, because it will look cool, or because you have the space to do it, or for acoustic reasons, then go ahead! The construction won't be much more difficult. I'm also wondering if there might be a better layout than what you show there...
angled framing covered in mineral wool and cloth, maybe a few hangers inside? (similar to what is on the front and back walls of tracking and control rooms)
Well "angled framing covered in mineral wool and cloth" and "hangers" are two different techniques for treating two different issues. You can combine them if you want, but you should really understand why you are doing it, and what the purpose is, not just because you saw it in another room! Yes, you can do that if you want, but the purpose of hangers is not to treat flutter echo.
or maybe just angling the absorption panels.....
Angling absorption panels is done for another reason too. Once again, not so much for flutter echo. The idea with acoustic design is to see what problems your room has, and to treat those as necessary, but not over-treat. An over-treated room is a "dead" room: it sounds unnatural, unpleasant, especially for an LR. So if you do angle your walls in your LR, then you are already helping to control flutter echo and wont need so much absorption on the walls for that reason. You might need absorption for other reasons, but at least you won't be putting additional treatment there for flutter, if you see my point.
Is the absorption (will be angled slat walls) on the front of the control room enough of a splay for that room?
depends on what you are trying to accomplish! :) It looks like you are aiming at an RFZ design, yes? You are talking about the two side walls at the front, correct? The yellow lines on either side of the desk area, coming almost as far back as the mix position? If so, then that's a good start, and will help, but you will also need absorption on your first reflection points in that area. There may be an issue with your ceiling there, too. You show a very short section of framing coming back a bit towards the desk, labeled "angled ceiling 6 deg". What is that for? 6 degrees on the ceiling is too shallow for RFZ, the framing doesn't go back anywhere near far enough into the room, and doesn't seem to be part of the inner leaf either. Maybe if you can explain what you are trying to achieve with the front end of your room, then we can help you fix that.

You shouldn't really try to duct a unit that isn't designed for ducting, and yes, you most likely will need a silencer in the exhaust duct, but once again that will be determined by your chose NC curve and isolation level.
Am I being too naive or passive about this?
Put it this way: when you isolate a room acoustically, you create two complete hermetic seals around the room, and you surround it with several inches of very thick, very effective thermal insulation. The room is double-airtight. Then you put a lot of people in there, and make them work hard (a rock band playing can work up a decent sweat pretty quick!), plus a lot of hot equipment. What you have, basically, is a dozen hard-working, hot sweaty people, inside a sealed plastic bag wrapped in blankets, inside of another sealed plastic bag... So yes, you are going to need lots of cooling in there! :) People have this other problem: they need to breathe! They need to take in oxygen and they expel CO2 (among other things!), so yes, if you want them to stay alive, then you are going to need ventilation to bring in lots of fresh air, and other ventilation to get rid of the stale air. OK, next problem: As soon as you cut holes in your room to put the ventilation ducts in, you have now totally destroyed the isolation that you spent so much money to create! Now you need to deal with the ducts. You have to put silencers in them, and you have to decouple them correctly. If you don't to that right, then you wasted all the money for isolation. If you don't do it at all, then you have unconscious musicians lying around all over the floor... (or at best, suffering in rather uncomfortable, hot, smelly, conditions: they most likely won't be performing too well like that, either!). Hopefully, your HVAC guy understands all this, and knows how to do the math to calculate how much fresh air you need to bring in to keep the musicians alive and not too "odorous", plus also cool things down to a comfortable level for the people and the equipment, plus keep the humidity at the correct level for the musical instruments, people and equipment. And that he also knows how to do all of that while still retaining the level of isolation that your room needs, and that you paid so much money to get right. And that he also knows how to do all of the above while keeping the air moving slow enough and in the right directions so that it does not make any noise at all! It ain't easy to do all that. It is nothing like an average office of home HVAC system. It has to be QUIET, it has to move the right amount of air at the right speed in the right places and the right direction, it has to control the humidity correctly, and it has to exhaust the stale air correctly, and it has to bring in fresh air correctly, and it has to do all that without damaging your isolation. I don't know if that answered your question? I would really suggest that you re-think your budget. Normally, building a studio costs roughly the same as building a house, per square meter (or square foot). In your case you already have the outer shell, to a certain extent, so your cost is going to be more like a renovation or a conversion than a build. Call a local contractor and ask him how much he charges per square foot to convert a garage into a living room. That will give you an idea of how much this is really going to cost you. Your drawing shows an area of 864 square feet, and you say your budget is around US$5,000. That means you are estimating your cost as roughly 6 dollars per square foot. I'm not sure what building costs are like in North Carolina, but I reckon they will be quite a bit higher than US$ 6 / square foot. Where I live, building costs start at about 15 times that much, and go up from there. So you really should take a careful look at your budget. Sorry for being so blunt, but reality ain't friendly to studio builders! I'm not saying you can't build a studio on your budget, and I'm not trying to rain on your party: I'm just pointing out the reality of real-world costs. This is going to be a lot more expensive than you thought, if you want done right. (And if you don't' want it done right, then why bother doing it at all? So, hopefully you can get materials at a really good price, and that all your labor will be free. Labor is a huge part of building costs, so if that comes in for free, that will help a stack. But there are other ways you can save money: 1) Forget the sliding glass doors. Those alone will eat up most of your budget, if you get the ones you need for good acoustic isolation. And if you don't get the right ones, then you wont get isolation. So just go with blank walls and ordinary hinged solid-core doors. 2) Eliminate one entire isolation wall by moving your LR right next to your CR. Save you stacks! 3) Make the rooms smaller: Yeah it would be great to have such big rooms, but your budget cannot get you there. So make them smaller! 4) Simplify the design of the LR so that it needs less treatment. (Same for the CR, but to a lesser extent.) There are probably other things you can do to reduce costs too, but that's a good starting point. - Stuart -
