Jamie M26 February 2007#1
Hello all, After much reading and a few stupid questions posted on this site, the time to start building has arrived..... My previous posts are here…. http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... highlight= …regarding the layout here…. http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... highlight= …and a few essential building tips here… http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... highlight= …so I won’t bother to go over too much ground I’ve already covered. Basically, the space is being divided into three main sections, the live room (with booth), the control room and the small office / kitchen area. My original plans involved putting a toilet in but this will now have to wait – budget just won’t stretch to it and there’s one on site anyway. I’m leaving the space for it and the fully plumbed in kitchen with the plan of doing these as soon as I can reasonably afford it. The walls will all be wooden stud, 2.5 inches within the current walls with two layers of 12.5mm plasterboard and insulation inside. I’m building the control room and the live room walls as independent structures, nither connecting to the outer walls or to each others walls. The ceilings will again be totally independent, both resting on my new stud walls with a central steel joist carrying the main bulk of the weight for each space. There will be 12 inches space between my new inner ceilings and the current one (which you can see now in the pictures). The walls are being built directly onto the concrete floor, with this being the only solid connection between the inner structures. The floors will be laminate on top of celotex on the concrete. Work began two weeks ago and below are the first pictures. The first stage was to sort out the existing ceiling. This consists of one layer of plywood and a layer of plasterboard underneath, attached to the ceiling joists. After inspection this appeared in pretty good shape, with the plasterboard joins being in different places to the plywood, so we ‘mud and taped’ the joins in the plasterboard only, choosing not to take down the whole layer to do the same to the plywood layer. Was this my first mistake? We’ll see……. We also attached strips of wood to the edges of the ceiling to remedy a few small gaps and also to add extra support. We’ve yet to seal up all the holes from the old light fittings and to go around the edges of the ceiling with acoustic sealant (as this still hasn’t been delivered). There are a couple of pics of the ceiling as it is now. Then we started the stud framing. We’ve almost got to the stage where all the stud walls that go in front of existing wall are up, with gaps in for the doors / windows which we’re waiting on also. We’re putting together the stud walls that go ‘across’ the old space (which divide the control and live rooms) but we’re not fixing them in place just yet, otherwise we won’t be able to get the scaffolding around the room to finish off the old ceiling. What you can see in two of the pictures is the base plates for these walls, so you can see where they’ll be going. It looks pretty simple at the moment, but will look much better when the angled walls go in. We’ve got a plumber and electrician coming at the end of this week and the beginning of next, so next we’ve got to get all the noggins in place to fix the radiators and electrical sockets to, not to mention getting the windows and door spaces fitted. I do have a couple of questions and would welcome any comments from people regarding things so far. Firstly, I’m now well aware of the standard way of sealing the edges of the plasterboard walls by using back rod and acoustic sealant and I intend to do this. But can I just ask why it’s not acceptable to put the edges of the plasterboards firmly together and just seal it using the acoustic sealant? It seem like you’re just making a bigger gap to close if you do it the other way….. Secondly, Just want to ask your advice regarding sliding glass / patio doors.... My studio plan incorporates two of these in the independent walls between the control room and live room. Although I know these are not the best in terms of sound isolation, I thought that they would probably be best for my design, providing me with great communication between spaces and the necessary doorways. I have, however, been offered four double glazed units for free by a conservatory company who work on the site (and who are good friends of my Dad's). These units are 1980mm x 1350mm, all double glazed with 4mm toughened glass 12m gap and then another 4mm of glass, and are left over from a job that never happened. I know that if I used two of these in my walls then I would be putting together a four leaf section, which isn't good, and that 4mm glass is far from ideal for sound isolation. However, since I was going to build a very similar four leaf system by using double glazed doors instead (at quite a price) is this something that I could consider doing? Basically my questions are... Would the size of the glass units make them a major weak point in my walls (which are currently 2 x 12.5mm plasterboard on individual wooden stud walls 25cm apart)? As I can have four of the units would using all four in some ingenious manor help to improve their performance, or am I just ignoring the facts? I'm sure anyone who has built anything on a budget will understand how I'm feeling....it seems like a great opportunity, but is it just the easy way? Thirdly, the type of insulation between studs. I’m going to use 100mm batts between studs, but due to the length of my walls I’m going to need loads of it. If I can find something close in spec to Rockwool RWA45 (which is the British equivalent to 703 I believe) will this be suitable, or again, am I just looking for an easier (or cheaper!) option? Thanks for the advice so far everyone… I look forward to any comments you have. Jamie.

Hi Kevin, Thanks for the reply. I had already checked out your glass questions while trying to sort out my own! I'd be very interested to hear how you get on and what you eventually decide upon. I have to say that I'm leaning towards using the double glazed units that have been given to me. I figure that the sliding glass doors I was going to get were probably the same thickness or possibly only slightly thicker anyway. I've also been thinking that adding more leaves of glass to a window is obviously not ideal, and a two leaf system is clearly the most cost effective way to build a isolated structure, but there must be a point when just adding more layers does begin to help. This may well be another example of me coming up with theories to suit my decisions, but once you get past the 'using the same amount of material in a two leaf as you would in a four gives you much better isolation for the same money' argument, there must be a point when just adding more leaves does start to improve isolation. In other words, if you've got extra leaves for free then the cost element is made redundant, meaning all you have is....how many leaves might make this a bit better? I'm not entirely sure what I've just written makes any sense, but it's late at night and my head hurts. If anyone has any other random theories about this then I'd certainly like to hear them, as well as the odd proven one of course. :) Jamie.

Hi Joey, Below is a picture of the unit I put in when we were first testing it.... How has it worked out? Well, I'm now a firm believer in 'you get what you pay for'. I was quoted a silent air con and ventilation system for the space at around £6000 which was obviously way over my budget. I spent about £1000 on this unit plus maybe £200 on piping and connectors etc. It works fine, but slowly, exchanging the air. You'd need to have it running constantly to really feel the effect, but alas it is noisy. Not a problem in the live room if you're recording any thing loud (guitars, drums) but a definite no no for vocals, acoustic etc. One very bad design fault is that you can't have the unit just pumping air from one room, so even if you don't mind the small amount of noise in the control room you can't have it on if you're recording anything quiet next door. Very silly on my behalf, but you learn from your mistakes... On the plus side, I needed a ventilation system to pass building regs and this did the job, and I’m planning to install some air con next year for the control room. The live room is less urgent as it maintains a pretty even temperature in there. I think the lights in the control room are the worse culprits for the heat, but it’s perfectly fine on an average day. One of the first sessions I did in there was with six people in the control room (most playing guide parts) with both doors shut in the peak of (british!) summer and that got pretty warm. By complete accident though, I have double doors at either end of the building so all you need to do is open them up for five mins and the air just shoots through and freshens it up. Hope this helps a bit. I’m also going to post a few pictures of the (mostly) finished studio, but looking back I realise I’ve missed out a whole load of work from the pics I’ve posted, so if anyone really wants to see more details of stuff then just drop me a note. All the best, Jamie.
