1. With a recording room as opposed to a control room, is symmetry important?
Control room: yes Live room: probably not, but it depends...
2. Is altering the angle of the walls good for acoustics?
That depends what you mean by "good for acoustics". If you are talking about flutter echo, then yes, splaying walls can help with that. If you are talking about resonance and modes, then no, splaying walls wont help with that, and will just make things more complicated to calculate.
3. Carpet on floor vs no carpet on floor? I'd rather have carpet, for the purposes of comfort (don't care about spills).
Bad idea, for several reasons, and only one of them is "spills". You already have bare concrete, which is an excellent floor, acoustically, provided that you treat the rest of the room correctly.
4. What materials can I put up behind the wooden walls (left, front) to give symmetry with the opposite (right and rear, concrete + secondary wooden wall) -
I don't understand the question. You don't put things "behind" walls to change room symmetry. It is the walls themselves that change the symmetry. So if you want symmetry, you will have to build inner-leaf walls t give you that symmetry. What you put "behind" the walls has no effect on symmetry (although it will certainly affect the isolation).
5. Is a concrete floor problematic in any other sense (other than sound isolation which doesn't matter in this context)?
Nope! A concrete floor is great, provided that you don't cover it with carpet. If you don't like the look of concrete, then put ceramic tile on it, or laminate flooring. Both are good acoustically.
6. Have heard talk that an "infinite ceiling" (like what I've got essentially) can be beneficial for recording, then other talk that keeping everything in line with the correct ratios is best for acoustics. Which is wrong?
Neither. Both. It depends on what you are trying to accomplish. Since you seem to be talking about a live room, not a control room, I'd go for as much height as you can get, and make the room as large as you can in all directions. And if the ceiling is still too low for good recordings, then consider how you are going to treat it to improve that. But first get your ceiling as high as you possibly can. - Stuart -

The information I've supplied (now - have edited) is as much as I can actually provide
Except for the most important item of all: the one that says "Nothing says "I didn't bother to read or follow these instructions" like leaving your profile location blank!"... :)
Perhaps it should have read "greater absorption". Plan is to have the secondary wall, with limited reflectivity, some absorption, as opposed to concrete which is more reflective.
Well, that is two different things you are talking about there. Adding the wall won't change the acoustic absorption very much. There is hardly any difference at all between the absorption coefficients of concrete and drywall, for example. The only way to get great absorption for either type of surface, is to add acoustically absorptive material over it, such as fiberglass or mineral wool. You can do that to the existing concrete just as well as you can do it to an added sheetrock wall. They only two real reasons why you might justifiably want to add a wall are to improve isolation by creating a two-leaf MSM wall, or to splay the walls for RFZ or flutter echo control.
Overhead yes - rain and the occasional plane. The concrete sides of the building should take car of the rest.
Actually, that isn't correct. All sides matter equally. Acoustically, the room does not act like four separate walls and a separate ceiling: Rather, it acts as one single unit. If you change any one part of it, then you change the whole. Sound waves bend around things that are smaller than they are, and low frequency waves are very large: much larger than your room. So you cannot hope to stop them by only treating one of the sides of the room. The entire room has to be designed to work together, or it wont work at all. I often use the analogy of an aquarium: you cannot build an aquarium with glass only three sides, cloth on the fourth side and cardboard on the bottom. It will not hold water. You have to put glass on all five sides. The same with your room. You have to treat all sides the same, or it won't "hold sound", the same way that a faulty aquarium won't "hold water". Acoustics is not intuitive.
Hence the need to build a ceiling with plasterboard and rockwool
That is only two parts of the three that you need. An MSM wall (the most cost-effective sound barrier possible for a studio) consists of two massive "leaves" separated by an air gap which is filled with wool. You are talking about only two of those. You also need to add mass to the other leaf: the existing roof.
Depends On?
Depends On what you want to do with it! Symmetry might be useful for some things, but usually you don't need it in a live room. If you are only planning to record single instruments in there at a time, then probably not needed.
Thank you - I've read about carpet being frequency 'hole-y', are there other reasons again?
Many others. For example, our brains are very used to "hearing" reflections off the floor at certain timings, because wherever you go in the entire world, the floor is always the same distance below your ears. Distance to walls and ceiling changes wherever you go, but the floor is a constant. Your brain has learned that your entire life, and is tuned to it. That "distance cue" is encoded on the sound that strikes your ear, and the sound that strikes your microphones. When your brain doesn't get that cue, it sounds strange. This is one of the reasons why it is so unpleasant to be in an anechoic chamber for very long: your brain gets no acoustic clues at all as to where the floor is, and it doesn't like that much. The data from your eyes conflicts with the data from your ears. Disconcerting. Almost as bad is if you have a soft floor and a hard ceiling above at an unusual distance: your brain isn't very good at telling "up" from "down" (even though it is really good at telling "left" from "right") so if your ceiling is at, for example, 60cm from the mics and from your ears (eg, overheads on a drum kit), but there is no acoustic "floor", then it just doesn't sound right. Your brain can tell something is wrong, and thinks that the ceiling is actually a "floor" in the wrong place. You might not be able to put your finger on what the problem is, but you'll notice that it just isn't "right". And there is no way to fix that in the mix. OK, so the effect isn't huge, and won't send you (or the people who listen to your mixes) wobbling around, looking drunk and falling over, but it is still there, subtly, and your ears can still notice that the instruments/mix/vocals just don't sound right, or don't sit exactly right together. In my philosophy, I want the best dam room I can afford, with everything as perfect as possible, to minimize all artifacts to the smallest possible expression. Ten minor sloppy things in my room design might not be much each by themselves, but add them all together and you could just end up with a problem. So I'm a perfectionist, down to every last details, for as much as my budget and room space will allow.
Okay - I guess I meant in the sense that (even if I put up solid wood) there's going to be a certain amount of transmission through to the concrete behind (through the wood itself) - which will give a different sound again to a solid wood wall with nothing behind it. SO I'm looking to equalize the absorption/reflectivity symmetry.
Once again, that isn't really true. If you have two walls set up like that, then acoustically it is a two leaf system that acts as a single resonant unit, not as two separate walls. The entire system resonates at one frequency that is set (mostly) by the masses of the two leaves and the distance between them. Acoustically, everything inside the room only "sees" the surface of the inner leaf, and behaves more or less like there was only wall at that location with the combined properties of everything, and not like there is something behind that wall. In fact, all of your room ratios and dimensions should be taken ONLY from the inner leaf, not from the outer leaf. The inner leaf surface defines the room, not what might be behind it. The properties of "what is behind it" do come into play, yes, but only as far as making up part of the system which is the complete wall. (OK, so before some perfectionist climbs in and talks about perforated panels and such, I should clarify that I'm assuming a sealed, solid, rigid, massive inner leaf, not one with holes in it, nor one with very little mass, nor a flexible one, nor a limp mass, etc. :) just plain old drywall or maybe MDF, on studs, sealed.).
So the best ratio's (houden's etc) don't apply for recording rooms in terms of modes?
I think you mean Louden, not Houden. :) Anyway, ratios still apply to live rooms, of course, but only if the room is a rectangle. If you splay some of the walls (as you often do for live rooms) then simple ratios no longer apply. You cannot figure out the room modes for a diamond-shaped room using ratios. Ratios only apply to six-sided rectangular rooms with roughly parallel walls. Even then, all that a ratio tells you is where the modes are in the room, and how they are spread out. That helps greatly in a control room, since your mix position is located at a know point within that "sea" of modes, and you want that point to be as neutral as possible. A live room is different: you might set up instruments, and mics anywhere at all in that room and you might even have several at once, each at a different location, in a different set of modes. And you will be tweaking all of that (mic positions and instrument locations) to get the best possible sound for each, which might well involve moving it into or out of certain room modes. You really don't care about modes when you do that: you only care about finding the position for the instrument and the mic that sound best, for that specific instrument with that specific musician and that specific mic. So modes aren't much of an issue in live rooms, IMHO (others might disagree!).
I mean, if I'm to stick with best ratios, the ceiling would be (as stated) about 3.5-3.9m.
Which "best ratios" are you referring to? Contrary to popular belief, there is no "perfect" ratio, nor even a handful of "excellent" ratios. Rather, there are actually a large number of really good ratios, that fall into several groups along a roughly diagonal line in X:Y space, with a couple of outlying islands around them. Each of those "really good" groups is surrounded by a larger area of "quite good" and "not so bad" ratios, and only when you get a long way outside of those neighborhoods do you get to the "Beware! dragons be here" ratios, that you really do want to stay away from. So, you can probably find a workable ratio that is reasonably close to your existing dimensions, and them maybe change one or two of your dimensions to get even closer to a good one. Provided that you are talking about a rectangular room, of course. If the room isn't rectangular, the ratios are out the window. For example, what ratio would you use to calculate the modes for a pentagonal room, or an octagon? Or even worse, a circular room? Anything outside of a rectangle simply cannot be figured out using ratios. In any event, 3.9 meters of height is pretty good. You should be able to do well with that.
And what are the advantages of an 'infinite' ceiling?
An "infinite ceiling" is really just an ordinary ceiling treated with a very large thick layer of absorption, to try to disguise it acoustically, so that it seems to not be there, in the sense that reflections from it are minimized. But the concept is more applicable to very low ceilings, and at 3.9 m you should be fine. Probably normal treatment is going to be fine for that.
I can't make the width/length any larger, so would there any point to make the ceiling higher?
If you already have 3.9m, then probably not. Many folks here would happily commit murder to get such a height in their rooms! You'll find some people looking decidedly green as they read about your 3.9m room height.... :) I'm one of them! - Stuart -
