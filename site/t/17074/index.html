Soundman202022 September 2011#2
Hi "sptz". Please check the forum rules for posting: you seem to have missed something! :) Regarding your door: How is the REST of the room built? Is it built as a proper fully decoupled "room-in-a-room" MSM structure? If not, the door might not be the problem. But if it is built correctly, then the frame is probably creating a flanking path, or the seals are not sufficient. Please post some pictures of the seals around that door, with the door open. Also, is that a solid-core door, or hollow core? If it is not solid core, and very massive, that might also be an issue. One last thing: It looks like you have THREE panes of glass in that door! Is that correct? If so, that is DEFINITELY a large part of your problem. You should only ever have TWO leaves in any acoustic isolation structure. 3-leaves will always give you WORSE isolation than two leaves, especially for low frequencies, such as kick, snare, toms, etc.... It might not seem intuitive, but is absolutely correct that if you were to remove the middle layer of glass from that door, the overall isolation would increase by several dB, and most of that would be in the low end.
Basically, I'm thinking of removing this door altogether and build 2 thinner ones but creating an airlock between them, so obviously this frame has to go, what I wanted to ask is, what's the best way of achieving this in this situation? What must be done to the frame, doors, stops, etc?
That depends on several things, but the big question is: how much isolation do you need? You should put a real-world number to that, in terms of dB SPL Transmission Loss. With that number you can look at various types of door structures to find one that achieves the level you need. Anyway, please check the forum rules again for that missing item, then post more information about how the room is built, and photos of the seals around the door frame. - Stuart -

I'm 100% sure the bleed comes from the doors/frame, ... Also, the main thing that is leaking is not even low-end, but mids and high end, lots of it.
OK, in that case my prime suspect would be the seals. That is by far the most likely source of your problem. It's not 100% clear from those photos, but it looks like some of these seals are simply foam? That wont work. Foam does not stop sound: at best it might damp it, but cannot stop it. From image IMG_20110922_170048.jpg (looking down at the floor), it is clear that the seals up the sides of the door do not meet the floor seals: there is a gap there. This is a problem. IMG_20110922_170040.jpg shows two seals across the top of the door, but only one down the side. This is also a problem. Finally, there is only one floor seal, and it looks like a simple threshold weatherstrip. That is another problem. The key to door seals is simple: totally airtight. Hermetic. Foam is not air-tight: you need rubber, neoprene, or something like that. Putting that foam in might have helped a little, but it will not solve the problem, because air can move through it easily, and therefor so can sound. Here's an interesting fact: a tiny air gap around your door, just 1/32" wide and one foot long, lets through roughly the same amount of sound as the entire rest of the door (a bit more, in fact). So such a gap would decrease your isolation on that door by at least 10 dB. That's huge. In order for the door to work at all, there must be at least two complete perimeter seals, and they must both be totally airtight. I would suggest that, before going to all the hassle of replacing that door with anything else, it would be easier to first try replacing all those seals with two proper perimeter seals, made of neoprene, rubber, or something similar. The kind of seal used around the edge of car doors (on good cars!) is what you need. There must be two complete seals, each one must run around the entire perimeter of the door (top, bottom and both sides) with no breaks or gaps, and the door latch must hold the door tightly up against the seals. Try that, and I'm betting it will improve the situation greatly. If you still don't get the isolation you want after doing that, then it would be the time to think about the much larger (and far more expensive!) job of replacing that single door with a proper sound lock, with two massive back-to-back doors. (You could probably even re-use these new seals tha I am suggesting for now, if you had to go with the "double-door" path, since each of those doors would need its own set of double seals. So you waste nothing by trying this solution first.) But the key thing to remember here is: airtight. There must be zero possibility for any air movement around the edge of that door. Even the slightest gap wrecks your isolation. - Stuart -

Soundman202026 September 2011#12
I think I already explained that: You need two complete independent seals around the entire door perimeter, and a door latch that puts enough pressure on them to seal tightly. Neoprene is fine, but most good quality, soft, flexible rubbery compounds will do. Do not get a hard, tough, inflexible rubber. One really good option is the type of rubber that you find around the edge of car doors. Look around your city for companies that supply rubber: where I live (down at the bottom end of the planet, in Santiago, Chile), there's a place called "The House Of Rubber" (in Spanish), that has a huge selection of many different types, sizes, thicknesses, shapes and prices. If there's a store like that here, I'm sure there must be something even better where you live! So find that store, and post a link to their website here, then we can help you choose the type of rubber that will do the job. - Stuart -
