Now look at the name of the guy who posted that: Rod Gervais. He wrote the book on building studios. Literally: http://www.amazon.com/o/ASIN/1598630342/ So what other credentials does this guy have? Well, he also designs and builds movie sound stages, and Steven Spielberg once complimented him on how much he liked the acoustics of the sound stage that Rod built... When a guy like that talks, you should be listening. If he tells you that angling your glass is NOT going to help you acoustically, and in fact will REDUCE the isolation of your window, then I think I'd sit up and take notice, and investigate the matter a little further. The scientific reason for NOT angling your glass is that, in order to get the best possible isolation (which is what you said you must have) then you have to maximize the air gap between your two panes of glass. You want the two panes as far apart as possible. If you angle them, then you are not getting maximum distance between them. At the point where they are closest, you have minimum isolation. Bad idea, if you want maximum isolation. So what you SHOULD do, according to the experts here, is use two panes of different THICKNESS glass. The panes must be the right thickness that will give the same mass per square meter of your wall construction, roughly, and they must be LAMINATED glass, not plate glass or anything else. (If you want the "reason why" for only using laminated glass, and matching wall mass, then use the search feature. Yes, they are both very important recommendations, if you really do want maximized isolation). See? You learned something important very quickly here. If you had gone ahead and built your window with thin angled glass, you would have compromised your isolation severely. Aren't you glad you came here? Like xSpace said: Beware of all those places on the internet that give you advise without any science behind it. Especially if they have a product to sell you! 2-leaf. is that better or worse?
2 leaf is the ONLY way you want to build! Two and only two. Never one, never three (unless you want to spend a lot more money for worse isolation! :) ) But do be sure that it really is two-leaf: That means that you have one bunch of lots of mass attached to a set of studs, then a large air-gap that is filled with rock wool or fiberglass insulation, then another bunch of mass that is attached to another set of studs. Mass-Air-Mass. MAM. There must be no physical (mechanical) connection between your two "leaves". So, for example, if you have an existing wall with sheetrock on both sides of a single set of studs, then you need to rip off the sheetrock from one side of those studs, build another stud frame a couple of inches away, and screw sheetrock to the outside of that set of studs. With lots of rock wool packed in between. That is a two-leaf wall. That is how you maximize isolation. As xSpace mentioned, if you have ANOTHER leaf (such as by NOT removing the sheetrock from the inside of the original wall), then you have a 3-leaf wall, which contrary to popular belief will give you WORSE isolation at low frequencies. This is not intuitive, but is totally backed by masses of laboratory tests and theoretical work. 3-leaf will ALWAYS give you worse isolation than 2-leaf, all other factors being equal. Follow the links that xSpace gave you, or search the forum for "MAM" or "MSM" or "two leaf" or "3 leaf" or some such, and I guarantee you that you'll find more than enough research to keep you reading for your next quota of 50 hours... And yes, you absolutely DO want to spend the time to fully understand the two-leaf wall, BEFORE you start framing. Or else you will most likely NOT build it correctly, and then you WILL have isolation problems. Especially if you rely on your "construction-savvy" friends who probably have never heard of MAM, and don't have a clue as to why it is so very, very important. I strongly suggest that you do NOT start building until you get a really good grasp on two-leaf, three-leaf, MAM, flanking, TL, STC, and a whole bunch of other stuff that will kill your isolation if you don't get it right. Do you really want to spend your US$ 5k and not get every last penny's worth out of it? Can you afford to build it all without researching it, then find out that you have lousy isolation because you didn't understand the concepts of air springs, or flanking paths? Your call... - Stuart -

xSpace26 December 2008#18
Be careful in the demo process. When you are removing the existing sheetrock, remember that it can be used again. This is a significant savings to you, although it is a pain to do. Good skills, steady hands, a sawzall with a short blade work here. You want to cut the rock in between the studs so as to just cut the entire piece out. You can then install this piece of sheet rock into that framing bay, beefing up your exterior leaf! As a point of interest, anytime I have done this, the joint area (48 inches up from the bottom of the sheet) will typically want to bend/break. You may not experience this issue. But, you could take a single 1X4 and run a few screws into it, maybe four screws from top to bottom spaced equally, to strongback the piece. If it becomes an issue, you could do that:) Now that is good! WARNING! There are electrical wires in this wall and you must be careful. Some walls may have plumbing, same thing applies. But the gained isolation is worth the effort, and the savings, well that will, hopefully, remain quiet:)
