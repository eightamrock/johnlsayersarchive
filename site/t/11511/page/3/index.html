Soundman20201 January 2009#56
Good point! I'll research it. Any hints in the meantime?
The ducting that connects your studio to the existing HVAC must be larger (in cross section) than you think it should be, it must be lined on the INSIDE (not the outside) with absorption acoustic duct liner (maybe even mineral wool or fiberglass would work? have to ask the HVAC experts here on that, etc.), it must be long, and it must have several 90 degree bends in it. You want low velocity, high volume airflow, not the other way around. And you will probably still want to put silencers in line with the ducting. Silencers are basically just very large, long boxes with several baffles in them, 100% lined inside with absorption. The purpose is not just to kill fan noise and airflow noise, but also to stop sound getting in and out of your room along the ducts. Search the forum for "silencer" and "HVAC" and I'm sure you'll find plenty to keep you busy. A couple that I found for you: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=11410&p=82180&hilit=HVAC+silencer#p82180 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=7433&hilit=HVAC+silencer&start=45 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=1929&p=60300&hilit=HVAC+silencer#p60300 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=1929&p=60221&hilit=HVAC+silencer#p60221 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=4262&p=30547&hilit=hvac+silencer#p30547

CMHC Technical Series 02-108 wrote:
Generally, at low frequencies, the increase in the transmission loss of a partition obtained by adding a sound absorptive material inside its cavity is equivalent regardless of the material used. Above 250 Hz, mineral fiber and blown cellulose give the best results; mineral fiber insulation provides slightly better transmission losses than glass fiber, especially around the critical frequency. Also, in the case of glass fiber, a greater transmission loss can be achieved by using denser batts.
The above document is based on analysis or NRC IR 761. For more analysis have a look at USG Density report attached, paying particular attention to the low frequencies in Table 1. Note to others: An acoustics professional will save you money by knowing what to buy and not follow net myths. Andre

if so, it seems there is nothing to keep the walls from tipping from side to side. how do you finally stabilize a fully decoupled double wall?
You build the wall next to it at right-angles, and the ceiling on top of it! Exactly like for a normal house: the walls support each other at the corners. The ceiling helps, too. Think about it. The wall that runs north-south is supported by the wall the runs east-west, at the corner, and vice-versa. Two walls that intersect cannot fall over. Each supports the other. Each wall keeps the other from falling over. When you have all four walls up, none of them can "fall over" because each one is supported at both ends, and then the ceiling on top ties them all to together firmly, so they can't even wobble. It is exactly the same construction that you use for building a house normally! No different at all. If you know how to build the frame around a house and put the ceiling on, then you know hoe to build another identical one, scaled down to be a bit smaller, inside of the first one. "Room within a room". That is what you are doing.
another version of the above question is what is recommended if you run into a situation where separated/decoupled elements need to join?
That cannot happen. They should never need to join. If you designed it right, of course. If it is designed correctly, then it is impossible that you need to join "decoupled" elements. If you did, then they will not be decoupled! If you are running into a situation where you think you need to join them, then you did not design it right! It's that simple.
we've been putting in there the spongy thin styrofoam
What???? Styrofoam? that won't help at all! Its a good thermal insulator, but a lousy acoustic insulator. Styrofoam is closed-cell. Acoustically useless. And once again, if you are joining things that are supposed to be decoupled, the you are wasting your time and money. Why bother?
But maybe silicone or green glue or felt would help?
Nope. Nothing will help, because you should not be in that situation! You might need to SEAL a gap between two decoupled elements, and there are ways of doing that, but you cannot possible ever need to JOIN to decoupled elements, if you designed it right. You might also need to brace things temporarily, while you get other things in place to hold them up, but them you REMOVE the bracing. Like for example after you lift the first inner-wall into place, you may well need to brace it against the outer wall, with bits ot 2x4 nailed in temporarily. But once you get the SECOND wall in place and correctly tied to first one, then you remove the bracing.
is it really possible to keep things totally decoupled forever?
Yep: the way to do that is to take the time to design it right in the first place! That way, you won't have to wast time and money ripping things apart after you notice that you did it wrong. Sorry to be so harsh, but we did warn you up front to take the time to think it through carefully... I'm not gloating: just reminding you to be careful, think things through. Stop. Look. Backtrack. Rethink. Undo whatever it is that caused you to join decoupled elements. hopefully, it is not too late. Jiminy cricket here!!!! :)
what happens at the top and bottom? how do the walls (or whatever items) keep from flopping over?
Build another wall at right angles to it! It's that simple. Same as building the outside walls of a house: the corners keep them from flopping over, and the ceiling adds stiffness and rigidity to the structure. The only difference is that you don't put a roof on top of your inner leaf, but apart from that it is built in the identical manner of building the outer leaf.

"It is exactly the same construction that you use for building a house normally! No different at all." The corners do keep the framed wall standing upright, but it isn't what stops the walls from racking or collapsing. This is usually done by installing 4X8 sheets of plywood stood vertically on each wall that makes up a corner. You may also use a 1X4 diagonal brace cut into the framed wall. In residential construction, joists make the framed walls move as one but do nothing beyond that to add any rigidity to the structure that is missing from the walls to start with. They are used to keep walls from bowing outward from the weight of a roof and to install interior room finishes to. There are some differences as you can see, and that may be of some use hopefully.
