i'm looking for something to decouple areas where the two walls end up touching a common wall.
Once again, if you design it right, then decoupled walls CANNOT touch a common wall. If that happens, then you have a design flaw. "Decoupled" means just that. Not coupled. No connection. If two things that are "not coupled" are both touching something else, then they ARE coupled. The only way you can fully decouple two things is to remove all mechanical connections between them.
which are ... ?
Closing up an air gap between decoupled elements, so it looks neater? One way is to stretch a bit of acoustic cloth across the gap, and staple it on both sides. Another is to attach a wooden strip to one of the elements with a thin spacer behind it, such that it extends across the gap and in front of the element on the other side, but does not touch it. A third way is to wrap mineral wool or fiberglass in acoustic cloth, and line the gap with that. You can probably think of more. The point is that there are no mechanical connections. No flanking paths.

Well, you CAN do it with one door: Rod's "Superdoor", I think he calls it. But that is one massive monster of a door, with metal plates inside and three levels of seals, IIRC. Not easy to build. But it works. Otherwise, if you want good isolation, then the recommendation is usually two hefty back-to-back solid-core doors with two seals each, or two sets of sliding glass doors built the way John does them. But once again, it all depends on how much isolation you need. For a vocal booth that is only used when all else is relatively calm around about, then a well built single door MIGHT be enough. On the other hand, if you have Mr. Gorilla bashing out 115 dB on the drums, then even Rod's Superdoor would not have an easy time containing all that if you have a cranky baby trying to fall asleep on the other side.... Once again, it is a matter of the relative levels that you have / need. In any case, it makes no sense to build a door that is better than your walls: It will most likely be your weakest link, but it might not be, depending on how you are building those walls. I hope that glass is really thick laminate! And carefully sealed. Otherwise that will be your weak point. When you say "solid core fiberglass", do you mean that the door has a solid core, such as wood or metal, and has a fiberglass outer skin? Or do you mean that the door is the usual kind of door with thin plywood panels on a frame, and a rigid fiberglass core? I suspect you are talking about the later. If so, I have my doubts about those! Foam-core-door-with-glass is not going to be a good isolator. The glass might also be an issue in your booths, with regard to reflections. But unless it is thick laminate glass, its not going to help much for isolation, and might even end up giving you resonance problems. The resonant frequency of thin plate glass (the kind you normally get in doors) is going to be in the range where you don't want it for your kind of instruments, I think. If you can give us more details on the glass (thickness, type, etc.) then someone can probably calculate what those frequencies will be. I do recall reading some posts a while back from someone who had "ringing" resonance problems with the glass in his doors, and was asking for advice on how to damp that ringing. I don't recall if there was a solution or not. You might want to spend a few minutes searching for that thread here. How about a photo of your doors? If they are they way I think they are, then I'd really suggest that you re-think using only one. Also regarding doors: Don't forget that you do NOT want to drill large holes all the way through the door to mount through-the-door handles/locks. That compromises the acoustic isolation. Instead, use surface mounted hardware for handles and locks. Looking forward to new pics and SketchUp! "No pics means it didn't happen!" - Stuart -
