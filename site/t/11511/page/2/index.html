Take heart. As Phillip Newell wrote in Recording Studio Design
There are essentially three aspects to sound isolation; mass, rigidity and distance. Taking the last one first, if we can get far enough away from noise sources and our neighbours, then we will have solved our isolation problems. That is probably the one and only thing that is totally obvious in the field of acoustics.
The bolding is mine. There things that are constants due to acoustics, that (again) are not intuitive. Quoting Newell again:
It is often difficult to get the fact across to people that 70 dB or 80 dB of isolation demands the same degree of treatment, irrespective of the purpose for which it is needed. ‘But I only want it for practising my drums’ is a cry so frequently heard. It is as though people expect that the cost of isolation somehow reflects the cost of what they are doing. Because they are only spending the cost of a drum kit, they expect that the isolation to the bedroom of a neighbour will somehow be cheaper than if they were spending a hundred times as much on a complete set of studio equipment. Obviously, (or at least it should be obvious) the two situations will have identical isolation requirement, whether the drums are used for practice only, or for a very serious recording process. Basically there are only two factors involved in extreme cases of sound isolation; great weight or great distance. This brings us nicely back to the consideration of the layout of rooms in multi-room studios, as the isolation between sections can be destroyed by inappropriate door and window systems.
We want your studio to be the best possible. Very little is obvious. Andre

• West (exterior) wall . Remove inside rock, leaving exposed studs and (fluffy) fiberglass. One sheet of rock remains on the outside.
Depending on how much isolation you need and how loud you are, one single sheet of drywall on the outside is probably not nearly enough. But the good news is that you can beef it up from inside the wall, by cutting strips of drywall to fit in exactly between your studs, up tight against the existing layer, sealed all around the edges with caulk. Two layers of 5/8 drywall, maybe 3 or 4 layers, depending on your calculations that you do...
Build new wall 2” from existing exterior wall. No rock on side of new wall that faces old wall, ...
so far so good! But...:
then alternating studs packed with mineral wool,
Why "alternating"? You need the ENTIRE air space FILLED with mineral wool, ideally (depending on how much isolation you need). But the very worst case would still have mineral wool (or fiberglass) packed the full depth of the stud, in every single stud gap, on at least one side of the wall.
then a sheet of ½” rock caulked to the studs and caulked to a sheet of 5/8” rock on the side facing into the tracking area.
There is no need to use different thicknesses of sheetrock. It won't buy you anything worthwhile, regardless of what you might have read on other web sites. AFAIK, this is one of those "... but everybody says to do it that way to avoid resonance ..." legends that seems to stick around, but try to find some actual hard data to show that it makes a worthwhile difference... Just go with two layers of 5/8.
and caulked to a sheet of 5/8” rock
Forget the caulk in between the layers! Bad idea. Another legend. If you caulk the layers together, then it becomes one solid layer, which you don't want. What you REALLY should do (but probably will not be able to afford on your budget) is to spread Green Glue between the layers. Green Glue is not glue at all: it is a special acoustic damping "gunk" that works really, really well at low frequencies, such as those where your drum kit and bass guitar live. If you can afford it, then get it. If you can't afford it, then a third layer of 5/8 sheetrock will help nearly as well. - Stuart -

What André means is that there is a "bug/feature" in the SketchUp program. If you create any "components" (a component is basically a group of surfaces/lines/objects/other components/whatever) then it is stuck inside your SketchUp file forever, even if you then delete that component. So, if you create a 2x4 as a component, then you immediately delete it, it isn't really gone. It is still part of your file. Even though you deleted it, it is still there. So over time, your file just grows and grows, as it accumulates dead components. The trouble is, that you absolutely do need to make components in SketchUp! Things need to be components to be useful. You do need to make your SketchUp model as accurate as possible, including everything, all components. But just be aware that the "bug/feature" will make your file huge eventually. So you are stuck with a growing file. What Andre was telling you was to not make anything that you don't need. There are ways around that: You can copy all of the actual real "good" components to a brand new clean file every now and then, but it's a pain! There is also a tool that is supposed to purge those "unused" components, but it doesn't seem to work too well.

Once again, you don't need to do that with two different thicknesses. Contrary to internet urban legend, it won't gain you anything worthwhile. Better to go with two layers of 5/8 and green glue. If you already bought some 1/2 and some 5/8 and can't return the 1/2, then so be it: Just use it like that. It wont do any harm, but 2 layers of 5/8 would be better. And do NOT caulk or glue the layers together!! Bad idea! Just screw/nail the layers to the studs, with nothing in between. The only exception is if you use Green Glue in between the layers, which is highly recommended. Take a serious look at Green Glue, (which isn't glue). It is expensive, but WELL worth it. It works miracles on your low end. No other product even comes close, so far. - Stuart -

Soundman202031 December 2008#50
Hi Matt,
Today HVAC guy said keep it simple.
But does he understand the issues of HVAC in studios? Most likely not. For example, did he tell you how many 90° bends you need in each leg? Did he tell you about the insulation in the ducts? Did he tell you how to build the silencers? Did he even MENTION the silencers? If you do HVAC wrong, then you might as well not bother building isolation walls at all. Just think that the ductwork is a direct path for sound that bypasses your walls: It is an open hollow tube. Sound like to travel down open hollow tubes. You have to do it right in order to PREVENT the sound from traveling down your open hollow tubes...
Will get cool air into main tracking room and return out. Open iso booth doors between takes
Opening the door to the booths may or may not be a workable solution: Those are really small booths, as Andre pointed out. Not much air in there. They are also air-tight. (They have to be totally airtight in order to isolate sound). It's not a good idea for a human to be inside a small, sealed, air-tight box for very long, if he plans on staying conscious... People have this bad habit called "breathing". If they don't do it several times per minute, then they don't stay being "people" for very long!!! People also have this bad habit of giving off something called "body heat". So you will have hot bodies inside these small, air-tight, extremely well thermally insulated boxes, perhaps with lights (that give off heat) and equipment (that gives off heat). All that heat has no place to go: it is trapped inside the booth. How quickly do you think the temperature will rise to be very uncomfortable, with no ventilation and no air-conditioning? In other words, you need ventilation in your booths too, if you plan to use them for more than a couple of minutes at a time. Ventilation means insulated ductwork, silencers, and fans. All of this takes up lots of space somewhere. That's why we suggested that you should design carefully BEFORE you start building. Now you are stuck with the problem that you already started building, and you have not thought out any place to put your silencers and ductwork, which are rather large. So either you have to tear something down and re-do it, or you have to use up some of the very small amount of space that you have available in those rooms.
Bought some recessed lighting from ikea, but rethinking that: might be too hot up near the fiberglass.
How are you going to install recessed lighting in a sealed leaf? Your ceiling is your inner leaf. If you poke holes through it to install lights, then you no longer have a sealed leaf. You no longer have isolation. A leaf is only as good as its weakest point. If you cut a hole in it for the light, then that hole is the weak point. Sound will easily leak out through a hole that size. Unless you can find a light fitting that is just as massive as sheetrock and you can seal it in place such that it remains acoustically impermeable, then there is no point in spending all that time and money on doing a proper two-leaf system if you punch holes in it. - Stuart -
