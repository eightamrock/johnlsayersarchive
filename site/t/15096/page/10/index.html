Personally, I'd change the speaker angle... Keep them in the middle of the soffit, but angle the entire soffit a bit more. --------- EDITED TO ADD: Since originally writing this a few years back, I have since learned that the speakers should NOT be in the middle of the soffits, but rather offset to one side or the other, in order to avoid creating peaks and nulls in the frequency response. It seems that a position about 2/5 of the width is good. They should also not be centered vertically, for the same reason. That said, you do still need a lot of surface on all sides of the speaker. - Stuart - ----------

Soundman202028 August 2011#228
To get an idea of what I mean, imagine that your speaker soffits are actually doors with their hinges next to the window they aren't REALLY like that, of course! Its just to give you the idea of what I'm trying to say). OK, so now if you "open" or "close" those door-soffits, then it changes the angle of the entire soffit, so that the speaker points more "down" the room or more "across" the room. If the door-soffits were fully closed, then the speakers would be directly facing the rear wall, firing parallel to each other. If the door-soffits are fully "open", then the speakers would be pointing directly at each other. So, all you do is open/close the door-soffits until you find the perfect angle so that the speakers are aimed exactly at your ears when seated at the mix position, which means that the acoustic axes of the speakers will intersect a few inches behind your head. So your ears would be roughly at the 38% position (which is only a guideline anyway!), and the axis intersect point would be a bit further back than that. The intersect angle would be somewhere between 60 and 90 degrees. Not sure if that made it any clearer! - Stuart -

Soundman202028 March 2012#240
-If the outer wall is the frame of the building and the inner 2x4 wall of a MAM structure connects to the same ceiling
I don't understand why you'd want to do that! The entire concept of fully-decoupled MSM "room-in-a-room" is that there is no contact at all between the inner and outer leaf. The inner "room" is built as separate, free-standing structure: four walls with a ceiling on top. The new inner-leaf ceiling ONLY touches the new inner-leaf walls. There is no need for it be attached to the outer leaf at all.
how would you go about keeping the inner wall decoupled?
Simple! Don't couple it! :) Just build the inner four walls and the inner ceiling together, as a separate, independent structure. No contact at all with the existing structure (the outer leaf).
Or is it that important and if so how important?
Extremely important! If your inner leaf is hard-connected to your outer leaf at any point, then your isolation is severely compromised.
So far this seems to be the best solution to connect the top plate to;
Well, you COULD do something like that if you don't need maximum isolation, but the question is, why WOULD you, if you have that huge space available to you, with nothing in the way? Why not just design the entire inner leaf structure such that it maximizes height and volume WITHOUT needing to touch the existing outer leaf? - Stuart -
