jhbrandt13 November 2010#4
seaweed, How much isolation do you need? This is the first question you should answer when trying to figure what type of isolating wall system you should build. I recommend that you do some noise testing in the neighborhood and determine how much STL (sound transmission loss) the walls must provide. That's for outside noise coming in. Don't forget there are thunderstorms and aircraft to deal with too. Now on to the noise that the studio will create; How loud will you be? What are the acceptable noise levels at the property line? Answers to these questions can help us guide you to the best isolation procedure. Good luck! Cheers, John

No, it should be focused on lows. If you take care or low frequency isolation, the mids and highs will be taken care of automatically. Lows are the most difficult to isolate, since they carry the most energy, and are the longest wavelengths.
As at night for example, if the lows leak out, the neighbors are inside their houses and it isn't an issue.
I beg to differ! Normal house construction does little to stop lows, and under the right conditions could even end up amplifying the lows! I can promise you that, from first had experience: I live a couple of blocks away from what was a construction site about two years ago (the building is now finished, thank God). But back then, there were some rooms in our house which were absolutely unusable at some points during the construction. They were using some kind of machinery that put out lots of low frequency energy, which set up severely annoying resonance in a couple of rooms. Stand outside, and you couldn't hear a thing, but go inside, into one of these rooms, and it was LOUD. Low frequencies are unpredictable.
In the day if lows leak out the general daytime curfuffel would mask them.
The construction site didn't make any difference to us: day or night, it was the same. Until we got the municipality to shut them down at night, for disturbing the peace. :)
So that leaves me with stopping detail of guitars etc from escaping.
Guitars are easy. Drums and bass are not.
I have the luxury of trying one layer of 5/8s drywall, then if it doesn't cut it, 2 layers. Does that approach sound reasonable?
To be brutally honest, no it doesn't! Are you REALLY prepared to rip out all your treatment and gear after the room is already finished but doesn't work, just to add a second (or third) layer, and then put it all back again, only to find that the changes to the room dimensions screwed up your modal spread, and you now have additional problems with your room? That's your call, of course, but I certainly wouldn't take the risk, if that were my build. Like Glenn said: do the measurements, then decide how much isolation you need, then design the structure to achieve that. - Stuart -
