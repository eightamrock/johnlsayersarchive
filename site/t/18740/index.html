I avoided parallel walls in the control room
Actually, you don't need to do that! It's one of those many acoustic myths that refuse to die! Yes, it is true that parallel walls can produce flutter echo and can enhance certain room mode issues, but flutter echo can be dealt with fairly easily with treatment (which you need anyway, regardless of shape), and room modes cannot be eliminated by angling the walls: all you do is make it much harder to predict the response when you do that. It is dead easy to predict the modal response of a rectangular room, and therefore dead easy to adjust the dimensions to optimize the room ratio. But as soon as you angle the walls, or add extra walls (such as with a 7-sided room like yours, instead of a simple 6-sided room) you take all of that off the table, and you now need to resort to far more complex and expensive methods to predict room modal response: you now need FEM/FEA software, and an operator who knows how to set up the boundary conditions in the software for your room, and how to interpret the results. So I'm not saying that you can't or shouldn't angle your walls, but rather that you should be aware of the issues involved in a non-rectangular room, and ensure that you deal with them in the design.
My grandfather, who has been a contractor for the last 50 years, is coming out in April to help us frame a new space inside the shed in addition to upgrading and running new electrical.
Great! You'll need someone like that if you have never done this before! But you should warn your grandfather that building a studio is nothing at all like what he is used to, and he'll have to modify many of the techniques and methods that he's been using for the last 50 years, since they don't work for studios. Same tools, same materials, but different methods, and different things to take into account. For example, air-tight seals absolutely critical to studios, but not needed at all in normal house/office/shop/school construction. Decoupling is something that he's probably never done before, or at least not to the extreme extent it is needed for studios. Electrical is completely differentm since you cannot cut any holes in the drywall for mounting switch boxes, outlet boxes, lights, etc. Everything has to be done inside the room, using surface-mount techniques and materials, and you can have only one single wall penetration to bring in your main electrical cable from the outside world. Even that penetration is done differently than he would expect. Doors and windows are also done differently, since you need perfect seals on all sides of everything, and not just one seal: you need several seals on each door (at least two full perimeter seals), etc. HVAC is another big issue, and I don't even see any provision for that in your plans. It is probably the single most overlooked aspect of home studio building, yet also the most critical, for several reasons. Many people think they don't even need HVAC for their studio at all, and then argue strenuously when they get the explanation as to why it is a basic necessity, as much as lights or a floor. some plan to just open the doors and hope that air will somehow magically percolate through the studio, and even more magically cool and dehumidify it :shock: :? :roll: So hopefully your grandfather has some experience with HVAC, and we can fill him in on the differences between normal HVAC installation and studio HVAC installation, which is rather different. One other point on this: you mention "frame a new space inside the shed", but you don't mention sealing and beefing-up the existing shed before you even get to that. That corrugated metal is not much use by itself for your outer leaf. That's going to need dealing with before you can even think about the inner-leaf. Anyway, to answer your original question: Yes, your basic plan is along the lines of being workable, but it will need lots of refining in order to get it to the point where it actually WILL work, particularly in the issue of the doors. - Stuart -

Soundman202023 January 2014#8
I was thinking of building it with single walls for the time-being, then I can beef them up and convert them to double-walls later when I have the time and budget
That would basically give you a three-leaf design, which is usually not a good thing, but give the cost and complication of trying to beef up the metal, it might not be a bad option. The issue is this: a three-leaf wall will always give you worse low frequency isolation than the equivalent two-leaf wall, all other factors being equal. The reason is sort of complex, and I can explain it if you are interested, but it's just one of those many things that is not at all intuitive about acoustics. You simply assume: "If one leaf is OK, and two leaves is good, then three must be better!" But in reality it doesn't work like that: 3 leaves is worse for low frequencies. OK, having said that, it can still be an option if you have no choice. That probably applies to you. So you can build your room as a two-leaf structure, then compensate for the poorer performance by using larger air gaps and more mass. Simple! :) I would also suggest that you cold try building it in such a way that you can easily modify it later. For example, design in "break points" in the framing, so you can easily cut into the wall later to add the huge doors in the positions where you will need them, without needing to tear down the entire wall and re-build it! So put the door header and side frames in now, ready for the future doors, then fill in the "doorway gap" with more framing that you can simple cut out in the future when you get to that point. The same applies to your storage/ entry area: build in break points and door framing now, then just cover them in drywall. Then when you have the money, carefully cut out that drywall to reveal the already-complete door framing, slap in the doors, build the extra farming to complete the storage room, and bingo! You are done! If you work smart, design it all in advance but only build what you can afford right now, then you can save money both now and later, and also save time, simplify, etc.

Regarding ratios, through my research it appears good ratios for a room are based on this ratio: 1 x 1.12 x 1.23 with the ceiling being the largest ratio. Am I on the right track here?
The room height is always the first "1", at the front of the equation. width comes next, then length. So a room with an 8 foot ceiling and the ratio you mention, would be 8 feet high, 8' 11-1/2" wide, and 9'-10" long. But that's not the best ratio. It's a good one, but is only one of many. The best ration is Sepmeyer's first ratio, which is 1 : 1.14 : 1.39. There's a bunch of good ones, and you can check how yours pan out, by using one of the good room.mode calculators, such as these two: http://www.bobgolds.com/Mode/RoomModes.htm http://amroc.andymel.eu/ As long as you are inside the Bolt area, you should be fine, and if Bob Golds' calculator shows a good smooth Bonello diagram and passes all three BBC tests and shows a ratio in the top ten, then you are doing great! There's a really good list of the best modes over at StudioTips forum, but they seem to be offline right now. But keep trying: http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.php?t=684 - Stuart -
