Wow... OK... Well, you're surely in for a wild ride then! :twisted: You mentioned "tight knit community... Do you have CC&Rs? Have you checked with your local building/planning department to see if you can legally sacrifice your one car garage and convert it to habitable space? One option I recently pointed out to someone... Granted, you'll burn some usable floor space... But imagine your garage door fully open (without an automatic door opener). Now picture a wall across the garage about four feet into the garage from there. That wall goes straight up towards the ceiling, about four feet tall. Then the rest of the way up, the wall is slanted, like an attic wall / vaulted ceiling, at 45 degrees, towards the back of the garage, where it connects to the ceiling a little bit past where the overhead door tracks end. This would allow for a storage area to exist between the garage door and the studio, good for bicycles, lawnmowers, garbage containers, a motorcycle, etc. And the studio wall would not interfere with the garage door track or opener and both would be accessible (albeit awkwardly) for servicing. The studio console would fit in the "alcove" area inside the room - the wall would be angled over the console. :) Yes, the garage door would technically be a third leaf... But the third leaf effect, if any, would be eliminated if the door were left open when noise levels were high. If you're willing to lose a little square footage to useful storage, preserve the integrity of the door, and live with a console in an alcove (which might offer some acoustic advantages :roll:), this would be one way to do it. Also, if you're lucky and the back of that electrical service panel is within that first few feet in that would-be storage area, you wouldn't even have to deal with that! :twisted: I just threw together this very crude illustration to show what I mean... --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward26 June 2007#6
First of all, you're not building a "workshop." Workshops are non-habitable spaces. My city's senior building inspector and plan checker told me, when I was early in my design phase: "Is it going to have air conditioning? Yes? Then it's habitable." That might have been an oversimplification, but he was trying to impress upon me that the room I wanted to build had a higher standard to meet in terms of ventilation and such. Another thing: habitable space additions/conversions increase the square footage of your home. This is, overall, a good thing. In my case I'm increasing the habitable square footage by about 12%. Granted, I'm losing about 30% of my garage, but the garage footage is valued lower. This increases the value of the home a little. I may have to pay higher property taxes though... which is fair when you think about it: Whether you bought it or built it, you're taxed on its overall value. CC&Rs stands for Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions. They are the "laws" written by the developer and signed by all homeowners in a community governed by a homeowners' association (HOA) - even subsequent owners must sign them. If you have an HOA, with or without dues, you definitely have CC&Rs. If no HOA, then you probably don't. Most CC&Rs are pretty strict on garage conversions, especially full ones like you are proposing. Willfully violating them can subject you to thousands of dollars in civil penalties and liens. When in doubt, check it out. :-) I'm glad you're conversing with your county building department. I've found mine to be especially helpful. Just be sure to be explicit with your questions and don't try to "soften" your description of what you plan to do: the distinction between habitable and non-habitable has a substantial impact on what is allowed and required, and you don't want to paint yourself into any corners. :-) On losing too much space to my storage idea: Is your garage empty right now? That stuff's gonna have to go somewhere, you know! ;-) --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward26 June 2007#8
My wife and I are on our second home. In both cases (previous house and this one), we've been governed by CC&Rs, and our homeowners' associations were established by the builder. The HOA board consisted initially of representitives of the builder. Once the homes were all built, the HOAs were transferred to the homeowners, so the board was changed to members of the community. Unfortunately (or fortunately, depending on your perspective I guess), such HOAs often die on the vine because there are no homeowners' association dues to keep them viable. The community's interest in the HOA tends to wane, if ever there was any, because there aren't any financial decisions to be made. It becomes, in effect, an enforcement activity that many interpret to be unpopular. I guess the point I'm making is, if you aren't sure whether or not you have CC&Rs, be absolutely sure that you don't. The county recorders' office might be able to tell you, as I believe CC&Rs are recorded with the deed and are attached to the property forever. I hope this doesn't turn into a pro-vs.-con argument as to the merit of HOAs and CC&Rs... I just wanted to make sure you don't run into a problem later. :) I'm looking forward to finding out what you learn from your county in terms of ventilation requirements. I'm predicting you'll need 0.35 to 0.5 ACH (air changes per hour) of fresh air ventilation or an operable window of a certain size... And since isolation is a top priority for you, an operable window is pretty much out of the question. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward26 June 2007#12
I realize you're kind of going for the "chiller room" concept, where the leftover garage space acts as the chiller room, with the window air conditioner being the source of the cooling and the fan on the back wall of the studio supplying the room with cool air. I'm not sure this is going to fly with the county building department though... :roll: Plus, that window A/C unit is going to protrude into the area between the walls, which is your pathway to the service panel. I'm 99% sure that's not going to fly. That service panel faces the garage, so I cannot imagine a plan checker would allow for the scenario of a breaker tripping, and someone having to shimmy their way in the cramped area between those walls to get to the breaker panel. Getting back to my sketch... You're burning a lot of floor space in the back... What if you made the rear wall of your studio a few inches away from the back wall of the garage? The threshold of the door leading to your studio could be at the same height as the door leading to the house, and then you'd have to have a 3 foot by 3 foot (or so) pad/platform at the studio entrance, with one or two steps down into the rest of the room. You'd basically gain the space at the rear and lose it at the front. If you do that, then I think your service panel woes may go away. You may need to bite the bullet (as I did) and do the combo mini-split A/C plus heat recovery ventilator (HRV) / energy recovery ventilator (ERV) concept. That'll add $2,000 to $3,000 to your build I'm afraid... :roll: --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward28 June 2007#18
I don't have a copy of the National Electric Code (NEC) book (I know Rod does, as does guitardad72), but I am pretty certain the requirements for electrical service panel access and clearance are more strict than "as long as you can get to it." For instance, I spotted this plan checklist document on the County of Salt Lake site that indicates "not in bathroom or firewall, 30 inch clearance side to side, 36 inches in front, and 6.5 feet in height." That may be just Salt Lake County's extra requirements above and beyond what their model codes call for, or (as I suspect) a paraphrased summary of what the model codes do call for. You're to be commended for following the rules and going through the process -- and by all means, congratulations on your securing a building permit with so little hassle! 8) However, do understand that the plan check process is intended to catch many things, but in the event that a code violation exists on the drawing that is not caught by the plan checker, the oversight does not allow the violation to occur. The same is true if something is missed by an inspector signing off on work done. It's frustrating because the system is in place, in theory, to prevent errors and violations, but many things can and do slip through the cracks... Anyway, I would highly recommend consulting with someone familiar with the model codes in place for your county and, ideally, receiving a copy of the specific language on the subject (assuming there is some, and I predict that there is).
