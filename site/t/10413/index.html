Hey Charles, Nice layout! I wish I had that much space... Regarding HVAC, I've been looking into that for my build, and some of the comments I've read here highlight the fact that, in building a studio, we surround the thing with acres of really thick, very efficient thermal insulation, plus we put thick walls around it with large insulating air gaps, then we go and seal the damn things airtight! Clearly, this creates a very, very well insulated hermetic bubble, where heat accumulates rapidly, since it has no other place to go. Soundman202015 November 2008#6
Regarding your question on the separation: Below is a graph from an old paper on the acoustics of wall design, that shows the effect of larger and smaller gaps. Careful! The numbers are NOT just for the air gap! They are for the ENTIRE wall thickness, from the outside face of the outer leaf to the inside face of the inner leaf. Also, the dB numbers are not straight Transmission Loss (TL): They refer to how much BETTER the TL is for each case, as compared to normal mass law. So, assuming that you are using 2x4 framing double stud walls, if you have about 1 inch of separation between your frames (inner leaf to outer leaf), and two layers of GB on each side, then your total wall thickness would be around 9 inches, so your TL would be around 15 dB better than mass law. If you increase the stud separation to 4 inches, your total wall thickness would be around 12 inches, and you'd have a bit better than 20 dB above mass law. In other words, reducing your stud separation from 4 inches to 1 inch will cost you about 5 dB in transmission loss, theoretically. Of course, read the fine print: Those numbers are valid for frequencies in the range 125 Hz. to 4 kHz., and are not so good at lower frequencies, and they are theoretical! Reality might be somewhat different! But at least that should give you an idea of what the "penalty" is for narrowing your gap, and figuring if you can live with it or not. Don't forget that stuffing the entire gap full of rock wool or fiberglass also helps a lot. - Stuart -

Opening doors for cooling / ventilation is an option that I've seen discussed and approved in some places, but personally I don't see it as being a good idea. Air won't decide to move in and out of a sealed booth with the door open, all of its own accord. It will take a long time for warm stale air to move out, and cool clean air to move in, unless maybe you put a fan on the floor to "help" it a bit. But that's a bit of an ugly kludge, if you ask me. It will work, sure, but for me, I'd prefer to have proper cooling / ventilation in each room / booth. But that's just me. I know others do use the "open-the-door-and-wait" technique quite happily.
I'm expecting to need corner bass traps, and some of the predictable absorptive treatments, but I'd really like to have it be as warm, undeadened-sounding of a room as possible.
Are you talking about the live room, or the control room? The LR you should treat the way you want it to sound, but the CR needs to be treated for the best possible neutrality, otherwise your mixes won't translate. You don't want your CR to add too much "character" or "color" to what you are hearing from the speakers, as that would make your mixes not translate. So your CR will need oodles of bass trapping, mostly dead ceiling, mostly live floor, good absorption on all of the first reflection points, and probably lots of other treatment which you'll only be able to decide on once you have done all of the above and and actually tested the room to see how it is responding. About your "volume expander" idea: I don't think that will work. The tubes will coupe the spaces, of course, but they will probably act as some sort of weird Helmholtz resonator, accentuating certain frequencies and killing others. The impedance mismatch in and out of those tubes would be pretty large, so I reckon you'd get all kinds of strange and wonderful honky clanky things going on! I can't see it doing anything useful, and probably it would just make for an acoustics disaster. Acoustically coupling two spaces is pretty hard to do transparently.
Idea 2 - Take away the vaulted ceiling for a flat ceiling that thereby gives more volume, but then has parallel ceiling/floor. Which one would give me better acoustics?
In my non-expert opinion, I'd say that more volume is preferable to a vaulted ceiling. More volume is always good, acoustically (except when the extra volume is down the other end of a pipe! :) ). So I think I'd maximize the volume at the expense of the vaulted ceiling.
I'd love to be able to put a couple of stereo tlm149s for room mic's on a drum mix and actully use those tracks because they sounded good.
Take a look at Lou's build! He has a space similar to yours for his drum room, and a few days ago he posted some samples of some pretty damn good drum recordings he did on that room. Take a look at how he did it, and you should get some ideas about how you can do it. Of course, he went to some amazing extremes with his build, but you should be able to get convincingly close in your drum room on a smaller budget. - Stuart -
