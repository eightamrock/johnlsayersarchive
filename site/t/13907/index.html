Soundman20206 January 2010#10
The more drastic option, if you are certain that it really is coming through the wall, is to take off the drywall, on your side of the wall, use it to beef up the drywall on "their" side of the wall from the inside (between the studs), then build a new frame an inch or so away from the existing frame, and put a couple of layers of drywall on the outside of that, filling the cavity with rockwool, of course, and carefully triple-caulking all around for an air-tight seal. That would probably solve the "wall impact transmission" problem, if that's what it really is, provided that there is no flanking going on. If there IS flanking going on, then the above would not accomplish very much (except to make the TV and voices even quieter). Even then, you'd still have some flanking through the ceiling/floor/sidewalls. Not sure if it worth the effort, unless you can be 100% certain that it is ONLY impact noise coming ONLY through the wall. - Stuart -

Soundman202029 January 2010#16
Sounds like a triple leaf to me... Or maybe a quadruple leaf, if I'm understanding your previous post, where it said "1/2" drywall - 1.5" pink insulation between 1.5" deep wood studs - concrete. I'm sure it's the same on the neighbor's side." If so, and you do already have a triple leaf, then adding a fourth leaf would probably not be a good idea, unless you can spare the space for a very large air gap (many inches), and put lots of mass on that fourth leaf (several layers of drywall). Was there a reason why you didn't just take off the framing and drywall on your side, as was suggested above, and rebuild a new leaf a few inches in? That would have been the best bet, I think, from my limited understanding of the issue... Maybe André can chime in again, with his comments on that. That thin 1.5" original cavity with a thin layer of 1/2" plus your new layer of 5/8" sounds like it would be resonating at a highish frequency (small air gap, relatively low mass, poor damping), plus it is still coupled to the concrete wall. Not good at all for transmission loss in the low end. I'm not convinced that creating yet another air gap and adding yet another relatively low mass would accomplish what you want, especially seeing that you seem to think it is impact noise you are hearing. I'm guessing it would have been better to take off all the original thin coupled framing and thin drywall, like xSpace suggested, carefully seal that concrete block wall airtight, build a new frame several inches away from that concrete block wall (decoupled), then put all your mass on that (at least a couple of layers of 5/8, with GG), and fill the cavity with fiberglass or rockwool. Assuming there is no flanking going on, that should have gotten you better than what you have now, I'd say. Was there a reason why you could not do that? I'm not really sure what your options are now, but the math isn't on your side any more. You got (subjectively) a roughly 10 dB increase in isolation from increasing the mass of your third leaf by 125% and using GG. To get another 10 dB (halving the level again, subjectively), if my math is right, theoretically you'd need to do at least the same again, another 125% of the CURRENT mass, which basically means adding TWO MORE layers of 5/8 with GG. I don't think you'd want to put that much mass on simple 2x2 studs anyway, even assuming that no flanking is going on! - Stuart -
