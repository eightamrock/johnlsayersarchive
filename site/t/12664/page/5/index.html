I've made a concrete door which consists of 3" x 2" C16 tanalised wood made into a frame 3 inches deep, I clad one side in 12.5mm plywood and filled the door with concrete so it was level with the top of the wooden frame. The concrete was left to dry and another sheet of 12.5mm ply used to to clad that side, AC-50 acoustic sealant was used to bond and seal the ply to the frame along with screws every few inches all round. I'm not sure if this would pass any type of building code or regulations but it's worked for me. The door could actually be made a bit thinner as I found the weight was much higher than that of the dense concrete blocks I used for the wall. It probably weighs around 250kg. I hung the door with 5 very high grade hinges, although I may add one or 2 more to make it extra safe

Soundman202028 January 2014#119
Please explain 2 doors back to back? I'm just going to have one of those doors for each door enterance. NOT two. one two leaf door NOT two two leaf doors
Good isolation for any studio is based on the MSM principle, also known as fully-decoupled two-leaf isolation. In other words, if you want decent isolation ("soundproofing") for a studio, then it has to be built such that the walls and roof are two-leaf systems, where the leaves are separated from each other by an air gap. So for example you might have an outer leaf that consists of OSB with siding on it, attached to one side of the first stud frame, then an inner leaf that consists of two layers of drywall attached to one side of the second stud frame, and the two frames do not touch each other. That's a two-leaf system. This is sometimes also called "room-in-a-room" construction, since basically that's what it is! You build one room inside another room. So, since the walls have to be built as two-leaf systems, that means that you MUST HAVE TWO DOORS for each entrance: one door goes in each leaf. One door is in the outer-leaf, attached only to that first stud frame, and the other door is in the inner-leaf, attached to the second stud frame. If you only have one door, then that means the other leaf has an empty hole in it, where the door should have been, and therefore there is no isolation at all... - Stuart -

Well, no, that's no correct. STC rating has nothing at all to do with how many decibels the wall will attenuate, not on A or C, or any other weighting scale. STC is a single-number system for RATING the isolation that provided by walls in the normal speech and typical house/office/school noise part of the spectrum. It tells you nothing at all about how well the wall isolates music, machinery, or anything else. I can build a a wall rated at STC-50 that does a terrible job of isolating rock music, and I can build you another wall rated at only STC-40 that does a fantastic job of isolating rock music. That's because STC does not consider the entire spectrum of frequencies: it omits two and a half entire octaves at the bottom end of the scale, and another octave and half at the top end of the scale. It was never meant to be a system for rating studio walls, or industrial walls, or any other type of wall than a typical wall in a house between two rooms, where people are taking, the radio is playing, and the dishwasher is running. STC tells you a lot about how well walls will stop that type of sound, since that is what it is designed to measure, but tells you zilch about any other type of isolation.

Unfortunately, there's no simple relationship here. That's why Transmission Loss curves are the best way of understanding how a wall performs. Compare that to the actual spectrum of the noise that you need to block, and you can predict how well each wall type would work for you.
then how do I turn stc in to usable number or is there another number I should be looking aswell in my walls?
Unfortunately, there is no "single number" that tells you everything about how a wall isolates. Isolation is different at each frequency, so the only way of understanding what isolation a wall will provide, is by looking at the Transmission Loss graphs. Take a look at these documents, to get an idea of how it works: https://app.box.com/shared/jcaoavdc8g http://archive.nrc-cnrc.gc.ca/obj/irc/d ... /ir761.pdf
so the A number is useless then?
No, it's not useless: it's just not much use for measuring loud music or studio isolation! It is useful for other purposes. A-weighting is meant to measure low-level sounds, at the 40 phon level, and is frequently used to specify environmental noise regulations, since that's where it makes sense. It closely matches the Fletcher-Munson 40-phon hearing response curve. C-weighting is more useful for loud sounds, around the 90 phon curve, which is why it is used for measuring studio levels, calibrating studios, and also measuring studio isolation. Studios are often calibrated at 85 dBC and, acoustic tests should also be conducted at 85 dBC, for the same reason. And 85 dBC is also the maximum constant level that you should mix and monitor at. - Stuart -
