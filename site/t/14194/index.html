Hello everyone, long time reader first time poster. So I’ve finally scraped together enough funds (hoping to get away with 50k including equipment) to take the dive and throw myself into a residential recording studio project which has been a long time in the making. I’ve been living on a beautiful slice of secluded French countryside for two years now and have been gradually getting basic renovations done(new plumbing, heating, roof repair etc) its time finally to move on to the studio itself and I’m hoping to get things finished by the end of the summer. Attached is a large plan of the whole property and a floor plan of the attic which will house the studio. Underneath this the downstairs area is composed of 6 small ‘bedrooms’ and 2 bathrooms to accommodate the musicians. The attic has a triangular roof which starts above the 1m high side walls rising to about 5m at the apex. There are two V-lux windows on the south facing side as well as a bay window at the east end of the southern wall. The larger room is divided in 3 lengthways by two sets of heavy wooden crossbeams which traverse the room 2.3m above the ground. The existing walls are all 500mm solid stone held together with various coats of plaster and cement. To every external wall I’ve just added 75mm of ‘acoustic plus’ insulation attached to a double layer of plasterboard with an additional 260mm of fibreglass insulation under the slate roof. Taking in to account neighboural proximity I think all outside noise can safely be considered as dealt with. Floors throughout are old wooden boards with little to no sonic isolation from the downstairs bedrooms so I will have to come up with some kind of solution. The whole floating floor issue seems mighty controversial from what I’ve read and my carpenter has suggested simply laying down a 50 mm high wooden frame covered by chipboard panels and filling the gap with either rockwool or Styrofoam balls but i'm open to suggestions. So far the attic is simply divided in two by the single interior stone wall which has a chimney running through it at about the point that I’ve indicated on the drawing. I was planning on smashing the wall around here and putting a pane of glass on either side of the flue. Do you think this could have sonic repercussions due to the fact that the gap between the panes extends above and below? I’m assuming I would at least have to block the fireplace and the chimney. Otherwise all the partitions marked on the plan represent just one possible layout. I’m curious to see if anyone else has a better suggestion. Theoretically I could devote the whole eastern space to one massive live room as there is already a bathroom just down the stairs and a large commercial kitchen and communal hall in another building close by. However I do like the idea of keeping some kind of relaxation space close to the studio for those long gruelling sessions and I figure a rec area can always double as an extra iso space if needs be. I haven't properly attacked the CR treatment program yet but my room modes look fairly clean. I’ve lowered the ceiling to 3m with plasterboard leaving a slope at either end. I'm attempting an LEDE design hence the 6m depth which will mean fabrics up front and some kind of diffusers in the back. There is a 1m gap between the rear stone wall and the plasterboard/insulation which could conceivably be converted into somekind of bass trap. There is also a single double glazed window to which I plan to add a second. The most interesting aspect of the project is the church 5m away from the studio. My plan is to run a multicore in there and use it as a second live room/mega-reverb unit. I hadn’t considered treating the church for fear of damaging the natural acoustic although I’m curious to see if people have any ideas on how best to make use of it. There are two possible noise problems the first being loud farm equipment during harvest time and the second being army jets who train occasionally in the area. Could I sonically isolate the church without hurting its sound? I would probably be used for classical music so even small amounts of background noise could be problematic. Its 20m long by 11m wide with a 9m high vaulted arch ceiling. The interior is all stone with a dozen 2m by 0.7m stain glass windows. The studio as a whole needs to cater for a wide range of styles, my personal area of expertise is dance and hip-hop but I already have interest from classical musicians, rock groups and African percussionists amongst others. I have attached a sample gear list, it’s a little more than what I can afford but should give an idea of the kind of set up I’ll be dealing with. I’ll try to post some pics soon and try to keep updated with any progress. So to recap my main questions are 1) What to do with the floor 2) How to best layout the interior 3) Chimney-window, can it work? 4) Is it feasible to sonically isolate a church? Thanking you all for this wonderful resource which has already helped me so much. -Sam

Death By Darwin1 June 2010#11
In my previous studio I built a drum riser to isolate the kick drum from the concrete floor to avoid sound transmission via mechanical vibration through the floor. What I did was build a 2x4 frame sheeted with plywood on both sides and filled it entirely with sand. I then carpeted the top to keep the drums from sliding around. It worked extremely well, the drum riser did not resonate at all, infact I had to remove the blanket from inside my kick drum so I could hear it over the rest of the band when playing. Sand works wonders for low frequencies because when it vibrates all the surface area of all the little particles rubbing together convert a lot of energy to heat. It is heavy though, I am not sure you'd want to cover the entire floor of a second floor building in 4" of sand, however you could try building risers for the drums and amps. Also if you build separate risers for each it will considerably lessen the transmission from other sources up your mic stands if you are tracking more than one instrument at a time. -jER
xSpace5 June 2010#12
Death By Darwin wrote:
...I am not sure you'd want to cover the entire floor of a second floor building in 4" of sand...
Dry sand weighs approx. 100 pounds (45.36 kilograms) per cubic foot. It may not be the best material to use in an upper level wooden structure. Assuming the floor can support the additional weight there are several ways this kind of project could go. You could place 2" thick 703 on the floor and install plywood/OSB in a staggered pattern to help keep some of the sound in your room and less from going downstairs. You might simply add the additional plywood/OSB over 15# felt to add the mass and/or remove the ceiling below to add resilient clips/channel to decouple the ceiling from the floor, assuming (once again) that the ceiling is one membrane and not several rooms to deal with. Stone construction that you are talking about, with timber for the joists, has the floor joists mounted to or rather set into the stone wall. So my question would be, if I were in your shoes, how is the flanking affecting the lower environment? If you were to do something to the floor to reduce the air-borne sound how much of an issue will flanking be in the downstairs or vice-versa? Just some of the things I would be thinking about.

Soundman202010 July 2010#14
...covered by a layer of felt and 18mm OSB, I plan to lay carpet possibly over an additional layer of felt...
Probably not a good idea! Carpet is really good at absorbing frequencies... selectively! It's great for absorbing highs, not so good at absorbing mids, and really lousy at absorbing lows. That's kind of the exact opposite of what you want in a studio. Normally, you need a large amount of bass trapping to control the lows, while you want to keep the highs in the room, to keep it lively. So I'd say forget the carpet, and go with something like laminate flooring. - Stuart -

Soundman202011 July 2010#16
I'd worry about bass trapping first, and then deal with any high-end issues that you might still have afterwards. Bass trapping is large, deep and plentiful: it should take care of a lot of your highs in any case, as well as the lows, so you might find that after you get the bass trapping right you actually don't need any more absorption for highs, and you might even need to put some highs back into the room! It's not a good idea to cover the floor in any case, for several reasons, so I'd leave that for a very last resort; if your room is still too lively after you do everything else, then you could put down a couple of thick throw rugs to finish it off, but I doubt that you'll get to that point. You'll probably be putting reflective surfaces back into the room after the bass trapping is done. - Stuart -

I would have installed the felt underneath the 63mm wide joists joists as a matter of typical procedure.
