benjas11 August 2007#1
Hello, This is my first post to this forum, though I've been reading extensively from it for months. I’ve attempted to comply with the guidelines for posting to this forum. Let me know if I need to improve anything. I've just begun actively planning a conversion of what used to be a two car garage into a studio/rehearsal space. I've been more passively researching and playing with different designs and ideas for months (really for years). I own and have read both Rod Gervais' and Cooper's books on designing/building a studio. I've re-read parts of Rod's book several times. I'm a little on information overload. Thus far I've mostly been focusing on familiarizing myself with all the issues I need to consider, rather than getting down to specifics. About a month ago I realized that, unless I wanted to spend a year or two in the design phase I was going to need some design help. I hired an architect (with hands on building experience) friend of mine to help me get down to specifics, make sure the design is code compliant, and generally help me think of things I wouldn't have thought of. Though we've really just gotten started this has already proved worth the money, as he's thought of some things which will improve the studio that I never would have considered. Before I go further, a little about myself. I'm a classically trained semi-professional violinist (hoping someday to be full time). I play a variety of non-classical music, such as jazz, swing, bluegrass, fusion (full electric rock band), irish, etc. I play with multiple bands, gig multiple times a week (gig or rehearse almost every day) and have had the dream of having a functional home studio for over a decade. The studio will be used for, in order of importance: 1) Practicing and rehearsing my own acoustic music into the wee hours. 2) Recording my own acoustic music into the wee hours 3) Teaching students 4) Recording and rehearsing my own amplified rock/jazz music. This will be less often, but I still want to allow for it. 5) Possibly helping to pay for gear (and get some recording practice) by recording other bands demos and such. 6) My home office 7) Crash pad for visiting friends/musicians My wife (a classical violist who teaches Suzuki violin/viola) and I moved to Portland, OR, USA just over a year ago. We bought a house in an urban neighborhood (41' X 100' lot) that has a large (inside dimensions ~25' X ~21') existing detached 2 car garage with an apartment above it. The plan is that I will build my studio in the garage, and my wife will teach lessons upstairs in the apartment, and we get both activities out of the house. I've attached a variety of pictures to give a feel for the space. I reached my max attachments, so I'll do another post with more. I need the studio to be as isolated reasonably possible from the apartment upstairs. It will probably be ok to work around my wifes teaching schedule, or have her teach in the house if a big recording project is underway. But I'd still like to isolate as much as I reasonably can from upstairs. We may need the possibility of renting out the upstairs if finances ever get tight, and I want the studio to still be useable. I also need the main tracking/rehearsal room to be as isolated as reasonably possible from the neighbors. I have not yet measured sound levels, but there are sometimes very loud children next door about 15' from the studio. For this reason I'm planning on full room-within-room double walls/ceiling shown in the 50-60db STC range in the books I have. My house is about 20' away from the studio on the South side, and my wife is, despite being a musician, fairly intolerant of constant music going on. So this studio is a necessity for the lifestyle I want (where I play as much as I can). In fact, because our bedroom window is ~30 from the garage, and we sleep with windows open, and she always goes to sleep before I do, my goal is that my wife cannot hear an acoustic group (still pretty loud, though nothing like drums, etc.) playing in the main studio room. That's probably my pickiest neighbor requirement. I've been fortunate to have a well paying day job for several years which has allowed me to save up for this day. I'm not rich, but I've saved up, and I'm ready to make this happen. I'm really excited, but kind of dreading the process. I'm not particularly handy. I wish I could just pay the money and have it done right, but I don't think my budget allows for paying someone else to do it all, even if I could trust a contractor to do it right. I've allocated $20K for the initial build, though I really have no idea if this is a good figure to shoot for. If I can get it done for less, great, I'll buy some nice mics to get me started. If it goes a little over, it's not going to break me. I can't go way over that figure. I plan on doing a lot of the work myself (with friends who want studio karma), and also hiring a carpenter and other professionals as needed. I have almost no building experience, and a moderate fear of large power tools because of my violinist fingers and friends horror stories. Re: pictures of the garage. The previous owner removed the garage door, filled it in with sheet rock, and put down a vapor barrier and pergo on the slab floor. He did a terrible job of filling in the garage door, and I plan to tear it down, and do it over more carefully, and replace the door. My plan is to just leave the floor alone, at least for the first pass construction. I’ll put down area carpeting or something to adjust acoustics as needed. You'll notice there is a post in the middle of the room, and a large beam running the length of the room. My hope is that I will be able to remove the post by reinforcing the beam with steel (and that this can be done for a reasonable sum). I’ve been in garage studios with a post, and I find it breaks up the flow. In some of my early layout attempts I tried working in the full control room, etc. similar to the designs I’ve seen on this forum, by John Sayers and others. Darol Anger ( a world class professional fiddler who moved to Portland about the same time I did) has expressed interest in sharing this space with me. I’m really excited about that prospect, as he has a lot of great gear (mics!) I could use, and he’s had a lot of experience so I think I’d learn a lot. Anyways, he strongly suggested I leave the bulk of the space as a multi-purpose room. His opinion is that when space is your premium the rooms become too cramped feeling when you divide them up that much. He thought one isolation room big enough for drums or whatever else we wanted to use it for would be enough. He’s also recorded multiple albums in a garage about my size that he used to own in CA which was basically one room. He suggested building a ventilated cabinet for the computer if needed to silence it. He said it might not even be necessary given how quiet a lot of the new computers are. It means I can’t monitor while you are recording without headphones, but there are other advantages, such as a much simpler overall design. Anyways, after he suggested this, another good friend said the same thing, and when I read in Rod Gervais book on page 226, where he says he prefers a multi-purpose room given a space restriction, that pretty much sealed the deal. I’m now strongly leaning towards a more open floor plan. The more I think about it the more this makes sense to me, especially since I’ll likely be recording myself, and rehearsing/practicing in the room is the #1 important use scenerio for me. I’m considering a basic layout like the following: A couple of comments on this layout: a) The dimensions are not exact, I’m trying to get the overall layout then have my architect friend get down to specifics. b) Ideally I’d like some natural light in the studio. I understand this compromises ideal isolation. The above layout shows an outside window into the front isolation room. I’m thinking this room could double as my office (with a rolling desk I could roll out as necessary). My architect friend suggested a custom “door” on hinges that would swing and attach towards the ceiling to close the window off for better isolation, but could be left open most of the time, and provide some light through the glass into the main room. The outside window would be operable, and given the relatively mild weather in Portland, might make it possible to not extend HVAC to this room, thus making the system more affordable. Haven’t decided on that whole subject though. I understand that an operable window greatly impacts isolation performance, but I do like fresh air and natural light, and I think it would make that front room more multi-purpose. I’m hoping that, because there will be a full double wall from the isolation room to the main room, that the main room will remain isolated enough from the outside. c) The upstairs apartment has really amazing vaulted ceilings with angles that look like there were designed for acoustics. Isolating from outside it would be really difficult, but I’d like to plan on trying to get some conduit connecting the two rooms, so later on I could expand upstairs when my wife isn’t using that room. d) The hot water heater shown is for the apartment upstairs. The plan is to “flip” this into the shed, and replace with an on demand electric water heater, since the apartment’s hot water aren’t used often. e) There is a one story shed leaning off the back. It is fairly solid. I plan to use it for any mechanicals need for the studio, and for storage for the studio. It also houses stuff like bikes, camping gear, etc. which would otherwise be in the garage. f) I intentionally put the airlock, bathroom, isolation room on the south side since that’s the direction of my house, and my wife is the pickiest neighbor I am likely to have. OK, after all that (sorry about the long post), here are some questions: 1) Any overall thoughts about my current plans for a multi-purpose room or anything else that might help me avoid mistakes? Any better layouts which meet my goals? 2) The existing garage walls are covered in sheet rock. I believe they don’t have insulation behind them. The exterior garage wall appears to be some kind of particle board, covered in a black felt paper, covered by cedar siding. Our plan is to, as a first construction step, tear off the interior sheet rock from both the walls and the ceiling. This will allow access to existing electrical, etc. We’ll then fill with insulation, and create a double wall system per Rod’s book. From what I’ve read I’ll get better isolation, and use up less space by tearing that sheet rock off, then by leaving it. Does this sound right? 3) The current ceiling height is 9’4”. We plan to vault (6 degrees) towards the center beam a second ceiling which will be isolated as much as reasonably possible (per Rod’s book) from the beam and floor joists it will hang from. This will bring the ceiling height down to an average of about 8’4”. With this ceiling height, and the majority of my space multi-purpose, there is no way I can design according the optimal room ratios suggested by the experts. However, since my room is no longer in Rod’s “Small Room” category, maybe this isn’t quite so critical? The isolation room can be tweaked to approximate one of ratios I think. I will be avoiding parallel walls. Any thoughts on this? 4) There is nothing I can do about the existing beam, short of replacing with solid steel, which probably isn’t going to happen. I’m assuming it will transmit impact noise from above, but that this issue will be much improved because of the isolated ceiling I will add. The beam will be exposed however. Any thoughts on this? 5) The part of this process I’m finding the most daunting is the HVAC system. I’ve had friends with studios tell me everything from, “just open the door when you need air” to “you need the full system”. Someone I play music with just converted his garage (much quicker job then I’m planning on). He just put in a through the wall air conditioner with a door to block it off. This was my first thought after reading about all the complexities involved, and the nightmares people have gone through on this forum. This seems inconvenient, since I want to rehearse late at night, and I’ll need air. It also seems like it puts a huge hole in my isolation I’m going to put a lot of time and money into constructing. I have the large shed behind, which is actually almost like a small 1 car garage. It has a concrete slab. My plan is to use this for any mechanicals necessary. Any thoughts on what might be the most affordable system to provide AC and ventilation while maintaining isolation? From what I’ve read, I don’t need to worry too much about heat. I may just use some electric space heaters when needed. It hurts to think about spending thousands on this part of the studio. From what I’ve read this could eat up a significant portion of my overall budget. I looked at the exchange room concept. I’m not sure the shed is insulated enough for that, and in the long run seems like it would add in a pretty big inefficiency you’d end up paying for anyways. 6) Is my budget realistic? Thanks in advance! I’ve already learned a LOT by snooping around for the last few months. Ben Blechman Violinist/fiddler/software engineer Portland, OR, USA

i'll continue to be brief - very nice design. i think it will work nicely for both purposes and my only concern is the pole location but if you dampen that so its not reflective you should be fine. then when you get to build your live room upstairs, you'll be all set :-) as far as adjustments - i'd make the front corner and back wall a bit deeper and add more trapping. over the mix console i'd add a false ceiling to about mid-room with trapping, and clouds over the live space. i'd use slats and diffusion all round to keep the room as alive as possible and then add treatments to get it balanced for purpose.
