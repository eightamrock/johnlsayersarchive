Him Simone, and welcome! It's good to see a newcomer who reads the rules and does a great job of sticking to them. Great first post!. First, it looks like you have a pretty decent basic plan, just some details need ironing it.
I'm convinced of the advantages of flush-mounting the speakers in a small (and maybe not-so-acustically-perfect) environment, avoiding the rear wall reflections....but, how difficult would it be to change speakers at a later stage?
If you search the forum, you'll find a thread by Barefoot (I think!) with a design for doing just that: He created a large center panel in the soffit which can be removed and replaced with a different one, to accommodate various sized speakers. You just need to make it larger than the largest speaker you ever expect to use, and you'll be fine.
2) At what angle should the two sliding doors between control room and live room be? in my drawings they are at a 6 degree angle... would that be enough to avoid / reduce reflections between them?
To me, it looks like that angle is rather more than 6°! I may be wrong, but it looks more like 12°. But anyway, the minimum angle you want is 6° each side (12° total), and the actual angle depends on what you do with your speaker / soffit / mix position geometry. Currently you have yours at 60°, but if you increase the angle of intersection of the acoustic axes of your speakers (as you probably will need to do! see below...), then you also need to increase the splay angle of your walls in order to have a good reflection free zone. But having a bigger angle than you need does no harm, so if you like your current 12° angle, then stick with it. That would be good enough up to about 90° speaker axes intersect angle. The same is true of the ceiling: that also needs to be angled at 12°.
3) If I build a step at the rear of the control room, how should I fill in the space underneath? Do I use the same principle as I do in treating the walls / ceiling?
What kind of "step"? Do you mean a small platform for the sofa, or something liek that? If so, then build it like a drum riser.
Would I be able to use / recycle the Auralex Wedgies perhaps in the Acoustic Hungers (both side of the Fiberboard) instead of the usually used insulation material?
I don't know how well they'd work on hangers, but a better use might be as part of a cloud, or part of your bass trapping, or part of first reflection point treatment. They probably aren't thick enough to be really effective, but you could still use them along with mineral wool or fiberglass insulation, maybe as a "sandwich" layer or something. To deal with the "ugly factor", you could cover them with cloth.
I would also like to recycle the two windows that are present in the existing construction, which are standard ones.
That probably wont work. Ordinary window glass is too thin to be of much use in studios, and also isn't laminated. Best glass for studios is thick laminated glass. The exact thickness will depend on your wall construction, since you need to keep the surface density constant over the entire surface.
planning to use two thick glass windows at an angle on the "inner construction"
Yep! Thick laminate glass is what you need. What do you mean by "at an angle"? Are you talking about angling each pane of glass downwards slightly, or left-right slightly? If so, that has no acoustic advantages at all (despite myths and legends to the contrary that you might see oozing around the internet), and in fact it will decrease your isolation. For best isolation, the two panes of glass in a window should be parallel and as far apart as possible. Angling them always reduces isolation, all other things being equal. You might want to angle them a bit to avoid annoying lighting reflections, but not for acoustics. Even then, it might be better to carefully think out your lighting plan to avoid such reflections in the first place.
Would I be able to still get a "decent" isolation by using double (or even a triple) glazed windows
Triple glazed? NEVER! That's a three-leaf system, which is a bad idea in a studio (unless it is extremely thick glass, with large air gaps between). Stay well away from anything that has three leaves. Normal double-glazed windows probably won't do the trick either, as once again the glass is too thin, and the panes are too close to each other, to give you good isolation, especially at low frequencies.
(with the additional advantage of being able to open the windows on a sunny day and get fresh air in
An operable window is probably not a good idea: For proper MSM isolation you need total air-tight seals in both leaves. That's very hard to achieve with an operable window.
I haven't got money for a proper AC system yet, so I was looking for a portable one (here's the link for what I have in mind (one of the first three ones): http://www.airconco.com/portable-air-conditioners) which I reckon should be enough
Those portable units are usually VERY noisy, and in any case, I don't see how you can use it without cutting a huge hole in your excellent hermetically sealed 2-leaf MSM wall, to poke the air hose and drain pipe out of.... Search the forum for HVAC, to look for the best way of doing this, but probably a min-split system would be the best way to go, and that won't cost you much more than the unit you have in mind. (Or at least, where I live there isn't much difference in price: that might be different where you live). You will also need a roper ventilation duct system with silencers, as part of your HVAC, but that goes without saying, no matter what type of air con you use.
Having a concrete floor suggests to me that I shouldn't need a floating floor
Exactly! In fact, you almost NEVER need a floating floor, anywhere. Doing one right is really hard, and really expensive. You could blow your entire budget just on doing a floating floor... In any event, you do not need one at all, since your floor will be thick reinforced concrete slab on grade. There is nothing better than that! A large chunk of dense mass, damped by the entire planet underneath it.... hard to beat that!
If I want a wooden floor, shall I just lay down the underlayer and then the wood on top of the concrete (as you would do in your house
Yup!
Any suggestions / comments / criticisms are very welcome !
1) You need to fix your speaker geometry! Right now, it looks like you have your mix position at the 50% point of the control room (front to back). Worst possible spot! The ideal is at 38%, but it doesn't have to be exact, so don't beat yourself over the head trying to nudge things around by millimeters to get there. It's just the theoretical best spot. If you can get close, then you are fine. But 50% is bad. 2) Also, your CR appears to not be symmetric: your right wall is parallel to the outer leaf, but the left wall is not. You should fix that, probably best down by splaying your right inner-leaf to match your left. You absolutely do want to have a perfectly symmetrical CR, or at the very least the front half needs to be symmetrical. 3) You also need to fix the wall between your CR and LR: Right now that wall is coupled in several places, and you will have very poor isolation. Each room needs to be independent, totally decoupled from the other. Build it as a 2-leaf wall, where one leaf belongs to the CR and the other leaf belongs to the LR. The ceilings must be independent too: The LR ceiling rests ONLY on the LR inner-leaf walls, and the CR ceiling rests ONLY on the CR inner-leaf walls. There must be no mechanical connection at all between the two rooms (inner-leaves), nor must there be any connection between the inner leaves of the rooms, and the outer leaf, which is the shell of the building in your case. Anyway, those were the issues that I happened to notice at first glance. Others might find other things, but those are the glaring "biggies". - Stuart -

Stuart i have a question that may relate to the topic here, Why are we to angle glass to avoid standing waves, but not the double walls? wouldn't you expect that in a room within a room build (or any double wall) that the two walls should not be parallel? is because you're essentially dealing with two inner surfaces of insulation (facing each other) in each wall and not straight drywall? whereas with Glass you have a perfect flat surface and thus standing wave potential?
Adding to what André and John said about not needing to angle glass or walls, the way I understand it (which might be wrong) is that angling the walls does not get rid of the standing waves: it just moves them around. The room will still have modes, but they will be harder to figure out. You can no longer do it with simple math if the walls are not parallel. But having said that, I'm not sure if you are talking about "walls" or "leaves". To clarify the jargon, for me a studio "wall" is practically always an MSM system composed of two "leaves" with a damped air gap between them. Each "leaf" is the sum total of all massive surfaces that contribute to that side of the wall, including the drywall itself, the window glass and the door panels that belong to that leaf, as well as all of the associated framing and sealing. So if you talk about a "wall", to me that means you are talking about the entire tuned two-leaf MSM construction that separates and isolates one acoustic space from another acoustic space. In that case, the space between two walls is the room itself, while the space between two leaves is the cavity inside a single wall. (I hope I'm explaining this clearly, because after reading it over, it sure ain't so clear to me, and I'm the guy that just wrote it!!! :) ) Anyway, I think you might be talking about the cavity between the leaves, rather than space inside the room, so that's what I'll assume from here on. If the two leaves of a wall are not parallel, then all basically what happens is that the MSM system will be tuned to a range of frequencies defined by the maximum and minimum gap, instead of a specific frequency as you'd normally get with parallel leaves. I may be wrong here, but logically I would expect that the result would be to "smear" the MSM resonant frequency, and hence broaden the dip in the transmission loss curve for that wall, while also flattening it out a little. So the dip will not go as low, but will be much wider. In other words, you won't have quite so much low-frequency reduction in TL, but you will have reduction in TL happening over a wider range of frequencies. The dip isn't so sharp, but is now wider. (Not sure if I'm making sense). That may or may not be a good thing, depending on where the highest part of that dip falls. If it gets too close to the lower end of the audible range, then it compromises isolation, but if it stays far away from the lower end, then it should improve isolation, since the dip itself isn't as low. That's just my own theorizing: I may be wrong. Maybe André or John can comment more on that. Getting to the point: If the TL dip is still there but smeared out due to the angled leaves, then obviously the standing waves in the cavity did NOT disappear : They are still there, but now there are more of them going around at different angles, doing all kinds of different things all over the place, making it harder to predict mathematically what is going on. The angle doesn't get rid of them: it just "moves" them to different locations. I guess you could say it is kind of like splaying walls to get a "reflection free zone" around the mix position. That does not actually stop reflections from getting to the mix position at all: They still get there eventually, but delayed enough in time and attenuated enough in intensity that they are no longer a problem. Or another analogy would be parallel walls vs. no parallel walls for resonance: Making walls non-parallel does not get rid of resonance in the room: it just changes the characteristics of the resonance. You cannot get your RT60 time down to zero just by tilting and splaying the walls of your studio at weird and wonderful angles. All you will achieve, at best, is changing the "timbre" of the resonance, but it will still be there. Long story short: There are pros and cons to angling walls, leaves, and windows. It all depends on how you do it, and what you are trying to achieve. Like john said, you don't NEED to do it, since parallel works just fine (and is easy to calculate, easy to build). So if you DO do it, then you should be well aware of WHAT you are doing, WHY you are doing it, and what the outcome will be. If you don't want to go to all that trouble of figuring it out, then just stick to parallel: it makes the math easier, and also makes the construction easier. Anyway, that's my long, meandering, convoluted and meaningless waffle on the subject, which I'll probably find just as unintelligible as you just did, when I wake up tomorrow morning and read it back to myself! :) - Stuart -

- what do you mean by "the exact glass thickness will depend on your wall construction, since you need to keep the surface density constant over the entire surface" ? How do I calculate that?
The idea is that you want the density of the wall, in terms of kilograms per square meter of wall area (or maybe pound per square foot, if you are back in the 18th century still.. :) ) to be roughly the same at all places on each leaf. So if it turns out that you used enough drywall, green glue, and whatever else to get to (for example) 15 kg/m2 for the leaf itself, then you should get glass that also works out to about 15 kg/m2. If you were to get less dense glass, then you have created a weak point in the wall, since where the window is, the density drops (and thus the MSM frequency goes up, you lose isolation, etc.). If you were to use higher density glass, then that's not an issue really . . . Except that you wasted a lot of money on it, because thick laminate glass glass ain't cheap! How do you calculate it? Look it up on the tables of densities of common building materials! :) There are tables the give you the figures for numerous types of building materials, or you can probably ask the manufacturer, too. Glass is several times more dense than drywall, so your glass will not be as thick as your drywall. I can't give you exact figures, because it all depends on what type of drywall and glass you use, but it isn't hard to figure out. EG. If your glass is three times the density of your drywall, and you ended up with two layers of 15mm drywall, then you have a total of 30 mm of drywall, so you only need 10mm glass to maintain the same density. That's just an example!!!
- you said that there is no acoustic advantages in angling each pane of glass in the window, but I notice that on "The Recording Manual" on John's website he states "The two sheets of glass must be at an angle to each other else the two sheets will interact in a resonate sympathy and the sound reduction properties will be reduced.
John wrote that book a long time ago, and like he said above, he used to think that it was necessary, but now he doesn't. It use to be a "generally accepted rule" that you should angle glass (and years ago, everyone DID do it), but today it is now known that you get better isolation by keeping the glass parallel and putting damping (fiberglas, mineral wool, etc) around the interior frame to deal with the resonance. It works better like that. You can also use two slightly different thickness of glass (one on each leaf), if you are concerned about having the panes themselves resonating at the same frequency.
obviously, if I can get away with NOT splaying the glasses ..... it will make my life SO much easier :-) What do you suggest?
The issue is this: Isolation in an MSM wall depends mainly on the mass of each leaf, and the air gap between the leaves. A smaller air gap reduces isolation severely, while a arger air gap increases it nicely. So if you angle your glass, then obviously you have to make the gap SMALLER at one edge of the glass. Thus, at that edge you have thrown away depth, and thrown away isolation. EG, if you have a 6 inch air gap, but you angle the glass down to 2 inches on one edge, you have given up a lot of isolation across the entire surface of your glass, and thus created a weak point in your entire wall. If you keep the glass parallel, then you keep the entire isolation that you started with, no compromises. Of course, if you really do want to angle your glass, you can compensate for the isolation lost due to the angle by using much thicker glass, but then your cost goes up.... Designing a studio is a juggling match! Fiddle here, fiddle there, play with thicknesses, distances, materials, prices, and then yell and scream as nothing works out the way you want.... :) So really, it's up to you. If you can design your lighting so that you wont get annoying glare and reflections on the glass as you look through it, then great! Use parallel glass in your windows. But if there is no way you can keep glare off the glass, and "glaring glass" is a deal-breaker for you, then go ahead and angle it, but make it thicker to compensate, and talk even more nicely to your bank manager...

won't it make the sound from the speakers radiate "unbalanced" (for lack of a better word to describe it) as the left side is Glass and the right side is wood (Slot resonators)?
I think so. It goes back to your symmetry issue again! You need to fix that. A slot wall is a tuned resonator that absorbs certain ranges of frequency and reflects others. It also acts a bit like a diffuser. A glass door doesn't do any of that: it just reflects. So you need to figure out if you are going to get rid of the slot wall on the right of the CR, or fiddle with the glass doors so you can get a slot wall on that side too. For my studio design, I started out with glass doors like you, since I like the idea of good sight lines, but then I figure out (like you will, shortly!) that my room is too small to fit in sliding glass doors and also slot walls and also first reflection point treatment, so I ended up with plain old wood doors from CR to LR, small windows, absorption at the reflection points, and slot walls below and above, wherever I can shoe-horn them in. You might want to consider doing something like that, too. - Stuart -
