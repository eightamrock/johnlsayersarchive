Erock wrote:
Right now the owner wants to keep the garage fully operational (although I will try to change his mind on that), so adding a layer of foam or drywall to each panel is the only idea we've had other than laying a big mattress against it when we play?!?!
Forget foam. Foam is not for soundproofing. Any Web site or manufacturer of foam telling you otherwise is at best misinformed and at worst lying to you. An extra layer of drywall will be an improvement, but alone it is not much of one, and with huge holes the size of windows (an air conditioner in one of them does not negate the effect of the hole in your isolation!!!!!!), you may as well not even bother because of the "weakest link" effect. Mattress? Oh, I'll get to that mattress idea. Read on......
Erock wrote:
If we get complaints, the owner agreed we could pull the window A/C unit out and plug-up the window, then run an A/C duct from the house. We'll even consider putting up another layer of drywall if we still have problems....
OK, so you figure you'll "plug up the window." Where will you get fresh air ventilation? Building code requires that habitable space without operable windows have mechanical ventilation that supplies the room with enough fresh air to keep its occupants safe. My city requires 0.5 ACH (air changes per hour). Oh, and before you even think about it, "We'll just open the doors once in a while to get some fresh air" will not suffice.
Erock wrote:
1. Is that a yes for using the resilient channel with 1 layer of 5/8" drywall as opposed to just screwing drywall to the studs, i.e., the RC1 channel is relatively cheap, so is this the best use for our $$$?
It's clear to me in the way you phrased the question that you don't yet understand the concept of mass-air-mass. Beefing up the outer leaf is a good thing. Resilient channel is a good thing for the inner leaf to decouple it (somewhat) from your outer leaf. (Independent frames are superior.) The size of the air gap between the leaves is every bit as important as the thickness/density of the mass of the leaves. If you don't fully get what I just wrote, and why, then you need to do more studying here on mass-air-mass.
Erock wrote:
2. Use the cheaper pink fiberglass insulation (and save the extra $$ for more caulking, etc.) or use a cotton fiber type in the walls?
The type of insulation is less critical than all of the other factors -- the mass, the air, and the mass. Insulation counts as air as long as it's breatheable. Everything I'm writing has been written here time and time again. Read more here. We can't afford the time to keep repeating the same information all the time.
Erock wrote:
3. What types of inslulation for the attic are good (does it even make much of a difference)?
You're focused on the wrong things.
Erock wrote:
4. Should we bother with glueing foam or drywall to the wood panels of the garage door or just lay a mattress in front of it (yuck)?
Laying a mattress in front of the door will do hardly anything other than attract moisture, dirt, and dust, become a havent for mold and bacteria and other critters, and it might help dampen the door from resonating a little. Again, you're focused on the wrong things and you're allowing yourself to be a sucker for mythology instead of the science that you need to understand in order to be at all successful in your endeavors. Plus, you can't glue foam onto a garage door without increasing the overall weight of the door, and then you risk damaging the door and the opener by overloading the spring(s). I have a lousy entry-level garage door that I considered insulating with a styrofoam insulation kit, but I decided against it when I consulted with a couple of garage door experts who told me that ruining my opener and hardware was inevitable in time.
Erock wrote:
5. Installation instructions for resilient channel recommend using 2" neopreme pads to support the bottom of the drywall on the walls where they meet the floor. Does anyone know where to get this at a good price or an inexpensive substitute?
No, you don't need Neoprene pads... But you do need there to be a gap between the drywall and the floor, and then that gap needs to be filled in with backer rod and acoustic sealant (caulk), which is not the same as regular caulk and is generally not sold in home improvement stores / Home Depot.
