knightfly29 June 2004#82
For in-wall service, you can use standard house insulation - if you want to spend more, Owens Corning's SAFB's (Sound Attenuating Fire-resistant Batts, I think) are denser (around 2.5 PCF as opposed to just under 1 PCF (Pounds per Cubic Foot) - any heavier than that, and you start degrading low frequency isolation. The foam insulation that Home Depot sells is probably all closed cell, and as knightfly says, is useless for soundproofing, although it has some mold uses. knightfly22 July 2004#89
OK, bit of a break in the fray - 2 - RC should NEVER be flush with an intersecting wall or ceiling - the idea is to not have ANY solid connection between adjoining wall or ceiling surfaces, so they don't transfer vibrations between each other. This is why using never-setting acoustic rated caulk is necessary; it's the only barrier that continues the (effectively) one-piece mass leaf around the various corners without transferring vibration in the process. Always leave at least a 1/2" gap between the end of a piece of resilient channel and the intersecting wall/ceiling surface, so there is no chance of direct coupling between the two. 3 - see the attached drawing, I tried to include enough details here to get you through this part. AC - One of the reasons I suggested a single sloped shed-type roof was so you could install a window unit "upstairs", placed in either side of one of the higher corners, and still make a "trap door" access in the wall that intersects the one the AC unit was cut into - by doing a center-peaked roof, this makes things more difficult for that plan. With your peaked roof, if you mount the AC in the peak with its control panel inward (as would be necessary to use the attic as a chilled plenum) I can't think of any easy way to access the controls. At this point, the only solution I can think of is to maybe do a small "dormer" near one end of your peak and mount the AC in the dormer, then do a small access door in the gable end of the roof so you can reach the controls. This entire plenum area would be outside your double envelope sound control construction. Before I could offer much more on the AC, I need to see pix of how far you've completed the roof, ceiling, etc, and where you might place the AC unit. As to pressures, if you were to build two duct-board lined, double-sheet rocked ducts with a couple of sharp bends in each and place a quiet fan at the start of one of them for circulation into/out of the studio, just placing the AC unit high as possible in the attic would allow it to self-exhaust. You would always keep the AC in the "fresh air" mode. SAFB's are fine; you might be able to save some $$ with rockwool, but 703 may be as expensive as the SAFB's, depending on your local sources. Where you're only using two layers of drywall, I'd stick with 5/8 for both layers. A third layer could be 1/2, or even 3/8 sandwiched between two layers of 5/8 - but before that would make sense, you'd need to be doing a full sound lock door and more elaborate cooling system or it would be a waste of time and money. Door swing - out might be easier; you could conceivably add an extra layer, at least an inch wider all around than the frame, and weatherstrip that corner for a better seal - I'll do a pic if you don't follow - Outside sheathing - see my attached drawing for the importance of seals and where - your lower floor sheathing and your outer wall sheathing should effectively be one piece; if they're not, heavy caulking is needed so they work that way. Portable AC - good parts are no ducts, no breaks in the walls, easy to do, and (duh) portable. Bad parts - no fresh air, just cooling - too noisy to have running when recording (the chilled attic method would probably allow this) and takes up valuable space in a small room. Cost is about the same for either from what I've seen. Shoot me some pix of your roof/ceiling, etc, so we can work out whatever's left... Steve

knightfly22 July 2004#92
Insulation - the 4" SAFB would probably be your best choice in this case. You could also use 1" 703 and 3-1/2" fluffy fiberglass stacked if it's cheaper; it would give close to the same results, probably within 1-2 dB either way. Stacking the 1" 703 and normal insulation batts would give a slight compression to the soft batts, which would damp the wall panels about the right amount; this should also happen using the 4" SAFB's, so it would come down to which is cheaper. I see the roof peak; my question is, how would you mount a window AC so it cools the attic and you still have access to the controls? My only suggestion would be to add a shed dormer to the roof on one side, put the AC in that overhanging the roof, and use the end gable to put a small access door at right angles to the AC mount. If you build your soundproof ducts with a couple of bends, line them with duct board (703 with a facing, basically) and separate the entrance/exit points, makins sure that the cool air duct draws its air from the lowest point in the attic and the warm air return comes back to the attic a ways away from the cool duct (and near the top of the space) then all you'd need is a medium sized, quiet fan to move the air in and out of the attic. One thing I'm NOT seeing that needs to be done before going any further, is insulation in the attic - both gable ends and the inside of the roof will need this. Because this attic will always be cooler than the outside, before you insulate you need to put a breathable house wrap (tyvek or equivalent, NOT asphalt-impregnated roofing felt) up against the inside of the sheathing, then if your insulation has a facing it should go against the sheathing, not toward the attic space - for this area, since it's not sound proof, you might be better off just using faced foam board insulation and gluing it in place with liquid nailz, putting the facing toward the OSB sheathing. Since the floor of the attic is bordering a cooled space (the studio) you won't need insulation there - makes it easier to get up there and do anything you need to do, as long as you make the access door big enough for a crawl through. Does your freebie AC have electronic controls, or actual knobs and switches? If the latter, you won't have to crawl up there each time and start it and set temps, etc - you could just use a heavy duty switch mounted inside the room and switch the actual power to the unit. You can find out if this will work by plugging the unit in, setting the controls, then unplugging it, waiting about half a minute, and plugging it back in - if it comes up in the correct mode, you've got the option of wiring it to a switch (or breaker, depending on your wiring job) instead of climbing up to turn it on/off. Place is lookin' good, man - you have every right to be proud of your accomplishments... Steve

knightfly25 July 2004#98
The AC unit, since it's designed for a stand-alone operation, should be installed in the attic just as if you were going to "live" there - in other words, the control panel would be inside the attic with the majority of the AC case hanging outside (be sure to tilt the outside of the unit down a few degrees, so you get proper drainage and operation) Then, you have a "chilled attic" - from this, NOT from the AC unit itself, you would pump cool air from a low spot in the attic thru heavy-walled ducting with at least two bends and several feet of length, INTO the ceiling and into the studio - at a point far away from the entry duct, you would cut a return hole and install a return duct, also double-walled and lined with duct liner fiberglass, and this duct should also have at least two bends and its other end should terminate near the AC unit and at a higher elevation than the cold air send duct. You pick up cold air low, return warm air high in the attic. You will need some type of quiet booster fan to keep air moving through the ductwork - Home Depot sells 3 different sizes of in-line duct booster fans, they run between $25 and $45 depending on size. The 8" round ones are pretty quiet, and if placed at the end of the duct furthest from the studio (as the air travels) should be inaudible. YOu should use flex duct to attach the fan, and shock mount it with either some sort of bungee cord or possibly just let the flex duct support it (taped in place with duct tape) - If you want to get tricky, buy one of those after-market electronic speed controls for routers and drills - they're about $25, and would let you slow down the fan to control noise, with a reduction in air supply. Oh, I think the drawing of this concept (linked on page 3 of this thread) may have one flaw - if you exhaust the air from the studio back to the outside, you might unbalance the pressure in the "chiller" room - this might work better by bringing the warm air back to the chiller room instead of directly outside. I just noticed this; maybe we can get a more learned opinion from Rod Gervais on this - hopefully before you're ready to do that part... Steve

Pucks - 2x4's WILL span to every other joist - if you use too many pucks, you won't get enough compression to make them work and may as well not even use them. Please trust me on this one.
