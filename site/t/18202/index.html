TomVan4 March 2013#1
This is probably one of the most ambitious builds I have seen on John's site. I am fortunate to help a friend of mine build his dream studio. The planning stages have taken many turns along the way but finally the plan has taken shape and we are off to a great start. He hired a structural engineer to draw up and manage the first phase of this build. It was quite the undertaking and we were happy to have him and the professional team that assisted in the lowering of the floor and re-building of the foundation. The entire basement was lowered 2 feet. I must start this by saying the house is over 3600 square feet,three stories and 100 years old. The inside of the house was remodeled extensively 10 years ago and we wanted to make sure the integrity of the floors above the basement didn't settle and or crack any of the plaster work in the living part of the house. The entire basement is approximately 1300 square feet. It will include a work out room, a bathroom and storage area. The rest of the space will include the studio and the following rooms; Live room/ ensemble room approximately 300 square feet Control room approximately 150 square feet ISO booth Approximately 150 square feet Break room 150 square feet Still have to meet with an HVAC installer to make sure we can do what we need to the space. Daikins are on the list right now but we will wait to see if they come up with something different. I am not a sketch up artist but can do a basic layout. All walls are decoupled, MAM and most will be inside out walls to save as much space as possible. The ceiling will be RSIC with hat channel and the floor will be polished concrete to a 1500 level finish. I don't want to say the budget has no limits, but we will do what we need to do to make it probably the best studio in the area. Notice the helical piles. They are drilled through the dirt to support the foundation. They were drilled 67 feet to bed rock. Also notice the house HVAC and water heater are suspended. They will have a platform built underneath to support. The expense to lower them was not cost efficient and a platform was the better of the options. The wine rack was left down there to put in the break room in case you are wondering what it is. With that said, "THIS IS A PROJECT STUDIO" for personal use only. This will "NOT" be a commercial studio.

You might want to leave the final polishing until right at the end, since doubtless the surface will be marred in some way during construction. You also might want to consider covering the floor with OSB as much as possible while you build: a dropped hammer can easily chip concrete... Or a dragged tool box. Or a leaky tube of caulk. Or a can of paint kicked over accidentally. Or ... :)
Furnace stand, new stairs and door
Nice! Did the furnace go on isolation mounts? If not, you might want to consider that. Probably not a biggie at all, but if you are going to all this trouble and expense, you might as well do everything possible to make it perfect. Isolation mounts would keep any mechanical vibrations made by the furnace from getting into the slab. You could also add isolation sleeves to the ducts, to decouple them from the furnace itself, for the same reason: Keep unwanted noises out of the building itself.

TomVan5 March 2013#7
Stuart, Improvements are always welcome. We are trying to utilize the space to it's maximum and line of site is very important. He is a guitar player and will have all his amps (which are too many to count) in the ISO. That is why we put the ISO where we did. We are toying with the idea of building an inset shelf/cabinet in the control room with all the amp heads, for easy tweaking The live room will house a drum set and 3 amps for live sessions and tracking, plus a small PA. The bathroom and stair case are unmovable obviously and the storage area leaves access to irrigation and the home sound system. The workout area seemed the most obvious but if you have some ideas, please by all means. My company polishes concrete floors for a living. To properly get in all the tight corners and close to walls, it will be much easier and a much nicer finished floor to polish it first. Many people use the word polished concrete loosely and don't understand the difference. We will actually hone the concrete with varying levels of diamond blades and very large grinders. It is a very similar procedure like marble and granite is polished. NO Sealer will be applied until the end of the project. But a hardener will be applied right after the polishing is completed. Protection is very important and we will do our best. I attached a picture of polished and dyed concrete. This is what the floor will look like when we are finished. The furnace will be in its own room decoupled from the rest of the build. However the doors to the room will need to be louvered for air flow because they are gas. Those doors will be in the hallway outside the studio. In addition the furnace is very quiet. But I think a once over is notable, just to make sure. Tom

"Notice the helical piles. They are drilled through the dirt to support the foundation. They were drilled 67 feet to bed rock" Just for clarity so others who view do not get the idea that this is an acoustic device, the helical piles were installed to coordinate with the footprint of the build and to support the existing structure once the existing support was removed. I have the same notions that the HVAC may not be all it could be but I do agree that the floor polishing should happen before the walls are installed but protection after the fact is a must. any hard material will most likely be a minus, we use carpet turned fabric side (top side) facing down to protect flooring as we have found that the shifting of the material underfoot will scratch a hard surface. But you know your job Tom, I am just offering some ideas about other solutions.
