Pointe
I had knowingly neglected my ceiling. The standard ceiling panels likely aren't doing enough, if anything.
If they are down from the ceiling by say 16", and strong enough, then another possibility is putting R31 (9") of ownens corning fiberglass plain (no kraft paper) pink batts up there. This wouldn't change the HF absorption since the ceiling panels are likely already good at that, but it would boost the mid frequency absorption, and possibly the LF absorption. It may be that the ceiling panels are not the usual HF absorbant variety (like gypsum panels or plastic panels), in which case replacing them with something else might be an option. As for wether this is a good thing or bad for your room -- that's not entirely obvious. But it's certainly in your price range. http://www.bobgolds.com/AbsorberCeilingJoist/home.htm
z6061123 July 2006#6
Pointe
Will extra panels behind the speakers remove too much high and mid?
If it's SBIR like issues confusing the sound (or variations on this theme such as you can't detect really good imaging), then covering the entire front wall with 1/2" linacoustic, and the first reflection points on the side walls, and improving the absorption of the ceiling, might help get rid of reflections between 2ms and 30ms by absorbing them. If it's just the side walls that are the problem, the cheapest thing to do is to toe in the speakers and take advantage of speaker directionality to reduce the dB of the reflections off the walls. For one listener you can laser pointer point the speakers at that person, and set them up on an equalateral triangle (speaker, speaker, listener) -- I believe John Sayer's designs have one triangle point being a little behind the listener, but pointing straight at the listener might be ok too. If your floor is wood or incredibly thin carpet, consider a thick area rug between you and your speakers. If you can envision sound paths that make the sound that comes back to you as long a time as possible, and absorb the shorter paths, that might help too -- but I'm not sure. For stereo, you don't want too much of the room absorbing or you'll loose that nice instruments sound pleasant effect -- i.e. the room is helping the speakers sound better.

John Sayers27 July 2006#9
I believe John Sayer's designs have one triangle point being a little behind the listener, but pointing straight at the listener might be ok too.
Bob, the toe-in has traditionally been the 30 degree angle, but I've used 45 straight at you to great effect. The pointing behind the listener occured because direct was too defining in the high end so when you were eqing the kick on channel one of an 10ft console or adjusting a unit in a side rack you were well outside the directivity of the speakers. By aiming them behind you, or in more accurate terms, stepping into the triangle, you covered the console with even top end response across it's length plus it allowed people either side of you to also hear an even high end. cheers john
