Sound isolation (a.k.a. "soundproofing") is actually a system comprised of both parts. It's not the the outer wall stops sound and the inner wall is decoration. It's that the outer wall and inner wall work together as a tuned system to stop sound. Change something in either of those walls, or even the gap between them, and you change the isolation. So you should approach your design from that point of view, taking the outer and inner as a combined unit, with all parts working together.
Should I mount RC on the existing OSB wall and hang two 5/8 sheetrock or attach two layers of sheetrock directly to the OSB?
If you are going to do true "room in a room" construction, then neither! :) Your outer wall can be only one "leaf", and your inner wall provides the other leaf. The outer wall has drywall (or OSB, or plywood) only ONE side of its studs, and the inner wall has drywall (etc) on only ONE side of its studs. Each set of studs has "stuff" on only one side, for a total of two leaves. You should never have three leaves, even less four leaves, as that would reduce your isolation for low frequencies. 2 leaves, and only 2. Each leaf can be built up from several layers if needed, ut they all go on the same side of the studs. So a leaf for very high insulation might consist of 5/8" OSB directly on then studs, then 5/8" drywall on top of that, then a layer of Green Glue, then a final layer of 5/8" drywall. The system looks something like this:

Or you can also do the inner-leaf "inside-out", like this, which has significant benefits (both acoustical and practical):

If you have several rooms, then your outer shell is one leaf, and each inner room is it's own leaf, as follows:
Image not preserved: msm-2-leaf-WallChunk-conventional-not-inside-out-2.png
Ceilings are the same: each room has it's own independent 1-leaf ceiling that rests only on that room's own walls, and the entire outer-leaf has one single leaf of roof. RC is meant for those situations where it is not possible to build a separate stud frame for the inner leaf, and the drywall has to be on the same studs as the outer leaf. In that case, the RC provides a reasonable level of decoupling, so it is a good solution, but that's not your case since you said you are going to do full room-in-a-room construction. So you do not need RC.
(With green glue caulking to seal edges.)
Careful there! The Green Glue company makes several products, only one of which is acoustic caulk: You use that for sealing gaps and cracks. Their flagship product is Green Glue Compound, which you spread between the layers of drywall. That is not caulk. And it is also not glue! (Despite the name). It is a special viscous-elastic polymer that provides constrained layer damping between the drywall sheets. That is very important for low frequency isolation, and very effective. So that's the way to do it. - Stuart -

Soundman20205 March 2014#4
and 5/8 and 1/2 on the inner. From reading this difference in wall masses with be more effective for a broader freq range?
well, yes, there is a slight advantage to having materials of different thickness or different density, but that advantage disappears when you also take into account the other aspects: total mass, stiffness, resonance, coincidence, etc. For example, with the 5/8 + 1/2 plan, if you make them both 5/8, then you have more mass, more stiffness and the coincidence dip rises to a higher frequency, all of which are good. You lost the slight advantage of having different resonant frequencies, and the slight advantage of the impedance mismatch across surface boundaries, but overall you gained more than you lost. Better still, what you lost is mainly applicable to high frequencies, which are easily isolated and which you had covered anyway, and what you gained is all in the area of low frequencies, which are always a bitch to deal with. In other words, just do everything with 5/8" fir.rated drywall, and you'll be maximizing your isolation.
I know this makes resonators and bass traps easy to integrate but what do people do for esthetics to cover the studs?
Normally: acoustic fabric. Just stretch it across the studs neatly, to hide the "ugly". Some people then put broadly spaced wooden slats over that, mostly for aesthetic reasons, but also to keep the highs in the room, without loosing too much into the absorption. - Stuart -
