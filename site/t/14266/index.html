bwilkinson11 April 2010#2
By the sound of it, (and some of the more seasoned people here hopefully can correct any mistakes I make) you will have a triple leaf with what you are building. If you move from the inside of your booth out, the first leaf will be the drywall that you put on your framing. You then would move, of course, through the airspace between your studs, until you hit the current wall of the loft. That wall would be your second leaf. Then you would move through the airspace of the existing stud wall, until you reach the wall of your neighbors' rooms. That would be the third leaf.

xSpace11 April 2010#4
A hotel or mostly any hallway in a commercial structure is 4 feet wide. The mass/spring/mass of the wall on the one side of the hall and then the 4 feet wide "air" (the actual hallway) and the mass/spring/mass of the opposite wall is the four leaf you are talking about. In a residential building the hallway will be closer to 36 inches, but can be wider as well. Hopefully you will find that you have a solid concrete or block partition that makes up the boundaries of your place. In order to get the frequency down low that air gap in your space should be wide...wide like 36 inches if at all possible. But that doesn't begin to address some of the other issues you are up against. One is you mentioned this space being a "loft" which means it is built on a higher floor level. Flanking could be a bigger issue if you and your neighbors floor are the same, with no breaks in the floor to decouple it from the neighbor side.

Soundman202013 April 2010#13
"Damping" refers to something that kills resonance in a resonant system (reduces the vibrations, or more accurately, absorbs some of the energy). A car shock abnsorber is one example. Another example: try to play a piano with a towel sitting loosely across all the strings, touching them: you can't play like that, since the strings are "damped" by the towel, and they won't vibrate. If your concrete slab is resting completely resting on the ground ("slab on grade"), then the earth underneath it is the "damping": the earth absorbs vibration energy, preventing the slab from resonating (or at least, greatly reducing resonance). But if your slab is only supported at the edges with nothing but air underneath (like it would be for the second floor of your house), then it is not damped well, and will resonate freely. That's the concept of damping. - Stuart -
