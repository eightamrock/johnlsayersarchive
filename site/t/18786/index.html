The basic concept for doing that is known as "room-in-a-room", or "fully decoupled two-leaf MSM isolation" in more technical terms. If you search the forum for those, you'll find numerous references about how to do it.
Do I need to go to that extent using layers of 16mm Gyprock and Barrierboard.
It's not that simple, unfortunately. If it were so easy, then this forum would not exist! :) Attaining high levels of isolation is not as easy as some manufacturers would have you believe. There are no "magical" materials that can beat the laws of physics...

There's concept you should look into, called "room ratio", which is basically about the relationship between the length, width and height of your room. There are "good" ratios, and there are "bad" ratios. This is all related to the modal response of the room, or how the natural resonant frequencies of the room are spread across the audio spectrum. With "good" ratios, they are spread around evenly, not too close to each other, and not too far apart. They are also musically related in the best cases. Bad ratios have the modes spread around haphazardly, with several bunched together, or even right on top of each other (worst case), and large empty gaps between them, where there is no modal support. So the very first thing you should do is to look for good ratios in the general area of the possible dimensions that you have to play with, and decide on what ratio (and therefore what dimensions) you will have for the final inner walls and inner ceiling of the room. Starting from that point, you then work "outwards" to figure out where the outer walls and ceiling of the room will be, and you do that by deciding on how much isolation you want for your room, then allowing for the necessary thickness of the air gap and the building materials. With those two sets of dimensions in place, you can then get your architect to draw up the plans correctly, with the two sets of framing (inner wall and outer wall) properly set in the right positions. Then you can figure out the best places to put your doors, windows, HVAC ducts, and suchlike, based on the acoustic goals for the room and the layout of the rest of the house. Since this is just basically "lines on paper" at this point, there is no cost at all to trying out many iterations until you get the best fit. You can try different things, play around, re-orient the room, etc., and all it costs you right now is the time to do it. The only additional cost that you will see in the actual construction of the room, is the extra cost of materials and labor for the inner wall and inner ceiling, but since they are designed in from the start, it is a whole lot easier (and much cheaper!) than trying to modify an existing room. Search the forum for "room ratio" and "modal response", and you'll come up with a ton of references that you can work through to get an idea of what you'll need to do. Also, plug in the likely dimensions to Bob Golds' calculator (here: http://www.bobgolds.com/Mode/RoomModes.htm ) and take a look at the results. Play around with the numbers until you get a good result.
I thought a skillion style of ceiling might be a good option?
It's probably better to stick to a plain rectangular room for your first attempt at designing a studio. You can go for more complex shapes if you want, but that just add unnecessary headaches: room mode calculations only work for rectangular room with size sides. As soon as you start angling one or more sides, or adding more sides, then the calculations become vastly more complicated. What you COULD do, is to hang angled ceiling clouds inside the finished room, which can give the same effect visually, and are very useful, acoustically. Search the forum for "ceiling cloud", and you'll find many examples of how people have built them and used them in their rooms. - Stuart -
