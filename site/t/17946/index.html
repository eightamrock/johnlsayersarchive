Hi Marc, and welcome! :) Sorry about the delay in getting back to you: there's a lot going on here on the forum right now.
So, I am a bit lost, because I don't know what to do to get the best control room possible.
First, it would help to understand what your goals are for this studio: Is this going to be a commercial facility, where you will have people paying you to record them, or is this more of a hobby studio, just for you and a few friends? In other words, do you need a large control room to accommodate a lot of people together, or do you need a large live room to track big groups? It helps to have the purpose of each room clear, so you can optimize the use of space.
If I do so, I will have a rectangular room, but then I think: "wouldn't it be better to build splayed walls and forget about the rectangular shape"?
Both shapes are good, if done correctly. The advantage of a rectangular room is that the modal response is easy to predict, and it usually maximizes the use of space. The advantage of splaying your walls it that you can get an RFZ easier, and it minimizes flutter echo. Plus, it looks cool. So once again, it helps to define your goals: if you want an RFZ design, then splaying walls is a good way of getting that. If you don't want to get into that amount of complexity and expense, then maybe a simple rectangular shape is better.
I don't know if it makes sense to speak about the budget at this stage ( basic layout ) but I can tell you that my budget is around $50.000
It is always useful to know what your budget is, and you seem to be budgeting very well. That should work just fine. A lot of people come here with a small budget and big ideas, which is often unrealistic. Your budget is realistic.
Can I demolish those pillars?
It MIGHT be possible, but you'll need a structural engineer to come take a look and design an alternative support structure. You probably have the budget to do that, but the question is: "Is it worth it?" How much space could you gain from that, and is it worth spending all that time and money on it? Only you can answer that question. And only a qualified structural engineer can answer the question about feasibility and cost.
This is not a professional studio, it's a hobby, so 90% of the time, I'll be alone, recording myself.
OK, so in your second post you already answered my question above. :) So this is hobby, not commercial, and mostly just you: I'd suggest changing things around to get a larger control room plus a drum booth, rather than a small control room and a large live room that probably won't ever be fully occupied.
So, I wonder if I should go for option A or B.
I would definitely go with option "A". It has all the advantages you mentioned, and the only disadvantage is that you need to go through four doors instead of two to get from the CR to the LR. That probably isn't a big deal, especially if you can duplicate a lot of the CR functionality right in the LR, such as with a second screen, keyboard and mouse for your DAW, good headphone routing, etc. Minimize the number of trips you need to make between the two. Maybe even consider a console that can be controlled remotely: for example, there are some that can be controlled by i-Pad and even i-Pod now. Might be worth looking into.
Do you think it's going to be a pain in the *** to open an extra door and walk these extra steps
Actually, you'll be opening four doors per trip, not two. Your control room door will actually be a pair of doors, back to back, and the live room door will also be a pair of back-to-back doors. (assuming you want good isolation between the studio and the house!). And if you went with option "B", then that would also have to be a pair of back-to-back doors. - Stuart -

Soundman202011 December 2012#8
Hey Marc, Good to see you back! Getting a designer to do it for you can actually save you money, in many ways, For example: it would be rather sad to have to tear things down that were done incorrectly, and re-do them the right way. There are cases right here on the forum of people who have had to do that. Or in optimizing materials: there are also cases of studios that have been waaaaaay over designed, using excessive amounts of materials. As well as cases of people using expensive or exotic materials that they simply did not need to use. So those are all factors that can save you money. On the other hand, you can also do it yourself, if you really want to, and that's what this forum is all about! Helping people to learn how to design their own place. Realistically, it takes several months to learn all the stuff you need to know, then several months more to do the actual design, before you even get to pick up a hammer and a piece of wood. There is just so much that you need to take into account, and learning it all takes time... If you want to go that route, then I'd suggest you start with two books: "Master Handbook of Acoustics" by F. Alton Everest (that's sort of the Bible for acoustics), and "Home Recording Studio: Build it Like the Pros", by Rod Gervais. The first one "MHoA" is more theoretical, about how things work, while the second one is more practical, about how to build it. I also sent you a PM with more information that you might find interesting. - Stuart -
