knightfly27 March 2006#2
Sorry, but yes - this WILL act as a 4-leaf partition. If you were only interested in blocking some specific midrange, it might work better than a 2-leaf; but for music, you want two panes of glass as far apart as your frames allow, and at least the same mass as your wall material. for gypsum, each glass should be slightly less than 1/3 the thickness of gypsum on each side of the wall... Steve

knightfly27 March 2006#7
Guys, first off you CAN equal a wall's performance with glass; if you use at least 1/3 of the gypsum thickness on each half of a 2-leaf wall for your glass thickness, and you VENT the window area into the wall area where there is insulation to help absorb pressure variations, and you make good SEALS all around for both glasses, it can be done. It works a bit better if you can afford laminated safety glass instead of float plate or tempered glass, because the laminations act similar to Green Glue, as a Constrained Layer Damping material. Second, there is NO case where using ANYTHING other than TWO panes of glass separated by as much space as your wall allows, will work well for music isolation. Mass-air-mass is the only practical way to get decent isolation at all frequencies. Period. The minute you add any more layers or air gaps, you LOSE. Lasso, if you have ENOUGH space between the existing double pane and your desired add-on, and you can find a HEAVY glass for the add-on, it may give a bit better results than just the double pane that's already there; I have no sane way of calculating multi-leaf walls so I can't give you a band-specific TL for such a window; I only know that it won't be very good at low frequencies, and that at one point in the midrange it will be BETTER than a 2-leaf by maybe a couple of dB. whatalob, a triple-paned window will NOT keep up with a double paned one - your inner mass leaf and your inner glass should line up, as should your outer ones. Also, what are you referring to as "sheet rock" - gypsum and sheet rock are the same thing... Steve

The main reason for angling the two panes of glass is visual reflections. I've had temporary plexy glass in my control room window for over a year now and for about half that time I had the two "panes" horizontal and if the lights were off in the live room, you'd see yourself 4 times in the reflections. knightfly28 March 2006#11
Yeah, this is another of those situations where acoustics, ergonomics and aesthetics are at odds with each other. Visual reflections can be very distracting, acoustic reflections can cause you to have to build portable absorbers to put in place while mixing, and sound isolation needs maximum air gap and mass. It's like a lot of life; compromises are necessary. It's just good to know what you're giving up when you DO compromise - and what other effects will result when you change something. IIRC, the main point in the "acoustic myths" article was the one that says splayed glass improves isolation by reducing flutter between the glasses; this has very little if anything to do with it. But if you get too carried away tilting glasses, you WILL notice a reduction of isolation; it's caused by the narrower air gap... Steve
