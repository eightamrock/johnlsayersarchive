xSpace20 December 2008#10
"Sound transmition between the live room and the control room, thats really the only area I am truly concerned with. " Hi again Bryon. Getting good with that Sketchup program:) A few things that work for me is to follow wall lines and see where they intersect. Your current design has the exterior of the build using a two leaf design and that is good. But following those lines around you see where corners tie into other walls and existing water heater walls tie into control room walls that intersect back into the perimeter walls. These are the areas that allow flanking, a big contributor to decreased isolation. Using a 2X8 top/bottom plate and staggered 2X4 studs really isn't helping your cause. 2X4 top/bottom plates and completely seperate walls will be far better. But it is going to depend on getting all the other intersects disconnected. I think small rooms, like vocal and such, are more part of the "blind leading the unwilling" syndrome. Better usage of smaller spaces might be a machine room. A small room tends to become acoustically dead as it pertains to vocal/reamp rooms, it is the only way to handle it:) With a few more days, one might even be able to see how the water heater closet could be just as effective? (Assuming electric and not a gas unit) But you do have a good start.

What is on the other side of those doors, and how much isolation do you need?
You only answered half of the question: How much isolation do you need? In other words, how loud will the sound be INSIDE that room, and how quiet do you need it to be OUTSIDE the room? You have a drum kit in your model, and I'm guessing bass and electric guitars too. Loud stuff. Theoretically you could be up around 120 dBC. What level do you need on the other side? Only you and those who live/work in that "outside" space can answer that question. Set up a source of the kind of music you will be playing in there, maybe with your home stereo or some such. Turn down the treble and turn up the bass (to roughly simulate poor isolation), and play the music. Get people from the "outside" area to sit/stand where they would be normally while you are tracking. Start with the volume really low, then turn it up slowly until they say they can hear it/don't like it. Measure the level at that point with a sound level meter. Now you have two numbers: Subtract "B" from "A". That is the MINIMUM amount of isolation you need, very approximately. Now that you know the numbers, you can decide how to build the wall and the door. But you need real numbers, not guesses, and you need them measured with a real meter, in a situation that closely simulates what reality will be. If you don't have real numbers to work with, it is pointless to ask questions about what type of door seals you need. It's like asking "how much food should I serve at my party next week?" when you don't even know how many people are coming! Is it just you and two buddies, or is it the entire neighborhood? Get real numbers, then you can figure out how to attain them. Having said that, I can't recall seeing any finished projects here that needed decent levels of isolation, and used double doors successfully. I may be wrong, and there have been projects like that, but I can't imagine how you would go about sealing double swinging doors to get good isolation. Rod recommends triple seals on his "superdoors", and even then they have to be done very carefully to get decent isolation. And those are huge massive single doors, not double. I think you'd have to work with a high degree of precision to get the doors to mate all around in such a manner as to create a good acoustic seal. Have you done any research on double swing doors on studios, to see: A) if other people have done it successfully, and B:) how they did it. - Stuart -
