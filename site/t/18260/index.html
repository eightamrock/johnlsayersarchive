Very true. Symmetry is critical, but from your images it seems you also have your desk and console right in the middle of the room, which is not a good place. The room needs to be symmetrical to the left and right of your desk, not to the front and back. Set up your room so that it is a rectangle, not a square, and set up your speakers against one of the short walls (which we will call the "front wall"), facing towards the other short wall (the "back wall"). I measured dBs with Monacor SM-2 sound meter (frequency weighting A).
For studio measurements, it is better to measure with "C" weighting, slow response. "A" weighting has fairly heavy roll-off in sensitivity for low frequencies, so it doesn't tell you much about the energy emitted bu drums, bass guitars, the low end of keyboards, etc.
- Acoustic band (through PA) max 90 dB. Typically about 80 - 85 dB. (Acoustic guitars, dobro, mandolin, lapsteel and two voices).
Measured with "C" weighting, I suspect those numbers would be 5 to 10 dB higher, as C weighting will take into account much more of the lows.
- Rock band max 100dB. Typically 90 - 95 dB. (Drums, bass, electric guitars, voices). Not so often.
I would expect those to be about 10 to 15 dB higher, measured with "C" weighting.
Is this data accurate enough?
Good enough to get started! But if you can, then please re-measure at some point, using "C" and "Slow".

Higher is always better, but your existing ceiling is not too low, and if you design and build carefully, using the "inside out" technique for your ceiling, you can keep it high, acoustically.
How quiet we have to be - this is hard to turn this to numbers - maybe you can advice with that number? How to measure it?
There are two issues here: legal issues, and "annoying people" issues. There are probably legal limits in your area, either national or local, that you must respect in order to not have problem with the police. If you call your local municipality, you an get a copy of the noise regulations, and they should show the levels that are allowed at each time of day. This night not even be a problem for you, if you live in a remote area with no neighbors nearby. The other factor is the "annoying other people" issue. If there are other people around you, in the house or garden, for example, then you need to get your levels low enough that it does not bother them. This is subjective, of course, since each person has their own idea about what they think is "loud" or "quiet". But to measure it, set up your sound system inside the room as it is now, play it really loud, at the normal level of a tracking session (probably around 110 to 120 dBC), and start turning it down slowly while those "other people" listen from places where they would typically be. When they tell you that it is quiet enough for them, and they are not being annoyed by it, then note down what the level is at their location. That's how quiet you have to be.
