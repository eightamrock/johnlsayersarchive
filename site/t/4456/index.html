knightfly6 October 2005#6
Plexi is roughly HALF the density of glass - so in order to reach the same TL through a plexi window, you'd need at least twice the thickness in each of two leaves, assuming the same air gap. Glass is 3 to 4 times the density of gypsum, so the glass in each side of a wall needs to be approx. 1/3 the thickness of the TOTAL gypsum (if that's what is used) - IOW, for a wall with 3 layers of 5/8 gypsum each side, you'd want glasses that were about 5/8" thick to match the TL of the wall - with one glass being slightly thicker to avoid identical coincidence frequency. If you were to use plexi, all other reasons against it notwithstanding, you'd need TWICE that thickness (approx. 1-1/4" each side for the above example) to maintain the wall's TL values... Steve

Okay here is my 2 cents. I had a "home" studio for about 7 years here. When I built the studio I did so more for asthetics than for acoustics. This time around I have been doing the research. In my old studio I used 1/4" lucite plexi. Two 2' x 4' pieces seperated by a 1" x 1" frame. Essentially two like pieces of material with a small airgap. This was acoustically horrible and was PURE HELL to see through. It should be noted that quite a few bands that I recorded wanted to shoot video through the window which was a reflection nightmare for that as well. Now, being the common sense genius that I am, it would seem to me.. that if you used 2 pieces of glass. Both different thicknesses, which to me would have different resonant sympathies, they could effectively be mounted parallel with no problems. Acoustic problems that is. Reflection problems would depend on what's directly behind your site line at any given point. It is the same principal that you face with a television screen. If there is a sunlit window at the right angle from your viewing angle, the picture will be difficult to see. Shade the window and you are all set. In most studios, the lighting comes from above so there are problems with these reflections that are difficult to erradicate without angling the glass. From the auralex site I get the impression that they are saying if you angle less than 8 degs then you might as well forget it. So parallel or angled? That is the question. I would think that it depends on what kind of glass you are using, thickness of your wall(s), lighting, etc. This is something that I will be experimenting with as I get closer to my window installation. I will be posting pictures and my results as soon as I can. The wall that seperates the control and live rooms in my new studio is nearly 10" thick. My glass is 3/4" lami and 3/8" tempered. I should be able to angle the glass without any real loss in acoustic isolation. Yes it is more difficult to build, yes there maybe a very small difference in isolation but not enough to worry about. If I can get rid of those damn reflections, it would be worth it to me. As with the Helmholtz calc error, I tend to go with what John says works. Experience wins over theory in my book anyday.

knightfly14 October 2005#13
With that large an air gap, and if your lighting isn't optimum (as in GLARE) you COULD angle the glass a bit and not see a lot of diff in isolation. The downside of angling glasses is that it reduces the air gap, thereby raising the mass-air-mass resonance and weakening the TL of the window - if your total wall thickness is under around 8-10 inches, you start getting into noticeable losses in isolation by tilting the glass. Angling glass does NOT improve isolation, it WORSENS it as I said above. Here's a "myth" paper written by Richard Schragg, who at one time worked for Walters-Storyk Design Group (not sure if that's still the case) - http://www.audio-muziek.nl/audiotechniek/acoustics.pdf HTH...
