Introduction My name is Padraig Donnelly and for the past year and a half I’ve been planning for the opening of a new recording studio in the South Derry area of Northern Ireland sometime in 2009. We’re hoping to start construction at some point within the next six months and we’ve already come up with a few possibilities for the final layout. (Still to come in digital form, but they do exist on paper.) I’ve been lurking here for a while now and I’ve just recently plucked up the courage to take the plunge and tell you about our project, as well as pose some of our more worrying questions to the community. I’m still a little nervous about posting, but nothing ventured, nothing gained. :) I’ve also got some pictures of the existing structure. I’d like to point out that these are indeed hosted off-site and not uploaded to the forums, however I have a long standing business account with my hosting company and have no plans to close the account any time soon. Here we go. I hope you like the space. Budget The absolute maximum: UK£30,000 (For those elsewhere in the world : US$60,000, €41,000, AUS$69,000.) Existing Structure

The structure is farm building, constructed over fifteen years ago. It was constructed without planning permission and at the time, it was beyond the reach of Building Control. According to the Planning Authority we have to put in an application for ‘change of use’, but unless we make any alterations to the exterior of the structure we should not need a ‘full application’. Additional permits will be needed for the installation of a new septic tank. Although the building is plumbed for draining, no facilities currently exist. As a commercial space (and considering the average diet of our average client) we’ll need to make provisions for the extra waste.
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.redmonsterstudios.co.uk/jls/interior1.gif
On the ground floor we have 190m2 of space to work with, as well as first floor decking that adds roughly another 50m2 of space. The floor is 18.72m by 10.2m of poured concrete without rebar. According to my dad (who built the shed), the concrete has an average depth of 6” and is poured on between 6” and 3 or 4ft of hardcore. (Update: Just after getting off the phone with my dad. He says that there might be a damp course after all... sadly he can't remember.) According to the engineers who laid it, it’s rated at 36N, but I have no idea what this means. :P
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.redmonsterstudios.co.uk/jls/interior2.jpg
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.redmonsterstudios.co.uk/jls/clearance.gif
As you can see from the pictures, the walls are concrete blocks (9” or 255mm thick) with no cavity insulation. They reach a height of 2.46m, with 600mm clearance between the tops of the walls and the joists for the first floor decking. I-bars are set into the wall at an equal spacing and serve to suppport the corregated iron and plastic roof construction. As far as I’m aware, it’s 1.8m from the bottom of the decking to where the roof starts to taper off into that dome. Height from bottom of decking to apex of the roof is (I think) 3.9m. The trusses have a span of around 10.5m.
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.redmonsterstudios.co.uk/jls/ibartop.gif
The decking is constructed from timber joists built into I-bars spanning between 4-5m from the central support. The edge of the decking comes to almost the exact center of the building. The central support is also aligned to the center of the decking. (Looking at the photograph, it’s approximately 5m to each wall and 5m to the start of the stairs from that central I-bar.) The decking is topped with a sheet of what looks like MDF or something similar. The interior roof above the decking has been sprayed with a waterproofing course to prevent drips from leaks and condensation.
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.redmonsterstudios.co.uk/jls/morespace.jpg
As already stated, the building is plumbed for water at one point near the door, although no facilities currently exist. It is powered from the domestic supply at the house, approximately 50m or more away, feeding into a circuit board near the door. Currently this serves a number of power sockets and the overhead strip lighting. Goals A large, spacious live room and control room, as well as two or three isolation booths. TL to the outside is not a problem as our nearest neighbours are half a mile away on the opposite side of the building from where we plan to bulid the live room. Our main goal is to achieve the best possible isolation between the control room and the live room and booths. We also want to install shower and toilet facilities for customers and perhaps have some kind of ‘green room’ or recreation area where they can just hang out. Restrictions The I-bars that support the first floor deck cannot be moved. They are load bearing and rated way above domestic requirements. Also, the location of the toilet facilities is limited to the point where the drainage pipe is laid. At the moment, we haven’t quite found it yet, but we know that it’s in the general area of the door. :P (Wish I could be more specific.) Questions 1. After looking at a number of the awesome build diaries on the site and after talking to an architect, it looks like we’re going to have to put a damp course and insulation down on the floor, followed by another pour of concrete, screed, etc. Is that correct? 2. Considering that the building has been standing for over fifteen years with absolutely no sign of any damp rising through the concrete, could we get away with putting the floor down directly on top of that without the damp course? 3. Is this something that has to be done to comply with Building Control, or is it just a preventative measure (in other words: ‘just in case’)? Misc

Howardk27 January 2008#12
I have been playing with design ideas for myself. . . I will not be building for a couple years. One model I am considering is a steel building, similar to yours and building another structure within it for sound isolation/proofing. I was thinking the drum/live room would have the an area with a tall open ceiling, like the design gullfo suggested. In your case I would consider locating a second control room, vocal both etc. on the top level with glass looking down on the live room and create a smaller control room and ISO booth on the main floor. I suggest this for a couple of reasons. The second floor for your larger control room should provide more ceiling height for which will be much better for a mixing room, (although I can not tell how much ceiling you have to work with), which would be better for 5.1 if that is an issue, and more space when there are a lot of people as part of the session will make it a better hangout. It might be better to have the offices, and smaller control/edit room, on the main floor so that they are closer to people dropping by/in/out and would not disturb a recording/mix session. The main floor smaller post/edit/overdub room could also be set it up with a view into the large drum room, and wired for recording track drums or guitar amps, when the large room was busy with a big mix session, etc. . . Of course really could acoustic isolation is key if you plan to do any room sharing. . . just some thoughts. . . . and just rough suggestions because the ceiling height is unknown. . Another suggestion, before you do too much detailed design, make a simple bubble diagram using flowcharting software such as Visio. Define each space you require as a named block, connect them with lines as they are to be utilized and arrange the spaces accordingly. This really helps plan out a good layout, and will save a whole bunch of time juggling things around, as compared to doing room planning in detailed drawing in a CAD system such as Sketchup, because it helps you works out the logistics and room/area interdependencies more efficiently. Don't forget to create placeholders for multiple storage areas, machine room, entrance way, offices, stairs, lounge, etc. . .

Howardk28 January 2008#15
When you mentioned 2 engineers in an earlier post, I assumed you would want to be able to do work in 2 different control/editing rooms. Be careful of dedicating the facility to one band. In experience schedules often end up overlapping outside of your control and there is a lot of benefit to be able to do 2 things at once, to meet deadlines and keep clients happy. . . so if you have the engineers/people a second control/editing room is a very good thing. Foot fall noise is a concern. I have dealt with it successfully in the past. In my case I put a layer of drywall on the existing floor, another layer of wood and finished with carpet. On the bottom side I filled the joist cavity with fiberglass insulation. From there I had an area with metal hat channel grid on springs in each individual room with insulation within the grid and 2 layers of 5/8" drywall attached to the grid. . . and another area where ceiling joists rested on the individual room walls, not mechanically connected to the second floor at all. Of course details will vary for more or less isolation. If you don't allow people to walk upstairs and have enough space on the main floor, you should be able to get away with less but still need isolation to keep adjacent room noise from coming in from the ceiling. I am less sensitive to the line of sight because I find, even in the best organized rooms, it can get tough for people to see each other due to lighting/glare/position/gear in the way, etc. I really like windows for how it makes a room feel and the distribution of light, but I plan to go with LCDs and cameras next time round, but it is an extra expense. . . if you can make it happen so everyone can see each other, great!
