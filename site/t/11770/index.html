Waste of money, in your case! You don't need it, since you already have a concrete slab on grade, and you don't have enough ceiling height to float your floor anyway. Thats a question that has come up. The studs that make out the ceiling should be secured by hanging from the existing ceiling right? Should they be totally separated from the walls? And if so, how does one secure the space in between? Caulk? Insulation?
Nope! The joists for the inner-leaf ceiling rest ONLY on the inner-leaf walls. They are not attached to the outer leaf in any manner at all. The existing ceiling (concrete, in your case?) is your OUTER leaf. You need to build an entire separate 5-sided structure inside of that (4 walls plus the ceiling). OK, so there might be more than five if you angle your walls, but you will have at least five sides. No part of the outer leaf should ever touch any part of the inner leaf. They are two separate, free-standing structures. The only place where the two structures meet, is the floor (unless you have a correctly floated floor, in which case the outer leaf walls are resting on the outer leaf floor, and the inner leaf walls rest on the inner-leaf floor. The concept is often called "room within a room", because that describes exactly what you are doing. A room has a floor, walls, and a ceiling. You build your inner room on the same floor, but you do build it as a complete, sealed "box". All around that "box" is air and insulation, on all sides and above. Nothing else. Having said that, yes, there are cases where you have no choice but to hang a ceiling from an exiting structure above (a low roof basement, for example), but you do so realizing that it is not quite as good as a complete "room-in-a-room", and you do it in such a way as to minimize flanking and coupling. If you have no choice, then you do what has to be done with what you have. But if you have the space and the budget, then go for the best you possibly can, which is totally separate "room-in-a-room", with fully decoupled MSM walls all around. IMHO

I thought that ceiling studs can't be long since they will start to bend. Is this not correct?
If they sag, then you did not dimension them correctly! The longer distance they have to span, the bigger they need to be. Instead of 2x4 you'll have to go to 2x6, 2x8, 2x10 or even more. You should splay the walls of your CR as well: 6 degrees if you are planning to use 60 degree intersection angle for speaker placement, or more if you go with a higher angle. Try rotating your CR 90 degrees to the left, then you could have sliding glass doors directly into the LR, and a door (or window) between your CR and iso booth. Soundman20208 March 2009#17
Say if I were to build the walls on top of the not perfect slab, could I fill the eventual gaps (slab/stud) with caulk?
That depends! How uneven is it? And are you willing to bet the integrity or your studio on not having a good seal? You have to caulk anyway, and that might or might not be enough. If it is just slightly uneven, then probably caulk will do the trick just fine. But if you have large ridges or deep cracks, then I'd go the extra distance and level it first. It looks like you are going through the exact same questioning that I went through several months ago, in deciding how to treat my floor. People kept telling me that the best answer was to level it and seal it. I kept on thinking "Naaah, there has to be another way. Maybe I can do ______ (fill in the blank) instead, and save money!". Until I eventually realized that they were right, after I researched and investigated all over the place. Make it flat and level to start with, and prevent problems down the line. Don't forget that adding a layer of leveling cement also adds mass to the floor, and that can only be good, even if it only helps a tiny fraction. All those "tiny fractions" add up. As Rod says (I'm paraphrasing) we really do need to pay attention to all the details, in order to assure the best possible studio. If not, then even a minor screw-up can have major consequences. - Stuart -

xSpace9 March 2009#19
The biggest concern, for me, with an uneven foundation is the potential for trip ups when walking. If that isn't the issue, it can be handled easily enough. Take a straight edge piece of lumber and lay it on the floor, straight edge side facing the floor. What width of cracks are you seeing? Is it a gradual dip, an obnoxious up and down, a little here a little there? If the area is relatively smooth with gentle highs and lows, then caulking with high grade silicone underneath the bottom plates of the walls before raising, should correct the issue. Of course, it is always advisable to caulk the sole plate again, at the areas that met the slab, once walls have been erected. Than caulk again after ever layer of wall panel. Placing cork or some type of flexible rubber underneath the bottom plate of the wall can also fill the voids if they seem to be creating "holidays" or spots that you can see under. It has to be low enough density to smash under the weight of the wall and fill the voids that exist.
