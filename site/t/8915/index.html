Eric_Desart6 June 2007#2
Paul can't you refer to the link you found this? It's a bit a quiz like this. Normally transmission loss is expressed as a positive dB or dimensionless number (but still referring to the dB scale number). If they express it as a negative number as done here (not standard) they MAYBE express it as the decay of the level at the receiving side (immission level) where no insulation then equals 0 dB (or 100% of the emission level). Mathematically 0 dB represents the factor 1 or 100 %. Negative dB numbers still represents an arithmetical positive percentage > 0% and < 100%. STC, RW and comparable standards etc. mostly refer to the 500 Hz band (after checking several defining spectrum related parameters) not 1000 Hz. Hence POSSIBLY these values are overated versus STC and Rw (1000 Hz will mostly isolate more than 500 Hz) 51 dB @ 1000 Hz isn't bad but isn't impressive either, and 38 dB is relative poor, but these comments of course depends on what we're referring to. And NO you can not translate a number of SINGLE band or frequency to an overal insulation. But I can continue making lots of assumptions, or you refer to the source where you found this, so people can see your question in context.

lovecow6 June 2007#5
paul, That's truly a pi$$-poor presentation of information. E.g., "greater than -39 dB" ... 0 dB is greater than -39 dB. :shock: Putting "min" in front of this ludicrous statement creates a lovely oxymoron. There's just no way of knowing from the information on that site what the performance would be. Besides, they're providing these specs for booths that the user builds from plans they provide (sell). To provide any sort of performance estimate is placing a lot of faith that the user will be able to build the booth exactly to their design. FWIW, there's not a lot of rocket science to building a booth. Use at least several layers of gypsum wallboard ("drywall" - 3-4 total layers usually does the trick), insulation between the studs, make sure the construction is airtight, minimize windows and penetrations, seal doors, etc. The Auralex reference is a good one, as is Rod Gervais' book.
