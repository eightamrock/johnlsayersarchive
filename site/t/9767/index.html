Ethan Winer20 November 2007#19
Eric_Desart wrote:
That calculator emphasizes the unimportance of non-axials
Not to open a can of gummy worms here, but maybe I can clarify a few points. Glenn Kuras just emailed me for my opinion:
I need to wrap my head around this: L. W. Sepmeyer: 1965 - 1:1.14:1.39. This is one of world's most common used ratio. When one judges those room ratios based on the Axial modes alone one can only conclude that the modal distribution of this ratio looks terrible. However L.W. Sepmeyer counted on the non-Axial modes in order to optimize the modal energetic distribution. The same is valid for his other ratios. M. M. Louden: 1971: Best ratio as per Louden - 1:1.4:1.9 Another of world's most common used ratios. When studying lots of Louden's room ratios, even the best ranked (he published 125 ratios in decaying order), one will notice that without the non-Axial modes, only judged on the Axial modes, lots of those common used and worldwide accepted ratios should be rejected resulting from an ugly looking distribution. Care to comment on this?
I'm sure that non-axials account for something. Jeff Szymanski once explained that in theory tangentials are 3 dB lower than axials (half as strong), and obliques are 6 dB lower (one quarter as strong). But non-axials do not happen at the very low frequencies that axials do. The first tangential mode starts almost an octave higher than the first axial mode, and oblique modes start even higher still. So by the time you get high enough in frequency for most of the non-axials to kick in, you're well into the range where bass traps can easily tame them. The main reason I don't show non-axials in my ModeCalc program is because it's simply Too Much Information for most people. If you include all the modes you'll end up with 100 frequencies, which is impossible to deal with. I'm sure you've seen those lists showing modes every 1 to 3 Hz. WTF is anyone, let alone mere mortals, supposed to do with all that? Worse, the importance of the modes is not made clear in most of those calculators, so the forest is hidden by the trees. If you look at only the axial modes you can see much better where the strongest resonances are. My ModeCalc goes further by highlighting modes that are close together with taller lines. IMO that is more useful than showing all non-axial modes which are 3 or 6 dB less important. I could even argue that in many case modes can mostly be ignored - though obviously you don't want square or cube or even multiples like 10 by 20 etc. Most of the people I deal with are not building $250K rooms in a 35 by 45 foot shell. So the room size and shape must be ergonomic and practical as much as acoustically valid. If the choice is between having a "perfect" ratio or having a room that can accommodate your keyboards and guitar amps and guest seating, I'll take the latter every time. And then treat the hell out of it with bass traps. Which is needed anyway even with an "ideal" mode distribution. Finally, as we now all know after my RO post Room modes, calculated versus measured, the modes you predict are not necessarily the modes you'll end up with. So you carefully plan all those modes that are precisely 2 Hz apart, then build the room, and all that precision is now out the window. At least at the lowest mode frequencies which matter the most because they're hardest to trap. --Ethan

myfipie wrote:
I will say I found Rods comment VERY interesting and explained a great deal of questions I had on the subject.
Yes Glenn, as Rod wrote, "the ONLY accuracy exists with the actual testing performed after construction" which of course is the whole reason I started that 14-page thread over at RO. However, I do disagree with this:
Rod wrote, I don't think (when we deal with sound) that we can look at it as percentages
I disagree with Rod (and Jeff) about percentages not being the right way to assess modes, for the reason I explained in that thread. Musical notes and octaves and frequencies are always expressed as percentages. (Logarithms, really, which is the same concept.) That an FFT algorithm happens to use a fixed Hz bandwidth is a side effect of the algorithm, not a design goal. FFT algorithms have nothing to do with the subject at hand anyway. A musical Bb note is always some percentage higher than the A note below, regardless of which octave you consider. And modes and their bandwidths (Q) etc are also always expressed as a percentage. Or they should be. I realize that Everest and some others talk about mode bandwidths in terms of Hz rather than Q which is a percentage. But Q is indeed the correct way to view audio bandwidths, whether for an EQ filter or in acoustics. --Ethan

OK, so maybe I have a little time... I couldn't let this go:
Ethan Winer wrote:
I'm sure that non-axials account for something. Jeff Szymanski once explained that in theory tangentials are 3 dB lower than axials (half as strong), and obliques are 6 dB lower (one quarter as strong).
I never explained any such thing. As I have explained before, the 3/6 dB difference is only relevant to modes of different types that occur at the same frequency. And it is theoretical. And it would be additive, whereby an axial and tangential mode at the same frequency could still combine to have an overall amplitude higher than each mode taken on its own. The actual amplitude of a mode is dependent on the characteristics of the room and the relative positions of the source and receiver in the room. Further, I have seen at least one software developer include "weighting" schemes for different mode types, adjustable by the user. Some authors (Bonello comes to mind) have offered up reasoning for this in their writings. But their work is often dated. From a 2007 perspective, I cannot say that I agree with this line of thinking since the modal response of a room can be predicted much easier these days, thereby removing any need to "weight" anything. In my experience, this calculated/predicted response often agrees well with the measured response. All the best,
