Actually, and not to add to your work load, but I would love to see a hypothetical set of room dimensions that someone might choose that looks good in my calculator but is a "serious error" in actual practice.
(Man, I just can't seem to let go of this today...) I don't think the reason I'm arguing is because something could "look good" on your calculator, but still result in serious error when using another calculator. That could happen, but I have cited works that support your argument of addressing room design from purely an axial mode perspective. I still don't think it's correct, but I can grant some leeway there nonetheless. I think my main point is that mode calculators are a tool used by many to identify the nature of problems that are already being perceived. Gross errors could result from using your calculator to design a fix to an existing problem. To that end, here is a specific example of how things could go wrong:
    Bubba's room is 14'x12'x9'. Bubba is mostly pleased with how it sounds, even without any major acoustical treatments. The only problem is that whenever the bass player hits that B note, it completely vanishes. Bubba gets on the web and finds his way to Ethan Winer's website. For the most part, people seem to trust this guy's opinions when it comes to treating small room problems, so Bubba's going to use Ethan's room mode calculator and see if he can't figure this out. Lo and behold, Ethan's program tells Bubba that, sure enough, there's an axial mode {0,0,1} right at 63 Hz, which is darn near that bass player's B note. Bubba presents his problem on {insert favorite Internet forum} as, "How do I treat a 63 Hz axial mode?" and finds out he can design panel "traps" that specifically address this floor-ceiling mode. And, further, if he puts them smack in the middle of the ceiling - where he has plenty of room - it should make a significant difference. Now, Bubba is a skilled carpenter and designs his panel traps to within 1/16" tolerances using all the right formulas. Bubba rigidly fixes his (very pretty) "traps" to the ceiling and fires up his mix. Well, now Bubba's plumb confused. He has done everything right and he can hear a slight difference, but, for the most part, the B note is still doing its vanishing act right at the start of the chorus. Bubba goes back to {forum} and presents his grief. Those who helped guide Bubba to panel "traps" obtain more information and only now learn Bubba's room dimensions. Plugging them into a non-Ethan Winer calculator, they discover the {1,1,0} tangential mode at 62 Hz. Bubba's now getting plain frustrated. Sure, corner traps could work just fine and he could build them. The trouble is, he blew his budget (and time) on the ceiling "traps." And he only has 1/2 of one corner in the room available that is not obstructed by doors, furniture, or gear. And he doesn't even trust that putting corner "traps" in could help since he barely noticed any difference when he put the (pretty, expensive, labor-intensive) "traps" on the ceiling. Before you argue that Bubba was hosed from the start because he couldn't have put anything in the corners anyway... And before you argue that Bubba will eventually realize some benefit from the ceiling "traps" if he can ever get to the corners... Consider that Bubba now thinks Ethan Winer is a soulless ne'er-do-well for plugging a mode calculator that didn't tell him everything he needed to know. Consider that Bubba put 3 days of work (when he could have been mixing) into "traps" that he now feels were far from worth the effort. Consider that had Bubba known that he needed to address both problems from the beginning, he may have approached things differently. Consider that had Ethan Winer been able to tell him from the beginning to address both problems, then Ethan may have come out being the hero instead of the villain. And so on...
Lots of things to consider, I guess. :roll:

Thanks for the link - that was a great post you made there! My ModeCalc program does not highlight too-wide modes, but it does highlight too-near modes. The ModeCalc web page states:
When viewing the results look for an even spacing of the modes regardless of their color (good), and also look for multiple modes that occur at or near the same frequencies (bad).
