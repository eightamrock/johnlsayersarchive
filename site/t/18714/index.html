For the ground floor tracking room, you need a wall that goes across on the left side of the page, between the main tracking area and the kitchen area, to complete the outer leaf.
Are there any acoustic issues with having the front and side walls brick but the rear wall vinyl siding?
For isolation, yes, there's a big difference. Brick is way more massive and dense than siding, so you will need to "beef up" the outer leaf on that side, adding considerable mass. If you search the forum for "beef up", you should find many references on how to do this correctly.
I can't find much info on a two-story build like this,
That's mostly because isolating a studio on an upper floor is really hard to do. Studios are normally built on a concrete slab that rests directly on the ground, since that's the easiest and cheapest way to do it. They can be built on upper floors, but it makes it much harder to achieve high levels of isolation. You mentioned that you need to isolate drums, and that is do-able for the tracking room on the ground floor, but won't be nearly so easy for the other tracking room, on the second floor.
but what about insulating and drywalling the 2nd floor further against the trusses on the sides rather than at the room studs,
Well, you do need to do that anyway! For a proper MSM wall, you need considerable malls on BOTH leaves (not just one of them), and you do need as much insulation as possible in the cavity between them. That's a must.
and using a slat/fabric construction at the actual wall studs in the "garage plans" image so that I can fill the cavities with absorption?
I'm not sure I understand that. Slot walls are not part of the isolation of a room: they are part of the treatment. First you build your studio as a proper fully-decoupled two-leaf MSM system, then when that is finished you add the treatment that is needed to make the room sound the way you want it to. Isolation and treatment are two totally different aspects of acoustics. So for BOTH floors, you need to build a proper 2-leaf system, then within that you add the treatment, which might include slots walls, or fabric-covered insulation, or other types of treatment. But a slot wall is never the actual inner-leaf of a room: it is just treatment. - Stuart -

Isolation is really an "all or nothing" proposition, meaning that you can't have good isolation on 3 sides of a room, but no isolation on the 4th side. Without that wall to close off the rear space, everything back there would be inside your air-gap, between the inner and outer leaves, which is not a great idea.
I guess I'll also compare the cost of this versus the added cost of doing the back wall with brick facade.
Right! Sound waves don't care how much you pay for the mass that you stop them with: The can't read the price tags! :) They just react to the total mass of the wall, so get the least expensive building materials that provide the amount of mass you need. In many places around the world, that is ordinary fire rated drywall, but in some places wood is cheaper, while in other places brick or even concrete is cheaper. So figure out how much you are paying, in terms of cost per kilogram, for each type of material, and get the one with the lowest cost (also taking into account the labor and other related costs).
I'm not too concerned about isolation for the control room as I do not monitor too loudly...but isolation between the first and second floors is a concern.
OK, so most of your effort (and mass, and money) will go into the ground floor which needs the most isolation.

Not really, since in your original post you seemed to be confusing your slot wall with your inner-leaf wall, and that applies to both the live room and the control room. A slot wall is treatment, not isolation. If you do need a slot wall, then you will build it inside the finished room (either LR or CR), after the inner-leaf is already in place. That's the point I was trying to make, since it seemed to me that you were thinking that your slot wall would also be your inner-leaf. - Stuart -
