sharward1 June 2006#4
knightfly wrote:
. . . in fact, EPDM tends to have quite a bit longer useful life than neoprene, in some cases about double.
Steve, Is this claim documented anywhere though, other than the U-boat manufacturer saying that is what someone said to them once? :roll: We already know that they didn't put U-boats through any rigorous (or any?) testing... :roll: James, it does appear that you know the perils of an incorrectly engineered floating floor -- overloading especially. However, there is also the risk of underloading. There is also the risk of too small a gap between floated floor and its subfloor -- and floating a floor using 2x4s laid flat gives you just over 1 1/2" of an air gap between floors, which is woefully insufficient for any M-A-M benefit. There's also the issue of what kind of floor you intend to float. It has to be massive in order to be effective... And I'm betting that with your minimal vertical clearance, you can't afford the space to have a thick enoujgh floor to make it worthwhile. Floating a floor may not be an option for you. Besides, you haven't said what type of surface you intend to put this on... Basement slab? Slab on grade? Raised wooden subfloor? Upstairs over a garage? There are significant ramifications to each of these scenarios, some of which make it practically impossible to float a floor safely. I realize your question was targeted and specific, but the question needs to be put into context. That's why following all of the critical bold items points documented in the "Before You Post" announcement are so important. :) --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward2 June 2006#7
There is a product called Sill Seal that does a good job of accounting for the irregularities of a slab and the soleplate. I plan to use them when I do my framing. However, you cannot legally and safely "float"wall frames. They must be firmly attached to the slab. Even if you could, Sill Seal wouldn't be the product for that. The firm connections into the slab that will hold your wall frames in place will negate any attempts to decouple the frames from the slab. --Keith :mrgreen:
knightfly2 June 2006#8
Even pressure treated wood should have what's called a "thermal break" between it and concrete - I've used 2 layers of 30# roofing felt in several places and it works well, or the sill seal product which is intended just for this. The actual seal in a sound wall does NOT depend on a tight seal under the frame though; there are a couple of threads in the REFERENCE section on drywall, caulking, backer rod, etc, which explain this... Steve
