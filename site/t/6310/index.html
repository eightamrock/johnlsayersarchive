knightfly26 May 2006#4
The three things that make this the most difficult are your budget, the glass, and the bedroom BELOW your room - unless your floor is built EXTRA sturdy, there is no reasonable way to noticeably improve isolation between floors. The only practical way to achieve isolation is with mass-air-mass construction - the more of each, the better. For full-range sound isolation, you need a double wall construction (no extra walls, this is part of the "mass-air-mass" thing) - realistically, this type of barrier will weigh at LEAST 10 pounds per square foot whether it's a floor or a wall or a ceiling - and for SERIOUS isolation in a floor, the existing floor needs to be able to support an additional 75 pounds per square foot (the weight of a 4" concrete floated slab, plus inner walls floated on that slab) - but this is already 'WAY beyond your budget. The type of construction I've just described is the ONLY thing that will stop high levels of low frequency sound; so unless the inhabitants of the bedroom downstairs sleep days and are away at night, you're in for some not-so-wonderful news - sorry... Steve

knightfly27 May 2006#12
If your handyman friend says he can guarantee you 80 dB isolation, you don't need US; but you DO need to keep your bull shit filter turned on; that figure is either an outright lie, or measured only at ONE FREQUENCY, or else your building is already made with foot-thick concrete EVERYWHERE and your handyman is VERY good at sound isolation... The more you read through the REFERENCE section here, or similar materials at other such sites (real ones) the more you'll find out that sound isolation gets more difficult the lower you go in frequency; by the time you get down to below 100 hZ, you're doing REALLY well to get even 40 dB of TL - which isn't all that bad, because at 50 hZ human hearing can't detect anything below about 55 dB, so adding 40 dB of isolation brings the actual SPL up to around 95 dB @ 50 hZ before anyone on the other side even knows there is anyone there... Try a google search on sylomer - this material is better for floating floors than nearly anything else in existence. Also, (be prepared for a long read) check out Paul Woodlock's studio build here http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.php?t=107 Outa time for now, HTH... Steve

len-morgan21 June 2006#19
Yes, it's possible to make a wooden isolating floor but what I'm saying is that (I believe) Sylomar is made to carry more weight (i.e., cement). It is a common misconception that some kind of rubber like substance under some kind of flat floor surface makes an isolated floating floor. There is a lot more engineering involved than that. If you read Paul Woodlock's studio thread on studiotips.com (from the beginning), you will see all the work that went into just figuring out which product to put under his cement floor. The basic idea is that any rubber like material is useless unless you can get somewhere between 20% and 50% compression. If you put too much weight on it, it will "bottom out" and couple any vibration directly to the floor below. If you don't put ENOUGH weight on it, the same thing happens - you end up with an expensive but pretty much useless floor system. In fact, if you do it wrong in the right way (how's that for an oxymoron!), you will make the situation WORSE than if you hadn't done anything at all. Plan, plan, plan! len
