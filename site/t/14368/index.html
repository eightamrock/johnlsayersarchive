Small, yes, but still usable. If that's what you have, then it is what you have! But I don't think you have space for both a control room AND a vocal booth. I'd just leave it as one room. With those dimensions, the ratio actually isn't too bad. It's fairly close to Sepmeyer's second best ratio, and there aren't any major red flags for big modal issues, except of course the usual caveats for small rooms.
House is made entirely of concrete and rebar (and despite what many say, concrete is NOT as dense as you think; at least not in Taiwan. I can hear neighbors scooting chairs, arguments, TV's, and especially dense objects dropped [like marbles, coins etc.] quite clearly at times)
OK, so isolation is an issue. It sounds like some of that is impact noise, and some is probably due to poor sealing / flanking issues. But your best bet is MSM construction, room-in-a-room walls and ceiling. Your on the fourth floor, too! Ouch...
I've read that creating a 'box' of a recording room isn't the best option. Should I angle the walls? So that no two walls are facing each other? Is that possible in my case?
Depends what you mean by "box". For your control room, a box actually isn't bad, as long as it has a good ratio and is well treated. You can splay the front walls if you want, for two reasons: RFZ and soffits. But you don't want to splay all your walls at random angles in a control room! Symmetry is critical in a control room, for good stereo imaging. Your room must be symmetrical.
3. Where should the 'control room' be located? (basically everything outside of the recording room is the control room....but where would you put the mixing desk
Once gain, I don't see enough room in there for two rooms. It is small already, and you haven't even built your inner leaf, nor your treatment! One room is all you have space for. And your options on layout are really limited: The only workable layout that I see is to have the front of the room facing the wall that says "protruding wall", and the rear at the "false wall". I'd start from that perspective, and see what you can fit in in terms of your MSM walls, soffits, mix position, etc. Do a rough layout in SletchUp, post it here, and folks will reply with suggestions on how to improve it.
2. My next quest is figuring out who to properly ventilate and cool the recording room.
Yup. HVAC is a huge part of the design, especially for somewhere like Taiwan, which you said is both hot and humid. Your room will basically be an extremely thick airtight blanket, and you'll need to move, cool and dehumidify a lot of air if you want three people plus equipment in there... This is NOT something you can do "jerry-rigged in some fashion to push cold air into the intake of ventilation system". Nope. You MUST design it properly, or hire an HVAC contractor to do it for you. "I think it might work," is not the kind of phrase you want to hear for the design of a recording studio.
3. After that I've gotta consider treating the room. The designers wouldn't touch this point. They claim it's the engineer's prerogative. My guess...they just don't really know.
Oops! Looks like you need to switch designers!
I can't seem to find a solid source on what exactly to use to make it extremely dead-sounding,
Bad idea! A dead sounding room is terrible. You do NOT want "lifeless". You want controlled neutrality. You want the room to sound as natural, open and uncolored as possible, but certainly not "dead".
All sources I've searched out have widely different methods;
Then it' looks like you've been searching in the wrong places! I'd really, really suggest that you buy two books, and spend plenty of time reading them over a few times each, studying them carefully so you can get a handle on this while acoustics thing. The first is "Master Handbook of Acoustics" by F. Alton Everest. It is the "Bible" of acoustics, and teaches you all of the basics, plus way more. The second book is commonly referred to as "Rod's book" here: "Home Recording Studio: Build it Like the Pros", by Rod Gervais. It tells you how to design and build your room, taking into account pretty much everything. There is a great section on HVAC that you absolutely must read.
