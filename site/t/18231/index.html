Soundman202014 March 2013#5
The homasote (if I understand it correctly) is supposed to decouple the drywall from the wall. Is that true?
Not really. It might damp resonance to a certain extent, but decoupling requires some type of resilient disconnection between the leaf (surface) of one side of the wall, and the leaf (surface) on the other side. That is usually done by using air as the resilient break (air is very "springy") by putting the leaves on separate stud frames with only air between them, or by using "resilient channel", which is a type of metallic support specifically designed to proved the necessary spring between the leaf and the studs that it is mounted on, or by using RSIC clips and common hat channel, where the hat channel does nothing except hold the leaf in place, while the RSIC clip does the decoupling. Homasote won't do that, since it isn't resilient to start with, and in any case you would still be nailing the drywall right through it, into the studs below, so the nails would be "short circuiting" any small resilience that it did have. Nails and screws are a major flanking path, and totally bypass decoupling. So in order to create a proper isolation wall (commonly referred to as a "fully decoupled two-leaf isolation system"), there has to be some type of mechanical "break" that separates the leaves physically, such that sound (vibrations) cannot propagate directly between them.
Ahhh I see. I was thinking I was doing something wrong designing a rectangular drywall covered room but that is the first step.
No, that's fine! You are on the right track. This type of isolation is often called "room-within-a-room", since that is what you are doing, in effect. You have an existing room, which sometimes needs to be stripped down until it is just one leaf, then you somehow build another "room" inside that one, by mounting the walls and ceiling (and sometimes the floor too) resiliently.
What would you suggest to keep the noise from other rooms from traveling through the ducts into the room?
Well, you do need silencer boxes, but you also need resilient breaks where the ducts cross over the gap between the two leaves of a wall, and you also need to dimension the system correctly, in terms of room occupancy, heat load, air-changes-per-hour of room volume, cubic-feet-per-minute of air flow, feet-per-minute of air velocity, cooling capacity, heating capacity, etc. There's a lot that goes into calculating the HVAC system. Basically, the ducts need to be sized correctly so that they can carry the necessary amount of air to replace the entire volume of air inside the room, the required number of times per hour, and do it slowly enough that the system itself does not add noise to the room, while also providing the necessary baffling and impedance mismatching to prevent sound moving along the ducts, and at the same time not raising the static pressure higher than the fans and air handler can deal with, without being overloaded. In turn, the air handler has to be dimensioned to provide the correct amount of cooling and de-humidification for the room, at the static pressure load of the duct system, across the entire range of room occupancy and heat loads, while never exceeding the recommended duty cycle, and being able to deal with all expected climate conditions. HVAC is not something you can just sort of throw together by cutting a few vents in here and there, and hoping it will work out. This is probably the single most overlooked area of home studio design, and the one that can make the most difference to a room being totally unusable, barely usable but very uncomfortable, or pleasant. You might want to check out the ASHRAE web site: they have a lot of useful information and links there, about HVAC in general. Rod's book also has a good chapter on HVAC and how to do all of this calculation for a typical home studio, such as yours. - Stuart -

Will this mess with the accuracy for mixing?
Accuracy starts out with symmetry, then geometry, then treatment. In other words, you first plan the room so that it is absolutely symmetrical to the left and right of the mix position, which is on the center line of the room. Then you lay out the speakers and chair following the normal rules, so that the geometry is correct. Then you add the basic treatment: absorption on first reflection points, bass traps in corners, ceiling cloud over the mix position, absorption on the rear wall. Then you add whatever other treatment is needed to balance the acoustics correctly, so they meet the normal specs. That's the usual procedure. - Stuart -

Soundman20209 May 2013#12
I'm not sure what I want to do. Any suggestions? Will one large room sound better?
In general, yes: a larger room will sound better than a smaller room, especially for recording some instruments (such as drums, for example). However, having said that, there are also big advantages to having two rooms, especially if you are going to be recording drums or other loud instruments. For example, to be able to place mics well on the drum kit, you need to hear how each mic sounds, by itself, on the studio monitors: Its impossible to do that if the drum kit is in the same room as the studio monitors! Also, if you want to record instruments and vocals at the same time, you can't do that with just one room, as the bleed from the instruments into the vocal mic(s) is just too great. With two rooms, you can put the vocalist(s) in the control room and the noisy stuff in the live room. Etc. In your case, the two rooms are not THAT small, and I think it is reasonable to divide up the space like that. On the other-other-other hand, if this is a studio for just you alone, and you'll never have more than one person in it (you), then one room probably makes more sense than two. So this really is more of a personal preference issue, and a workflow issue, rather than a purely acoustical issue. Overall, if that were my studio, for the way I work, I would definitely go with two rooms.
