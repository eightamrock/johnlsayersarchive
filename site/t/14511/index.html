Soundman20204 July 2010#4
Although I respect your experience in this field I do not see how a total 4 inches of isolation Rockwool (30 kg cubic meter/density) would have no impact on the isolation or the sound in the room.
Because contrary to popular belief (and contrary to myth-based "acoustic" web sites) insulation does not stop sound. Insulation and isolation are two entirely different things. Isolation (preventing sound from getting from "A" to "B") can only be accomplished in two ways: 1) Remove the medium through which sound travels between them, (ie create a total total vacuum between the points, by removing all the air and everything else between "A" and "B"), and 2) Place enough air-tight mass in the path between "A" and "B" that the sound energy is totally dissipated before it can get through. 4 inches of insulation does not accomplish either of these. Not even 4 FEET of insulation will do the job. (Maybe something like 40 feet would help.) Insulation is good for room TREATMENT, but does practically nothing for room ISOLATION. To isolate, you have to create a physical barrier that prevents sound waves from getting through, which implies a total air-tight seal, and lots of mass (since it is impractical to put a vacuum around your room, all you have left is mass), and decoupling. Insulation serves to attenuate sound energy, but does not eliminate it. One way you can think of sound waves is as a constant complex interchange of energy between velocity and pressure. Insulation works on the velocity portion of sound waves, but does nothing at all to the pressure portion. Mass works on both. So your 4 inches of insulation would indeed help to treat the room, changing the internal acoustics, but will do practically nothing to isolate the room. And even then, having so much insulation on such a large wall area might not produce the acoustics you want...
The isolation will be covered with cloth. There are two leafs (I hope I got the terminology right) since there are two separate walls which are separated by air in the front-end corners of the room.
I'm not sure I understand you: Are you considering that the cloth is one "leaf", and the drywall is the other "leaf"? Is that what you are saying?
1) I wonder if drywall is a good choice for the inside part of the wood frame that supports the isolation? If No, what materials are preferred and why? If yes, should I use different thicknesses on the connecting walls that contain the air pockets? (This question relates to the air spring in the front corners of the two leafs).
Maybe you could draw a diagram to show where you plan to install that drywall, and what you mean with the term "air pockets".
I would also be very happy to hear any suggestions on how to best place the bass traps at the back of the room.
Bass traps work best in corners, due to the boost that you get from the surrounding surfaces: 12 dB for the corners where two surfaces meet, 18 dB for corners where three surfaces meet ("tri-corners"). Bass traps need to be large and deep in order to work well.
Any input on how this design can be improved would be appreciated.
I think I'd go for a more traditional, proven design, such as using proper MSM walls for isolation, and the currently popular RFZ concept (Reflection Free Zone) for basic layout. Since you have the space, I reckon I'd also go with soffit-mounted (flush-mounted) speakers. I'd also check that the room is based on one of the known-good ratios, and that the layout (speaker location, mix position, geometry) follow the usual guidelines for such rooms. - Stuart -

Soundman20206 July 2010#11
You will see that the new inside wall goes all the way around. You must isolate all the surfaces including the ceiling..
Exactly. That's part of what I was trying to say. Think of it this way: If you only put half a piece of glass in the front of an aquarium, it will not hold water. Even if you put glass around three sides, on the floor, and on the roof but leave out the glass in just one side, it still will not hold water. You have to put glass all around the entire aquarium, on all sides, for it to hold water. The same happens with sound: Like john said, in order to isolate, you have to treat all six sides of the room, or at least the most important five of them: the four walls and the ceiling (assuming you you happen to have a massive and well damped floor, such as a thick concrete slab on grade). Isolation is an "all or nothing" proposition. Either do the entire room, or don't do it all. Just doing part of it will not help. - Stuart -
