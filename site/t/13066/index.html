Okay, I'll step through the current design in pieces. But in general, I'm going to build a floor with walls that rest on it (not touching the outer walls). It will be sealed up against a 2-leaf ceiling using the above floor as leaf 1, and a double 5/8 drywall attached to it with RC. The 9.5 inches that joists provide will be filled the recommended pink, fluffy insulation. The floor will have a layer of plastic so-called "sleeper" to give it some separation from any moisture that might seep into the basement. This is just sheets of plastic with little "cups" studded all over them that act as feet. Then it will be plywood, carpet pad, and carpet. Not wanting to float or put in any studs, need to keep it as thin as can be to save on space. I'm not finalized on how to handle to the small control room. Right now I'm just hoping I have enough room for those doors. The ceiling in the control room will almost certainly be level to the floor where there are no obstructions, since it will be complicated enough dealing with the hanging ducts and pipes. Note: Duct work is not an option, in terms of moving/adjusting them. Just not within the scope of this project. Budget: Trying to stick close to $2000, with a little wiggle room. Also, trying to accomplish the build in less than a month from the permit-issued date (don't know when I'll get that). No work is going to contracted except for an hour or two of an electrician's time (already quoted), as detailed in the next paragraph. Electrical: Made a plan (pending permits and schedules) with an electrician setup a two-stake grounding field for my star-pattern grounding system for the studio. We're also going to run a sub-panel into the control room (probably into the north east corner), likely a 12-slot 50amp box. All studio-gear outlets will be grounded to the star group bar (in the sub-panel) and run via a single #4 copper wire to the new grounding field. All lights and non-studio-gear outlets will be run back to an additional (new) grounding bar on the main house box, and run outside to the regular house ground. That's the current plan. Internal wiring will be done myself, box adjustment and external grounding fields to be handled by the electrician. Two 4-plug outlets are currently in the west (live room) area, and there is one 4-plug outlet in the east (control room) side. These will be rewired to the new sub panel, with grounding as described above. Also, I'm thinking about getting a snake or two to embed in the walls for microphone plugs. So... my dad called up an electrician to start the official side of things on this studio project. Turns out he knew my dad, having been a student at a high-school during my dad's brief time as a teacher. So before we even mention the word "studio" that day, we're on familiar terms. That was cool. So we go and look at the house, explain what I'm trying to create, and we check on the possibility of upgrading the house main box, and doing the star-grounding, and sub-panel all that stuff. In the conversation about grounding, I mentioned hammering the stakes in. He laughs and say they have a better way. But he tells me that even back before they had the current tools, he used to use water. "I learned from the old-timers", he tells me. And he explains that you pound in the stake a couple inches (with your hand) and fill the gaps with water, and then pound some more, and repeat. He said it works so well than when he was learning he actually got about half way down the 8 foot stake and was pounding away and the rest just disappeared into the earth. Which made the "old-timers" laugh at him and tell him he was pounding too hard. Just thought I would share that. Anyway, the upgrade work was quoted at around $1800 if he does the upgrade, the sub panel, and the grounding work himself, and buys all the equipment through their suppliers. So we worked backward, figuring out how much of the current supply is likely to be used, which was not much. So I say "How about we just run the sub-panel off the current one and don't upgrade the house supply." He says, "That's going to be a lot cheaper. If you buy the supplies, and do all the room wiring yourself, I can setup the sub-panel and do the grounding in less than two hours. Just give me a call some time next week or so, and I'll come by after my day's work is done and I won't charge and extra for after-hours work." So that was awesome. And we start talking. He mentioned his sons are in high school (same high school I graduated from) and they're in bands. He talked about how there's just not much support for all the talent the younger musicians have. So we end our meeting on a note of mutual excitement about support for musicians, and exchange business cards. It was a good day.

great story Seth - just a note about grounding - if you live in a very dry area it's common for your earth stake to dry out and lose it's grounding. It can easily be repaired by pouring a bucket of salty water over the area.

Had my first visit with the city inspectors this morning. I talked to the building and electrical inspectors. The plan for wiring was simple enough, and I got that permit today. The building inspector said that as of 2006, if remodeling is done to a basement, one window must be updated to be at least 5.7 square feet, and no more than 44 inches off the ground of the basement. That is the only major difference between my plan and what's required. So, a little more work, but there's a good part to this. My studio only takes up half the basement. Only one window is required, so I don't even have to put it in the studio space at all! I can build my walls solid where I need them, and make the window in the other half of the basement. Anyway, one permit down, two to go. Have to update my plan with the window and submit that for the building permit. And then I have to get a permit from the electrician who's installing my sub-panel and grounding stakes.

Thanks for that tip on the control room, Stuart! Glad you posted that before I submitted my design to the city. Sometimes, scouring expert forums on a subject you learn tons about finer details and miss the fundamentals. My previous design was more concerned with non-parallel walls, but missed symmetry entirely. For simplicity and time requirements of this project, I will have parallel surfaces in my control area, but those will simply have to be treated acoustically afterward. I've adjusted the plan to account for symmetry in the control room, and the safety window the inspector asked for (not located within the studio area). Ultimately, that will make my control room smaller, but an emergent benefit of the adjustment is a larger sound lock area where both doors can open into it. (I believe I read somewhere on this forum that both sound lock doors should open into the sound lock, whenever possible). So, I just have to deal with a small control room, but I have reliable headphones if the room gives me any trouble. I'll scan and post my updated plan when I get back home today. That being said, I got my building permit this morning, so I'm on good terms with the city now, and the project has a green light. Huzzah! Today's Lesson: I briefly asked about the subject of circulation/ventilation while I was talking to the building inspector. In McLean county, Illinois, if you are not adding/removing/altering the location of HVAC ducts, you can put vents into existing ones without requiring a permit. Not sure if that's going to be what we do, but now I know it's a permissible option. The other quick option is leaving one window in an area that can be opened. That, too, will suffice for ventilation requirements.
