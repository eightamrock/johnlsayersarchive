Soundman202015 November 2012#7
There are several things that can be improved with that plan. I would say the two biggest issues are the pillar, which I'm guessing cannot be moved, and the low ceiling. I would aim to structure the rooms around that pillar, hopefully so that the pillar itself is in the wall cavity between two rooms. since the pillar is, by definition, part of your outer leaf, having it run through a room greatly complicates your isolation. You would have to build a decoupled box around it to correctly isolate the room, with the box being part of the inner leaf. The pillar also creates potential issues for room acoustics, since it seems to be quite large enough to cause reflections and diffraction, so that is a secondary issue. And apart from anything else, it is just awkward to have that huge thing poking up through the middle of the room! The other issue is the ceiling that apparently drops down low on one side. Since symmetry is critical for control rooms, and since the room cannot get smaller at the back for acoustic reasons, the only possible orientation for your control room is to have the front of the room (where the speakers are) located under the lower part of that ceiling. (Assuming that you want to have you control room in that area). But whichever way you look at it, a six foot ceiling is very low, and that's without even considering the height you will lose due to the isolation, so if you can take that out and use the space above it, then I'd really suggest that you do so.

Is that before or after the isolation system is built? If you are talking about "after" (i.e., the final internal dimensions of the room, without considering treatment), then the room ratio works out to 1 : 1.22 : 1.63. It isn't too bad, but could be better. It fails one of the three key criteria used by the BBC for determining is a room can be used as a studio or not.

Not sure! You'd have to do the complete layout of the room, using correct geometry for the speakers and listening position, then see how the console fits into that arrangement. With a large console like that in a small roo, you also run the risk of having major reflections coming off the console surface, so that will have to be taken into account in your layout.
No HVAC at present, but I'm thinking of installing something -
It's not so much a matter of "thinking about it"! :) You have no choice! In order to isolate the rooms acoustically, each room must have two totally airtight (hermetic) seals around it, with zero passage for air. People have this strange habit: they like to breathe several times per minute, so in order to keep that habit going, you need to feed fresh air into the rooms, and exhaust the stale air out, and that has to be done through the isolation using silencer boxes ("baffle boxes") in order to not lose the isolation. People also have another habit: they put out moisture and body heat. Since your rooms will be wrapped in very thick layers of acoustic absorption, you basically have the rooms wrapped in very effective thermal blankets that keep the heat in. Your equipment also puts out a light of heat, as does the lighting system and other electrical things inside. So a correctly designed HVAC system is not an option to be thought about: it is a critical, essential, necessity. Without it, you won't last long in there. The air will get stale, hot, muggy and unpleasant very quickly, and dangerous shortly after that. Some people think "Well, I'll just open the door every hour or so, and that will fix things." Nope. Won't happen. Air will not enter or exit the room with the door open unless you give it a reason to do so: Since the door is the ONLY path into the room (the windows are sealed and inoperable), then you would need several fans to force air in, out and around the room, plus you would still have wild swings in humidity and temperature. Not a practical or realistic approach. You NEED a proper HVAC system.
Control room will have a desk, I'll probably make this a corner desk
Symmetry is critical for control rooms. Your head MUST be on the central axis of the room, equidistant from the two side walls, and about 38% of the distance from front wall to back wall. There can be nothing asymmetrical in the front half of the room. The left half must be a mirror image of the right half. This is necessary to ensure that the sound hitting your left ear is balanced exactly the same as the sound hitting your right ear. If that is not the case, then you will subconsciously "compensate" for the imbalance in your mixes, so your songs will sound fine in the room, but will sound lousy when played everywhere else. This is known as "translating": how your mix sounds when played in a different room. If your control room is not symmetrical, then your mixes will not translate. so setting up in a corner is not a realistic option. The desk has to be in the right location, across the center line of the room, with the console on top, also centered (since it is a very large surface), and your chair behind it, also centered. The speakers must set up against the front wall, equidistant from the side walls, and angled inwards at 30° so that the acoustic axes are pointing at your ears.
Monitor speakers can go on the wall,
Actually, they can't! :) They must be either flush mounted in proper soffits (the best option), or they must be on top of heavy, massive, solid, rigid stands, between the desk and the front wall. They must be set up such that the acoustic axis of the speakers is at a height of 1.2 meters above the floor (about 47-1/4"). There's a lot more to designing a studio than meets the eye! :) I hope all this helps to get you in the right track. - Stuart -
