gullfo7 March 2008#2
the soffit (more correctly baffle, we actually baffle mount speakers but soffit is frequently used...) can have absorption on it and still function for its intended purpose - namely providing a boundary surface for low frequency waves. so if you put mid and high frequency absorption on parts of the front soffit, (and even if there is multiple depths involved) you're unlikely to impact your high frequency response but you also want to consider that any hard/sharp transitions as potential diffraction points which can impact your overall imaging and response. 5.1 soffit mounting takes a lot more care to balance all of those considerations than simply having stereo...

gullfo9 March 2008#5
if you take a look at some of John's soffit designs, you'll see how the baffle hard surfaces and absorption surfaces work together. in addition, John has provided some drawings of portable surfaces where you can see a number of the absorption panels being integrated with the hard panels to create a good balance. on diffraction - if you look at many speakers you'll notice that the edges are rounded. this allows a smoother transition for the low frequencies to wrap around the speaker. this avoids a diffraction effect on the front edge corners of the cabinet from creating illusionary point sources which could alter your perception of the phantom stereo image as well as potentially create comb filtering and other undesirable effects.

gullfo9 March 2008#7
actually, because the edges are rounded, you probably want to route the inside of the baffle plate so the monitor sits flush - the idea being to reduce any gaps to a couple of millimeters if possible and avoid any potential diffraction effects - so your monitor is effectively on an "infinite baffle" (trademarked term btw...)
