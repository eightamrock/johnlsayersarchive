Aloha, Perhaps something like this? In Rod Gervais’ book “Build it Like the Pros,” http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=5670 he points out both L.W.Sepmeyer’s & M.M. Louden’s formulas most widely used in professional studios worldwide. He also notes that the orders listed are (top to bottom) the first to third best ratios in both cases. L.W. Sepmeyer Height Width Length 1.00 1.14 1.39-----------9’4” x 10’6” x 12’10” 1.00 1.28 1.54 1.00 1.60 2.33 M.M. Louden Height Width Length 1.00 1.40 1.90 1.00 1.30 1.90 1.00 1.50 2.10 A few thoughts to consider: - Moving the control room to the far side of the layout will eliminate having to cart large, clumsy equipment through the control room and keep from damaging control room equipment & furniture. - In keeping with your request, the live room is approx. 15' x 15'6". However, having a room that is almost square introduces some modal issues that you'll have to deal with. You may want to consider a something like 1.00 1.28 1.54 which would give you something like a 9'4" x 11'9" x 14'4" layout. Hope this helps, Aloha 8)

is it better to do non parallel walls in the live room?
Yes. Actually, that applies to the control room as well (all the while striving to retain symmetry.) Here is an excerpt from the SAE website -
the first thing you must do is eradicate all the parallel walls in a studio design. I believe that a wall must be at least 12 degrees off parallel to stop parallel wall standing wave interference. That's either one wall at 12 degrees or two walls at 6 degrees each. If you can afford to make the angle bigger, do so. Also you can create angled walls within a rectangular room by adding acoustic treatment. (I'll demonstrate that in the pages on wall treatment). (Note: having non parallel walls doesn't entirely stop standing waves - they still form within a room but along different lines of repetition). John Sayers http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html
and if so are there any formulas I should be looking at?
- The ratios outlined in Rod's book are a good place to start. - Excel Axial Standing Waves Calculator by Dr. Floyd E. Toole It has been stated that once you start angling the walls surfaces, modal response becomes extremely difficult to calculate.

gullfo18 November 2007#24
keep in mind that angling the wall doesn't necessarily solve the room mode problem if the average wall dimensions are the same as the straight walls... the main thing angled walls solve are slap echos or direction of reflections for high frequencies. room modes are solved by picking good ratios for mode distribution and proper bass trapping to correct remaining deficiencies... you might be better off keep the room rectangular and building traps in each corner.
