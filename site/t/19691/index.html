The vinyl siding and window will be removed to expose the frame, my question was how to treat this shared wall? There will be an interior leaf in the live room.
You seem to have missed the point that Brien was making: you can't just have a "shared wall" between the rooms. Rather, each room must be built independently from the other, fully decoupled, such that you have a proper "room in a room". Like this:
Image not preserved: msm-2-leaf-WallChunk-conventional-not-inside-out-2.png
The same applies to the ceilings: Each room has it's own independent ceiling that sits on top of the inner-leaf walls for that room, without touching the walls for any other room, nor the outer leaf walls, nor the outer leaf ceiling.
I measured 110db (C weigh) in the live room, playing drums. Approximately 80db outside (12 feet away) and the city noise ordinance levels should be under 55db. I'm looking for a room within a room approach with emphasis in low frequency attenuation.
So you need a total of about 55 to 60 dB of isolation. Right now you have about 30, which is typical of normal house construction. And you need to make it one thousand times better... ! Yup, do the math: each time you increase the isolation by 10 dB, you need to stop 10 times more energy. So increasing from 30 to 40 is ten times, 30 to 50 is one hundred times, and 30 to 60 is one thousand times. This is why the ONLY way you can do that at a reasonable cost, is as outlined above: fully-decoupled 2-leaf MSM isolation.
My contractor said he could finish the current ceiling by adding insulation and drywall to then install a new ridge beam below the existing one and new rafters that are essentially floating, although they will be attached to the inside wall frame. Then we will add insulation>5/8"drywall>GreenGlue>5/8"drywall - My first thought was this is a 3 leaf ceiling!
Right. As Brien said, that would indeed be a 3-leaf ceiling.
but I was wondering if it can work. We had a hard time figuring out how to decouple the ceiling without installing new rafters, and this was sort of his interpretation of what a room within a room means...
Yes it can work. Sometimes there is no choice but to do a 3-leaf, and even though the isolation is lower than for the equivalent 2-leaf, you can compensate for that with more mass and bigger air gaps. In your case, you would do that by putting drywall on the bottom of those joists to create the "middle" leaf, then building your rooms under that, and putting the final ceilings on as a third leaf. If you get enough mass and air gaps, then you can still get the isolation you want.
Lastly, I can simply have a 2 leaf wall between the control room and the live room, correct? Is there a better approach?
Yep! Do each room as a single leaf, within the outer shell of the building, which is your outer leaf. That gives you the correct "fully-decoupled 2-leaf room-in-a-room" that you need for maximum isolation. The same applies to all doors and windows: they must also be done double, with a pair of back-to-back doors, and a pair of back-to-back glass panes. - Stuart -

Right, but that's what it is. Even though you didn't intend it to be one, it still is one. It is basically a very large drum: a flexible membrane stretched over a rigid frame, enclosing a resonant cavity. Like all drums, it will resonate at certain specific frequencies, and those could be excited either by impact or by sympathetic resonance. The frequencies that it is tuned to depend on three factors: the mass of the floor (or rather, the surface density), the depth of the air gap (which varies in your case, as the underlying slab slopes, implying broad resonance at many frequencies), and the resilience of the spring in between (the air in the cavity, in your case). Basic MSM theory applies, the same as for the walls and ceiling. Basically, your floor will resonate at some frequencies but not others, which is what floating floors are supposed to do, but for a properly floated floor the mass, depth and resilience are carefully chosen such that the fundamental resonant frequency is an octave lower than the the lowest frequency that needs isolating. That needs a lot of mass, as the above thread points out. That type of mass can only really be obtained from materials like concrete. Wood isn't massive enough to do that, so the resonant frequency will be very high, within the audible range. How much of a problem that will be, remains to be seen, but there will undoubtedly be resonance going on. That wasn't shown in your PDF or SKP files. That's one of the reasons why we suggest doing a complete model in SketchUp before people start building, so we can spot potential catastrophes in the design stage, where they are easy to fix, rather than in the "Oops! But I already build it! What now?" stage, where they are much harder (and more expensive) to fix. - Stuart -
