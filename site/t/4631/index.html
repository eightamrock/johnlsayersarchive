knightfly23 November 2005#2
Symmetry is important to get an accurate stereo image, and greater depth behind you gives a bit more flexibility in positioning your head and speakers to keep them out of peaks/nulls in the room - instead of doing that, I'd consider getting a video camera and monitor, or two of each if you need visual communications. Then I'd absorb all first reflection points, ditch the foam, (mineral wool or rigid fiberglass costs less and does more)... In fact, if you need to see what's going on in the room I would turn your setup 180 degrees before I'd do 90 degrees; you'd keep symmetry, then you'd just need to position things away from peaks/nulls. (still need absorption at reflection points) This method http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?p=18059 should help you get close on positioning... Steve

knightfly29 November 2005#4
If you already have the foam and are counting pennies, then you could use about twice the thickness of foam - I'd also put the FLAT side of the foam outward, as the sculpting does nothing but make it cheaper to manufacture. (They can do mirror imaged sheets almost as cheap as one) - putting the flat side outward gives effectively greater material depth from the wall, which gives more absorption to a lower frequency. You can also help things by spacing the foam away from the walls; one way to do this is to glue foam blocks to the underside of the panels, then glue the blocks to the wall. 3M spray adhesive works pretty well for that. For a non-soffited monitoring setup in a typical room, you need deep absorption behind the speakers to combat SBIR (Speaker Boundary Interference Response) , ceiling "clouds" and sidewall absorbers to take care of early reflections, and deep trapping at the rear of the room. virtually all smaller rooms will also need broadband corner trapping, such as 3 or 4" thick rockwool or rigid fiberglass - if possible, filling the entire corner so the face of the insulation is at least 3 feet wide is even better. Auralex and Illbruck make a pretty dense product that can almost keep up with high density insulation, but the "wanna-be" sites like "foam-r-us", foam-by-mail, etc, have claimed Auralex specs on un-tested, NON-fire rated foam. To put it gently, those "wanna-be's" are a bunch of crap. One problem with using foam, for instance in a booth - people tend to try and save money, so they buy the 2" foam from foambymail, which is about like 1" auralex, then they cover the entire surface of the booth with it. You might as well just carpet the entire thing with stuff from a dumpster IMO - all you've done is to suck out all the highs and a lot of high mid frequencies, leaving the booth sounding like a box. A better, cheaper way is to use at least 4" thick absorbent, and only put it within a foot or two of the same height as the "instrument" (your mouth, if this is a vox booth) and on the ceiling. Then, make some recordings and listen to them critically. If they sound good, you're done. If they're too bright or "ring-y", you need a bit more. If you use this "cut and try" method, you'll use less material and "sneak up on" your ideal sound, without losing too many highs in the process. Remember, if you have a really dead booth and you "turn up the treble" to make it bright, you're ALSO turning up the HISS and any other high frequency noise in your recordings; do this on more than one track, and you MULTIPLY the CRAP. Not a good plan... Not trying to sound "rabid", but booths are hard enough to get sounding good without "mattress factories" trying to sell you ineffective stuff... Steve
