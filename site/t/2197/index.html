Ok, i'm not the most advanced around here, but let's go... in order to get a reflectionfree zone (rfz) at the mix position, each sidewall is angled by 6° from axis. this will direct first reflections from the sidewalls to the backwall where it can be absorbed (a must in small rooms) or diffused. it also kills flutter echoes which build up between parallel walls. If you are dealing with an existing room, you can handel this with slat resonators which give all advantages already described and at the same time also trap mid/low-mid frequencies. look here to learn more: http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html http://www.johnlsayers.com/HR/index1.htm have fun! dreamer

knightfly27 August 2004#5
Good call on the studiotips thread - However, on wall splaying - 6 degrees each side (or sometimes even 6 degrees TOTAL) will take care of flutter, but isn't enough angle to take care of early reflections (the RFZ, or Reflection Free Zone, principle) - for that, you need greater angles. I did an example of testing a design for early reflection possibilities, it's near the bottom of this page - http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... =8931#8931 Basically, the side walls need to be angled enough so that any first reflections from either speaker to walls will be re-directed AWAY from your head, making that sound path at least 7 meters LONGER than the DIRECT path to your head - this is trickier than it seems, but is doable. IF you don't have enough room to spare for the steeper angles, you need to use the "mirror trick" - this is where you have a friend hold a mirror flat against walls (and ceiling) and move it around the room - anywhere you can see either speaker in the mirror while sitting at the mix position, you need to place sound absorbent material, such as (at least) 4" (100mm) thick acoustic foam, or 2" or thicker fiberglass or rockwool - Hope that helps... Steve

knightfly27 August 2004#7
Yeah, actually that method also works just for checking angles of front half of control room - if you draw a line from either speaker center to each end of a wall section, then draw the reflected angle from each of THOSE lines (if incident angle is minus 14 degrees, reflected angle will be PLUS 14 degrees, relative to a perpendicular line off the reflecting surface) - then, after drawing the reflected angles from both ends of that wall section, everything BETWEEN those two reflection lines is a first reflection zone. If that includes your mix area, you either need to change the wall angle or absorb the reflection point. While I did that drawing based on a surround room, it still applies when angling front walls for RFZ - ceilings too... Steve

this reply will take this thread back a bit, but I wanted to post something to interest anyone building a 5.1 theatre, like GILES. I install a few a year. Basic, low to mid level systems. I use RBH speakers, and a few years ago they came out with "Virtual drivers". Basically a huge speaker driver (magnet and coil) with out the cone. Its only purpose is to shake. I have two bolted to my floor. Driving them with a 200 watt sub amp. I know it could be argued that if you have enough sub woofer, your room will shake. Fair enough, but whatever- I love my floor shakers. I sold a set to a guy who had them bolted to the bottom of his couch. I seen him a few months later in a store and said, "hey how you like those shakers" this big grin came acrossed his face and he gave this thumbs up. Enough said. RBH quit making them as there was not a big demand, but I hear they are out there. I would say build you theatre the way you would, and If you want to add a fair amount of realism to the room, consider installing some of these. d
