knightfly12 October 2004#2
Although splayed walls are better for mixing, and non-parallel rooms done correctly are better for recording, one thing rectangular rooms have going for them is predictability. However, you should NOT consider building any room with two dimensions the same, period. AS to floating or not - flanking noise will make it impossible to achieve better than about STC50 unless all interior surfaces are decoupled from the main structure. This means floating floor, walls not touching outer walls, and ceiling suspended resiliently from outer ceiling or resting on the floated walls. Anything less will disappoint you (and your prospective tenant, unless he/she LOVES your music over and over and over...) How much headroom is available to you? If you can spare about a foot, this should be easy. Any less, and we'll need to talk about your existing construction, budget, etc... Steve

knightfly13 October 2004#4
What you're considering could work pretty well; but actually, I meant that no two of the THREE dimensions of a room should be the same; acoustics is 3-dimensional, so ceiling height is as much a factor as width and length. There are several so-called "ideal" room ratios that supposedly are better for sound than others, but in reality you sometimes have to live with what you have. In your case, knowing that dimensions should differ can help you choose how much of your space to make into each room. One of the "ideal" ratios, put forth by Sepmeyer, is 1:1.6:2.33 - this means that if the height were 10 feet, width should be 16 feet and length should be 23.3 feet for best modal distribution (most even frequency response) - If you were to use that ratio for an 8 foot ceiling, you'd have 80% of those values, or 8 foot, 12.8', and 18.6' - there are other ratios of length/width/height that also work well - probably the easiest way is to stay away from even numbers and ask us to calculate these for you, if you don't want to get into it that deep. It's almost 4 am here, so gotta get some sleep; if you've not already checked out some of the other stuff at the SAE site, have fun; there's a lot there, several days worth of reading if you want... Steve

knightfly13 October 2004#6
First, even Google doesn't know what donaconda is - do you have a link or picture or anything? Second, what you're describing sounds more like at least a 4 leaf wall in total; and yes, that's NOT the best (or even a GOOD) way to use materials. Check the drawing out at the top of this page - http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... 1&start=15 It shows the proper order of materials for best use. Every time you use a small air gap and more mass, you "short out" whatever sounds are at the mass-air-mass resonance of that combination of materials - by keeping all mass in only two locations, and making those two mass centers as heavy as possible with as wide an air gap as possible, you lower the mass-air-mass resonance as much as it can be. Ideally, you would want this resonance to fall at least an octave below the lowest frequency you want to isolate. Having several small air gaps in a wall construction just makes the wall weak at several different frequencies. Think of the air space in your wall as a spring; which is stiffer, one long spring or several short ones? So, with several short stiff springs separating your materials, you will have much more coupling between materials and more sound will pass through. What you need is DE-coupling; this can be accomplished by using the widest air gap between two heavy masses. Are we there yet? Steve
