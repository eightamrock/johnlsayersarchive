Ethan Winer26 August 2008#2
You can measure a tracking room the same way, and with the same metrics, as a control room. Put a capable full-range loudspeaker at one end of the room, and put the microphone near the other end. Obviously, where you place these will affect the measured response, but you'll still be able to see the peaks and nulls at bass frequencies, and see the long decay times due to lack of high frequency absorption etc. --Ethan

AVare27 August 2008#6
You have received good information on testing frequency response. What value will it be for you? The response will show you what happens for that particlular sender (speaker) and receiver (microphone) location. Tracking rooms are judged more by their reflections, both early as discrete events and late bunched together as reverb or ambience. Because tracking is a subjective exercise, broad generalities are inappropriate. What is good for one instrument for one piece is bad for another. As an extreme case, a bathroom is bad, unless you want a small echoey sound. About the only the generality that can be made for tracking rooms is a low end with minimal isolated modes dominating the sound. Yes I did not write well damped low end, because some acoustic instruments sound better with greater re-enforcement from the room at low frequencies. what I am attempting to say, and I certain failing at, is to think through what you want the room to sound like and from that what the room measurable characteristics would look like like before measuring them and trying to interpret the results. Andre

andrebrito30 August 2008#11
For tracking room I usually measure reverberation time first. There are several indications by the ISOs on this matter but basically: The microphones shall be at least 1 m from room surfaces, including the floor. Sound sources are also located away from room boundaries and at a minimum calculated distance from the microphone. If the room is too dead one must use a barrier between the sound source and the mic to reduce the influence of direct sound. Low frequency behavior can be tested like Ethan said but that is a very general approach. Problem with tracking rooms is that you might not have a fixed source/listener placement. This makes thinks more complicated to understand but the truth is that they are some tracking rooms that have bass traps while others (usually larger tracking rooms) don't really need it. So you can also use your ears and a couple of random measurements in the room just to check if you have that necessity or not. Reverberation time can be measured with MLS, sweep as well as impulsive signal. I prefer an impulsive sound for large rooms due to background noise related problems but that is not your case. Frequency response I usually use sweep signal.
When I mic the drums, actually they sound quite good by themselves, but then I compared my recorded drum tracks to the commercially recorded dry drum tracks, especially the overheads, it seems that the commercial tracks are brighter, more open but not ear piercing, while my overheads had more low mid frequencies, and not enough high frequency details.
This might have to do with the lack of existence of diffusers in your room and the abuse of rockwool in it. I have said a million times here that a tracking room is not a control room.
