You will probably need to get a structural engineer to answer this question: how much load are those joists supporting right now, and how much more can then handle safely? That is a very, very important point.
Would this cause a problem if I built the frames for the new rooms directly onto the existing floor?
You say you don't need any isolation at all, so it won't be a problem from the isolation point of view (it would be a problem if you needed to isolate the rooms from each other, or from the control room, or from the building). However, it might be a problem from the structural point of view, which is why you need a qualified professional engineer to come and look at what you have, and tell you how much extra weight you can add to the existing structure safely. You CANNOT just go ahead and build without first doing this: that would be foolhardy. You need a qualified structural engineer to do a physical survey, and put down in writing his findings and recommendations. Do not proceed until you have that in place.
What would be the other options in this case?
One again, the structural engineer will be able to tell you that, but if the current structure cannot take the added load, then you will need to reinforce it somehow. He's the guy to tell you how to do that, after looking at your building.
I think I am clear in my head regarding what constitutes a two leaf scenario etc and as such intended to use the existing walls as part of the structure of the new rooms
The existing walls are already 2-leaf, but they are not decoupled and are not isolating well. Even so, you cannot add anything to them without creating a 3- or 4- leaf system.
Connect the newly created frames to the existing studs which are attached to the interior of the brick walls (hope this makes sense)
If you do that, then you are not decoupling, which is the most important thing you need to do.
Is this the right way to be thinking , i.e. that the existing stud and brick walls form a 2 leaf wall on the sides I am looking to use these?
Yes they do, but no you cannot use them as they are, since they are not decoupled and are not isolating. They are probably not built correctly for the type of application you have in mind, You will need to dismantle them partially and re-build them correctly, best case, or just take them out completely and build news, worst case (but probably easier in the long run).
The main isolation I am looking for I suppose is between the two rehearsal rooms, and then between rehearsal space and control room.
Whoa!!!! You first said that you don't need any isolation, but now you say that you DO need isolation! :shock: So which is it? Do you need isolation or not? :?: And if so, then how much? This is the basis of your entire design! You need to know how loud you will be in each of those rooms, and how quiet it needs to be in the other rooms when each is peaking at its maximum. THAT is what will define how you build your walls, not guesswork. You need to put real numbers on your isolation needs, in terms of how many decibels of isolation are required. That number will define the types of construction that are open to you, to attain that level of isolation. That, in turn, defines how much the walls, doors, windows and ceilings will weigh, which defines the total mass that you will be putting on top of the existing joists and floor, which is critical information that your structural engineer will need to know in order to give you answers! In other words, you seem to be going about this build a bit backwards! You need to start by defining the amount of isolation you need, then based on that figure out what type of materials and techniques you will need to get it, then figure out the total weight of all those materials, then call your structural engineer to find out if you can, in fact, put that much extra mass on the existing structure, or if you need to beef it up somehow. That's the procedure you need to put in place. You cannot start with a wall plan, not knowing if it will do what you want, or even if it can be built...
Would the two rooms benefit from both having the double wall to reduce leakage to the control room, or would staggered stud option be the best on the room nearest the control room?
Once again, that depends on your NUMBERS: A) "How loud are you?" B) "How quiet do you need to be?". Subtract B from A to get your number. Then look at things like IR-761, IR-832, IR-811, IR-766, IR-586, and IR-754, to figure out a good construction method that gives you the amount of isolation you need. You'll probably find several methods that you could use, so chose the one that you think is easiest and least expensive for you to do, and go with that. It might be staggered studs, it might be RC, it might be separate frames, it might even be single frame.... it all depends on your numbers. Without those, you have nothing. No point of departure. No base line. That should be your very first step: figure out your numbers.
what would be the best option regards the build ofthe rehearsal room walls - plasterboard gypsum on both sides with insulation/ airgap in the middle, with treatment in the rooms, or building one of the walls in each room inside out to make use of the space better?
Ummmmm.... that's two different and independent things you are talking about! You can build walls inside out, or not, as needed, and that has nothing to do with isolation. Isolation is determined by the mass on each leaf and the air gap, not by which side you put the studs on. The equations for MSM resonance do not even consider studs at all: they simply assume two leaves of mass and an air cavity between. How you support that mass is up to you. If you want to support it "normally" or "inside out" on one side, or "inside out" on both sides, is totally irrelevant to the isolation itself. That's just a preference that you need to figure out, based on other issues. Whether or not you build inside-out has no effect on isolation, provided that you have the same air gap and mass.
Presently there is a false ceiling in the main room which will be removed.
What is above the false ceiling? The roof of the building, or another floor above you? Hopefully, it is just another floor.
Re building the roofs/ ceilings onto these new rehearsal rooms, I cannot get my head round this at present.
Well, before you get to considering that, you need to consider the "what is up there?" question! That's the key. Whatever it is needs to be sealed air-tight, and perhaps beefed up with additional mass (consult your structural engineer on that point, too). Only once you have that done, can you start thinking about your new inner leaf. That isn't complex: You will have a new set of stud walls, your inner-leaf walls, which are only attached to the existing floor, and do not touch any part of the existing structure. You simply put new joists across the tops of those new walls, and put new drywall on them (either above or below, depending on if you want your ceiling inside-out or not). Done! :) - Stuart -

I'm assuming that "band" means a typical acoustic rock band, with drums, bass, electric guitars, keyboards and vocals? So we are talking about levels of upwards of 110 dB in each studio, maybe hitting 120, peak? Does that sound about right? You also say that you want to isolate well enough that band "A" cannot hear band "B"? That's a pretty tall order! To put it in perspective: To make 120 dB inaudible on the other side of a wall, you need at least 80 dB of isolation. A normal house wall with 2x4 studs and a sheet of 1/2" drywall on each side will get you about 30 dB of isolation. The decibel scale is logarithmic, so each time you go up ten points, you are talking about ten times the energy. Going from 30 dB of isolation to 80 dB of isolation means that the wall has to be 10,000 times better at blocking sound. Yup, ten thousand times. It must absorb or block ten thousand times as much sound energy as an ordinary house wall. This can be done, but is not easy. The isolation plan has to be designed carefully, and the studio has to be built carefully, with good attention to detail. The isolation is also going to take up a lot of space, and be very heavy. And you cannot attain such a high level with two rooms sharing the same simple "wooden-subfloor-on-joist" floor. Sorry to say this, but the simple fact is that, no matter how good your walls are, the sound will flank through that floor between studios, bypassing the walls. Regardless of how well you do everything else, the floor is just not up to the task. There are options, but they are not cheap. Definitely not do-able on an budget of 800, unfortunately.
If I did use the existing stud for the outer wall (removing plasterboard as you suggest to rebuild it as required), and built a new inner wall, would this not make the brick wall of the building the third leaf?
Why? Your brick wall will be leaf #1, and the drywall on the new set of studs will be leaf #2. I don't see where the third leaf is. My comment about "dismantle partially and rebuild correctly" assumed that you don't need isolation. That's why I also added: "... or just take them out completely and build news, worst case (but probably easier in the long run).". That's for the case where you do need isolation: take out your existing stud walls completely, leaving only the the brick wall as leaf #1. Then build a frame several inches away from that, and put drywall on only ONE side of the frame (not both sides). You will need several layers of drywall to get such a high level of isolation, but the all go on the SAME side of the studs. That is leaf #2 (built up from several layers of drywall). There is no third leaf.
Does the third Leaf only come into effect when it is part of the sealed new room.
Both leaves have to be sealed in order to work properly as a tune MSM isolation wall, but the third leaf effect does not require sealing in order to occur. Any time you have a large massive surface separated from another large massive surface by an air gap, you have a resonant system. So it doesn't need to be sealed in order to resonate, but it does need to be sealed in order to work as an isolation wall.
