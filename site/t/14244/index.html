Hi Mew. If you build individual structures you introduce many things that extend your work and money. It may get you the isolation between buildings but it will also require (1) each, HVAC per building and the electrical to bridge these structures together just keeps getting longer and higher in cost. I know you guys build mostly with brick and mortar so that is great for isolation as a start. I guess the question is how hard is it to get your hands on wood framing members and sheetrock as these are the easier materials to build inner walls for your build. Back to your slab, you can have developed a slab that would allow the entire studio to be under the same roof but still have a decoupled (cut) slab at the boundaries of each room. Depending on the actual slab quality (thickness, concrete PSI, etc.) you can build one slab and place the entire structure on this as well, and then sub-divide the interior to the dimensions of your rooms. In any event, getting the entire build under one roof is probably the better idea of the two. You are free to explore the option of individual buildings, but the cost goes up exponentially, and if money is a deciding factor, you will quickly alter your plans. The other concern about hvac air feeds under the slab is OK on the face of it. When your hvac system is properly spec'ed for this build it will include oversized ducting and silencer boxes, baffles, and often a series of 90 degree turns, etc. to break flanking and air-borne connections between the machine and the building and to slow down the air movement. This will be difficult to do with an under/in slab air feed. I am certain it could be done, but there again, it's not tried and true and comes with a package of problems that will be difficult to gather information, accurate information, on. And the level of difficulty to actually build this type of passage would require so much for-thought and blueprinting to make certain the concrete forms are placed where they need to be, at the level or degree or depth, they need to be...it's just to much like work ;)

"3. Is there a "golden" slab where flanking becomes a non-issue, or am i complete misunderstanding?" Well sir, it isn't about the flanking, it is about having the slab(s) designed to have a natural frequency of below 10Hz, as Rod suggests, 16Hz I think is what Everest states in the Master Handbook of Acoustics, and the frequency can be even lower depending on the use of the slab. I can read the math that it takes to figure this out, but I can't do it as of yet ;) Other things like consistency of thickness in the concrete pour and >proper< curing methods and time frames are imperative in having a slab that gets better with time.

It's not just a matter of level: it also relates to exposure time. In the USA, last time I checked OSHA recommendations were for maximum exposure before irreversibel hearing damage was something like 8 hours at 85 dB, then you have to cut the exposure time in half for every 5 dB increase. So 90 dB is 4 hours, 95 dB is 2 hours, 100 db is one hours, 105 is 30 minutes, 110 is 15 minutes, 120 dB is 3 minutes. (Drummers please note: at normal drumming level of 115 db, your maximum safe exposure time is 15 minutes...). So an SOL meter on the console is not going to cut it. It has to measure the "dose" (I forget the technical term), not just the level.
