Soundman202011 June 2009#9
The TL rating of the above at 63hz is only 20. So what does this mean in realistic terms ? Am I correct in thinking that 98db inside the room, will lose 21db when passing though the wall, leaving a perceived volume of 77db on the outside?
Well, not really. What the "TL 20 at 63 Hz." figure means is that for the single frequency band centered on 63 Hz, the transmission loss is 20 dB. So you'd need to use a spectrum analyzer to see what your level is AT 63 HERTZ, and then subtract 20 from that. But you are talking about only ONE band here: the 63 Hz. band. The "98 dB inside the room" refers to something entirely different: it refers to the sum total of ALL of the frequency bands, of which the 63 Hz band is just one. The energy in the 63 Hz band will be somewhat less than the 98 dB level (depending on what kind of music you play). In other words, 98 dB is all bands added together, and 63 Hz is just one of those, so it has to be much less than 98 in that band alone. So, unless you did that measurement with a single large kick drum tuned to 63 Hz, and you were really pounding the living daylights out of it with all your might (using a sledgehammer, maybe?) then I doubt that you have 98 dB of energy just in your 63 Hz band! :) If you were playing average pop music, then probably there wasn't much more than 60 or maybe 70 dB in the 63 Hz band. So if your wall is going to get you 20 dB of isolation at 63 Hz, that would give you around 40 to 50 dB on the other side of the wall, in that specific band. That's reasonable, actually. Could be better, but not bad. And since you are getting 20 dB at 63 Hz, which is about the hardest band to isolate anyway, you should be getting much more than that at higher frequencies. So your wall overall is going to isolate much better than you project, and the level on the other side will be substantially less than 77 dB.
If so, that is not good news, as previously stated, my neighbour sits on his patio 5m away. My aim was to reduce the leakage to almost nothing so as not to cause them any nuisance. Is this do-able?

Soundman202013 June 2009#10
I've measured my Bass Guitar and V-drums through a Drum Amp, using C-weighting FAST settings. While its difficult to get an accurate reading as the needle jumps all over the place, they generally seem to be around 95db, with a couple a peaks to 102db here and there. This was with the sound-level meter within 1m of the Amps. With rolling of the Bass EQ and lowering the gain a little, I guess I can stay consistently under 98db. I have to say, this shocked me as I didn't perceive my playing to be that loud?
That sounds about right to me. Around 100 dB(C) at one meter is what I'd expect from an enthusiastic bass player with a good amp. Don't be surprised about your level: Must musicians are! I teach a basic sound systems course for musicians, and most musicians actually are surprised at the levels that they play at, when you show them the meter. And they are even more surprised when you show them the OSHA exposure tables! At an average level of 100 dB, you have 60 minutes until your hearing suffers irreversible damage. In simple terms: Play for an hour and you go partially deaf. I'm not trying to be funny with that: just quoting simple real-world figures. Most musicians are shocked to learn this. At 105 dB you have 30 minutes. The big surprise for drummers is that at 110 dB your inner ear suffers irreversibly damage in 15 minutes. I never met a drummer who didn't hit 110 dB just warming up, with one hand tied behind his back! Most drummers can easily keep up a level of 115 dB without trying too hard, which sentences their ears to a life expectancy of about 7 minutes, before they start dying. And at typical rock concert levels (120 dB) you get irreversible hearing damage in 3 minutes. That "damage" in all of the above cases is estimated to be a 34 dB temporary hearing loss across some portion of the spectrum, with 90% recovery probable after 12 hours, provided that there is no more exposure to loud sounds during that time period. The most common sign that this loss has occurred is known as "tinnitus" medically, and commonly referred to as "ringing" in the ears shortly after the loud noise stops. If you hear that, then it's too late. No matter which way you look at it, these figures are scary. Many musicians already have some form of hearing loss, and don't even realize it. I saw a summary of a study done on a symphony orchestra a while back, where every single violin player turned out to have substantial permanent hearing loss in their left ears. And of course, the natural response to suffering from partial hearing loss is to compensate by turning up the volume... Anyway, I mentioned the above so that you don't feel alone at being shocked by the level you normally play at! You are in good company. So yeah, your numbers sound about right. Scary, but right.
Exterior wall with 2 layers of OSB ……230mm airgap ……. Interior wall with 3 layers of Drywall.
If you build it right, that should give you an STC rating of somewhere in the mid to high 60s, which is pretty good. However, that doesn't mean that your 100 dB level is going to be reduced to less than 35 dB outside, since it doesn't really work like that. STC rating doesn't actually tell you much about the low end of the spectrum, which is precisely where your bass amp puts out the most energy, and precisely where good isolation is hardest to achieve. However, you probably could expect to get it down to the 50s, which hopefully will be acceptable to your neighbors. That would improve even more if you were to use Green Glue between the layers of drywall. Green Glue does good things to the low end of the spectrum. It isn't cheap, but is well worth the price. So, yeah, I think I'd go with something like your "Option #1", since it should do what you want it to do, provided that you design it and build it right. Definitely build the two leaves of your wall on independent stud frames, put as much mass as you can afford into each leaf, keep the air gap as large as possible (230 mm is great), fill that air gap totally with isolation, seal everything totally air tight, then seal it again, and you should be fine. What I'd maybe change in your rough plan is to replace the OSB with something even denser and more massive (maybe thick fiber-cement board, or thick plywood, or both, or something like that), and specify that the drywall is 5/8 drywall, not thinner than that. Hope that helps somewhat! - Stuart -
