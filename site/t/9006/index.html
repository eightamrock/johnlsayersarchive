frostgfx24 June 2007#16
Jake: Buy Rod's book on Recording Studio design. That will give you tons of info and a better understanding about how to isolate sound and how to build an acoustically better recording space. If you go into the project with the word 'cheap' you will probably end up wasting most or all of your money by having to do things over and over and still not have the results you want. Take your time --- Read some books, visit some of your local studios and pick their brains about how they put theirs together. How did their studio sound when completed... Are they pleased with the recordings they are making? Listen to some in the studio and take a cassette or CD out to your car for comparison. It would be nice to know the physical make up of your space (i.e. what are all the walls, floor and ceiling made of? How big is your space? Is it damp or dry? From your posting about recording Guitar, Drums and vocals in the same space --- At a minimum I would suggest you build some Gobos --- portable frames built with 2x4s, Owens Corning 703 Insulation and cloth covering on one side and masonite or plywood on the other. These would give your some isolation between the instruments/amps, vocals. You can also purchase moving blankets that are about 5' x 6' and weigh about 8-9 lbs. each. These can be hung about 6-12" in from your problem wall, Make sure they overlap. This will act like a set of curtains in a living space and help 'deaden' the room if it is too 'live' or echo-y. You can find these blankets at better moving companies or theatrical supply houses for about $30-40 each. I use them on film/video shoots and they have worked well. They will not help much with sound of an acoustic drum set or a Marshall Stack on 11 escaping from your space though. As for your neighbors... make sure you check with the local officials about noise ordinances... Your neighbors today might become your adversary when you are recording too early or too late... or too loud! For comparison, I am building s studio in an old mill building. I am located in the basement of the building. I found a space that is only one story tall (14'6") The existing walls are brick with the exception of 3 areas (that turned into 4) of steel stud 2x4s and 1/2" sheetrock with no insulation... Guess where the sound goes... Cut to the chase... I've had to spend about $20,000 to demo the drywalled areas and 3 large window sashes (12' high) and then fill each space with solid concrete blocks. Now that that is complete... and I am almost broke... my studio space will be 6 distinct and disconnected (acoustically) houses. The floor, ceiling and walls of one 'house' don't touch the adjoining 'houses'... There is anywhere from 3-5" of air space between walls. The walls are insulated with rockwool and then covered with 2 layers of 5/8" sheetrock on each side of a 'double wall' (the inside of one house the the inside of the adjoining house). Each layer of drywall is taped and mudded. The corners are filled with backer rod and then sealed with acoustic sealant. Tedious but necessary to have a tight room. You get the idea --- You get what you pay for. You also need to do all the research you can before undertaking even the most modest of studio build projects to finish with a decent space. It will take twice as long and cost at least double what you initially project it to cost. Go slow...ly and may the Schwartz be with you!

Thanks for your participation on this thread, Jon! :) I was running out of ways to express the cautions that you very articulately expressed. Jake, check out Jon's project thread. I realize the scope of what he's doing and what you're doing are nowhere near the same, but you can learn so much from these projects and the mistakes we make along the way. I also wrote this in another thread last month... I'm repeating it here because I believe there is relevance. I hope you'll take it to heart.
    Success is defined by project owners on a project-to-project basis. If one has certain must-have goals and is able to achieve them, then by definition that is a success. Where it gets fuzzy is that people often aren't very explicit about their goals. When that occurs, we all start making assumptions, based largely on what our own goals would be. When we measure the imaginary goals against the stated limitations (usually budget), we make conclusions about the achievability of the unarticulated goals, using our fictitious goals as a substitute. This serves as a reminder to everyone to clearly state their goals when asking their questions, as well as a reminder to everyone answering the questions not to make assumptions about the goals. 8) The one thing that I (and I suspect Rod, and others) will always presume to be goals in a project is safety, structural integrity, and code compliance. Oh, alright, that's three things... But they're like peas in a pod when you think about it. So, if someone has a limited budget and certain stated goals, we'll assume it's going to be done safely, sturdily, and legitimately, which will increase costs over doing it unsafely, flimsily, and/or illegally, which may condemn it to the "it can't be done" category. Another thing... Sometimes we have goals in mind when we start a project (any project, not just studios), and then in while executing the project, we make compromises that compromise the outcome. Often the compromises are made with full knowledge, or at least high suspicion, that achieving the goals will or may be impacted, and then we usually adjust the goals accordingly. I also sometimes think there's a pride factor that comes into play when someone finishes a project and it doesn't achieve the goals as expected. Adjusting the expectation after the project is finished in order to call it a success isn't legitimate in my opinion. Simply put, such a project failed to meet its goals. We need to be more comfortable admitting when that happens so that we (and others) can learn from the experience.
