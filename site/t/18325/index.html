4. My general plan for wall scheme is a "decoupled" double wall with a 4" air gap in between. The walls nsulated with R13 fiberglass insulation and covered with 2 layers of 5/8 gypsum board (2 leaf). The windows will be a "decoupled" design consisting of 2 layers of 1/2" and 3/8" glass, angled to prevent parallel surfaces to minimize vibrations.
You were doing fine until you go to the "angled" part: angling your glass is a unnecessary, and in fact it it counter-productive. It is a much-repeated myth that glass has to be angled. IT does not. And angling it in fact REDUCES the total isolation, for a very simply reason. You get maximum isolation when you have maximum air gap between the two panes of glass. If you angle one of them, then you no longer have the maximum possible air gap, so therefore you have reduced isolation. Also, you have re-tuned the MSM resonance to a higher frequency, and therefore you have reduced isolation in the low frequency end of the spectrum even more. So if you need good isolation, especially in low frequencies, then do NOT angle the glass. And of course, the glass must be laminated glass, not ordinary window glass.
--rewiring the electrical circuits in the room for lighting, power, and audio lines. Something tells me I will need to hire out for help on this.
The electrical system and signal cabling system are critical, since they both provide potential flanking paths for sound. The two systems also need to be kept very far apart, to avoid induction causing noise in the sound system: the general rule is to run all your electrical conduit up high, a the tops of the walls, and run all the signal conduit down low, at the bottom. If they ever do need to cross over each other, then always do so at a 90° angle. They can NEVER run parallel close together.
--HVAC system (See photo for existing ducts) It has A/C, and seems very quiet, but how to extend it and create new vents.
HVAC is the single most overlooked part of studio builds in general. It's a lot more complex than most people realize, and needs careful planning. Especially if you will have many people in the rooms at some times, and just one or two at other times. This is probably the part of the design that is going to take up most of your time. It is not easy to get it right, especially if you need high levels of isolation.
-- FLOORS: would like to put in a wood floor --
Laminate flooring is great: excellent acoustically, easy to install, relatively low cost, wars well, etc. Probably your best bet.
Floating floors?

--we need to create a ceiling over the isolation booth and probably the control room to optimize the sound of the room for accurate mixing, but I really need advice on how to best to this.
Actually, you need far more than just a ceiling. The entire room has to be designed as a single unit, such that all parts work together for the same goal, rather than just add bits and pieces, hoping that tings might work out: they wont. A room put together haphazardly, without due attention to how all the parts interact, will end up with all the parts fighting each other, instead of cooperating, and the room will not be successful.

The actual construction is not really the issue that you should be concerned about: it is the design that matters most. If I were allowed to give you only one single recommendation for your room, it would be to spend about 80 to 90% of your available time on the design, and only 10% to 20% on the actual build. So if you figure the build will take you one month, then plan 6 to 8 months for the design. That might sound like a really stupid recommendation, way out there in left field, nutty, over-the-top, or whatever, but honestly, it is the absolute best piece of advice I can give you, and I mean that in all seriousness. Realistically, it takes about 3 to 4 months to learn the basics of acoustics and studio design, then another two or three months to actually do the design, and one more month to check it all over, fix the problems, review the calculations, etc. When newcomers to the forum first hear that, they often think we are nuts, but if you check over the successful build threads here, you'll see that this is pretty much the average, for any type of studio. And if you check the unsuccessful build threads, you'll find all the folks who though they could do it faster, skimp on design, cut corners.... and never get a usable studio. So very seriously and frankly, I would suggest that you start out by buying two books: "Master Handbook of Acoustics" by F. Alton Everest (that's sort of the Bible for acoustics), and "Home Recording Studio: Build it Like the Pros", by Rod Gervais. And also read John Sayers own work @ http://johnlsayers.com/Recmanual/index.htm Those will give you the basics that you need to know, then you can start the actual design process. You have a great space to work with, and a very admirable goal, so I really, really hope that it works out for you, and that you decide to use this forum to the max, for all it's worth, in getting you there. The forum is a veritable gold-mine of sound, solid, scientific information on acoustics and studio building: it's huge, and you are only seeing the tip of the iceberg right now. There's a massive wealth of information here, and a good bunch of people who can help you reach your goal, so I really hope you put it to the best use! - Stuart -
