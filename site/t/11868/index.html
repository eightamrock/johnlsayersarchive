xSpace2 April 2009#2
Page 125 of the USG sheetrock install manual: "Strip Lamination (vertical face layer, sidewalls only) This method is often preferred because it requires less compound and improves sound attenuation. Apply strips (4 beads, each 3/8" wide by 1/2" high spaced 1- 1/2" o.c.) 24" o.c. max. Place permanent fasteners 24" o.c. max. at each end of the face layer. Drive type G screws as shown in diagram." Same thing, excepting that USG is using the word "strips" of compound, implying that a deep notched trowel be used to generate the strips for this lamination process. The compound in this instance is used to laminate or "glue" the layers together, with screws being used sparingly. It should not surprise you that you do not want the fasteners to bind the layers up on the same support material. Everything that is looked at in these builds is about decoupling, flexing ability, etc.

This is Steve's 2006 updated response on this subject via the link in dfeiszli's post above...
Just got off the phone with Rod Gervais, a member here; Rod also moderates the acoustics forum on RO, and has more construction experience than most entire construction companies - he and I are in complete agreement on the following, confirmed by our phone conversation. USG, on page 118-119 of the USG construction manual, (once we deciphered it) does indeed say that it's OK to use strip lamination and laminating (Type G) screws for face layers on sound walls - they recommend only about 6 actual permanent fasteners per sheet of drywall, placed across the short end of the sheet - then, a stripe of joint compound next to each stud (placed immediately prior to installing laminating screws) and about 5-6 laminating screws per sheet (look at the manual, there is a a diagram next to the text on those pages) Both Rod and myself do NOT, repeat NOT, recommend this type of construction - neither of us has a high enough confidence level in joint compound to trust a wall with only 6 "real" screws to stay put with building shifts due to seismic, humidity, wind and temperature changes, especially since joint compound does NOT stay flexible enough to stay strong through all the normal movement of a building. The concerns of some over flanking noise caused by fasteners going all the way through several layers of wallboard would ONLY be valid if that wall were a single frame wall with NO separation of the two leaves - as soon as you put up RC or separate frames, you've broken the flanking path to a large degree - the addition of a full cavity of fiberglass insulation finishes the job. As pointless as it would be to use standard, fully coupled walls for sound attenuation, the use of any glue to avoid through-fasteners becomes a NON-issue - Since this post is locked, please direct any comments to the "glue and screw" thread, located here - http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=1603
I take this as saying neither Steve nor Rod approve this type of frame/drywall construction and that unless the frame is a single frame with no separation, having seperate frames (and insulation in those frames) would break/reduce flanking noise. In other words, you would attatch your screws for each layer of drywall into each stud.

Sandersd3 April 2009#25
Anyone interested could do a simple test: get a couple of 4'x4' pieces of drywall. laminate them, and see how hard they are to separate - then decide if delaminating is a concern. I mounted mine on RC. The first layer is not going anywhere because it's screwed on. The second layer has been on for several years without issue. The wall I tore down and replaced with the current was also laminated in a similar manner and it was pretty much impossible to separate the layers. I wouldn't do a screwless face layer for a ceiling or perhaps a 20' high wall, but an 8' wall is not a life-threatening safety issue. I don't think it's worth arguing over. Everyone has different levels of experience and comfort levels with construction methods and execution, and so will make different choices based on such.
