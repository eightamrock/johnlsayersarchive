knightfly2 March 2005#2
I'm seeing so far just single frames for the vox booth; are these 20 ga. or 25 ga. studs, and what's your plan for windows? Ideally, all construction would be double frames as well as windows, doors, floors, etc, so you had a complete mass-air-mass envelope between everything and everything ELSE - this isn't practical for smaller rooms if you're at all cramped for space in the first place. If you have separate frames, you should also have separate doors or it's a waste of space and money. For the booth, probably the best you can do is a good solid core exterior door (frame beefed up to support the weight) and good seals (like Zero International, about $250 for a seal kit) For your double walls, any doors should be ALSO double so you maintain your decoupled mass-air-mass envelope. Less will get you the chance to hear your drums BOTH ways - thru the wall, AND thru the mics (yecchhh...) Sorry for the short answer, I'll (hopefully) get more time tonight, AFTER some of the "Gators go to sleep... Steve

knightfly3 March 2005#10
Double framed walls should be separate EVERYWHERE possible, obviously they have to both rest on the floor but if floors are floated then even that isn't a common point. Do NOT put a 1-piece window frame in there, or you may as well remove one of the frames and gain some usable floor space. http://www.saecollege.de/reference_mate ... indows.htm Only diff is, your SEPARATE wood window frames would attach to the steel instead of more wood. Don't worry about angling the glass except for redirection of early reflections at the mix position; flutter echo off the glass will likely be solved by rear absorption in a small room anyway. Only other reason to splay the glasses is visual glare, which can be controlled with lighting position - After I get the metal framed out (and braces removed!) - I will then run the electric and XLR panels in the walls. No two outlets will be directly behind the other. Not just behind each other, but not in the same stud cavity. Then, I'll stuff mineral wool insulation into the walls. Amen. Next, I'll caulk all the track-to-concrete connections Not necessary; that seal will happen when caulking the perimeters of each layer of drywall. However, please tell me that's sill seal or some sort of Thermal Break material between your tracks and the concrete slab... After that, comes the double-layer rock. Have you read about alternating direction of layers, using spacers around perimeters, half-schedule fastening except for final layers, mud/tape of all bevels for under layers, etc, mostly at the REFERENCE set of links? Then, I'll frame out the RC on the ceiling joists - followed by the same - m.w. insulation and double layer of rock. Use "Z" system sequence - layers should be ceiling/wall/ceiling/wall for tightest construction, and walls should be done opposing pairs first, then the OTHER opposing pair, then ceiling second layer, then FIRST opposing pair, then LAST opposing pair - this will give a staggered joint, each layer caulked as you go, using acoustic rated (NOT LePages, or anything that's black and gooey) caulk. After that, I'll caulk all the wall-ceiling seems. Then come spackle(3 layers & sanded) then paint.. See my comments above for the order of these, except for finish. Doors will then be installed and *properly* sealed off. On your double walls, separate DE-COUPLED doors will help maintain best iso. Finally, the wood will be installed in the window frame and the glass will be set. (much reading-up on that topic) Some help on that above. I don't have a link to it, but there was an experiment done some time back where a completely floated, masonry double walled room was tested (something like 6" solid concrete walls with several inches air gap) - they actually fired a 9mm handgun into a bullet trap and couldn't hear it outside. Then, a small hole was core-drilled through ONE of the concrete walls, a small wood dowel was wedged between inner and outer concrete, and the hole filled with concrete again; Normal conversation within the room was audible outside. The hole was re-drilled, the dowel removed, and the hole again patched with concrete - No conversation heard, no pistol shot heard... Glad you're removing the braces - if need be, the tops can be braced together using RESILIENT sway bracing - check about page 17 or so of Aaron's floating floor thread for some on that, also Mason Industries, Kineticsnoise.com The back wall of the voc-booth is the "stairs" wall. I know I need rock on the side that faces into the stairs, but what about the other side - being that there will be another wall up against it - couldn't hurt to rock both sides, right? Be really careful here; it's sometimes hard to tell from pix or explanations, but you ALWAYS want TWO, and ONLY TWO, areas of (de-coupled) mass (separated with air/insulation) between where there is sound and where you do NOT want sound. So pretend to be a sound, and "try"(in your mind) to get from one area to another; if you have to go through some wallboard, then some air/insulation, then some wallboard, and (assuming you didn't get too tired :=) you are now weaker but where you wanted to be, then that is the right construction. Adding another mass (with its attending air gap) is NOT a "couldn't hurt" situation; it can worsen your LF isolation by as much as 20+ dB. That's the diff between being able to play drums at 2 AM and having your spouse get pissed because they can't watch TV for the noise. For the doors - so I guess I'll need to get a door for each wall. Was in Lowe's last night and saw some pre-hung SOLID-Pine doors (with no holes cut in them). Will this be ok - with proper sealing? Yes, those should work good. If the prehung thing includes a threshold and it's an aluminum extrusion, be sure to also buy a bucket of Plumber's Putty, and FILL ANY VOIDS in the extrusion before installing - or, you could change the threshold to something like my sketch in Hugo's recent post, http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=3293 Think that's everything for now... knightfly3 March 2005#12
You may get some corrosion from sweating between concrete and the bottom channel. Even galvanized steel can corrode if it's constantly being wetted/dried. If your concrete doesn't seem to sweat (tape some plastic wrap against a square of it somewhere out of the way, and check it early the next day; no moisture, probably no problem) Even so, since these tracks are generally cut on site there will be areas with no galvanizing; one way to handle this is to spray the bottom foot or so of your frames all around with Zinc Chromate primer (good for aluminum and galvanized stuff) before the wallboard goes on. Here's more on that - http://www.buildingscience.com/resource ... novate.pdf Specifically pages 35-6-7, but the whole thing is very valuable info. Windows - I just answered this in a PM, knew I shoulda posted it too - Yeah, that large a glass will be heavy and would tend to exceed compression recommendations - if you go with higher duro rating, you would just need to use a good silicone seal (like GE Silicone II) to get a good hermetic seal. Let the rubber support the glass. If you need to remove the glass in future, there's always razor knives. Another option is the high duro stuff under, and lower density held against the glass with the outer cleats/screws, for easier removal. (and a good seal) Be sure to clean the glass first, handle only with clean cotton gloves, add some packets of silica gel for moisture, and if a double frame wall do NOT bridge frames with anything more solid than Celotex; DO allow the between-pane space to vent into the wall cavity/insulation... Steve
