What I would do is to forget all about trying to have direct sight lines into the practice space, and concentrate my attention on getting good acoustics for the CR instead. To do that I would rotate the CR 90° so that it faces the live room, and install a cheap video camera and monitor for covering the practice space. That would allow me to make the room longer than it is wide, and to re-shape it to match a known good ratio. It would also be bigger in terms of dimensions), which is good. That would also allow me to get the mix position out of that huge disgusting dead null in the middle of the room, and put it somewhere around the 38% mark, where it belongs, as well as to get rid of the 45 degree front angles that are eating up the space for the front speaker soffits / bass traps, and also to get rid of the glass in those angled walls that is causing terrible reflections at the first reflection points (not to mention eating an enormous hole out of the budget too!). I'd leave just one smaller window in the center front panel, directly facing the live room. Doing all of the above would give me a roughly rectangular shaped room, oriented sideways across the page, where the dimensions of the rectangle match one of Sepmeyer's or Louden's good ratios. That would be my starting point. I would then design a proper 2-leaf MSM wall and ceiling for the CR, based on that rectangle, but splaying the side walls by 6 degrees each, and sloping the ceiling up from front to back at 12 degrees. I'd design the speaker soffits (or just the front wall, if I decide not to have soffits) to have the speaker axes intersect at 60 degrees, instead of 90 which tightens the sweet spot considerably. I'd put a pair of back-to-back sliding glass doors in the side wall that faces the entrance, and that would be the main entrance to the CR. I'd also either put a pair of back-to-back doors out of the center rear of the CR into the practice room, or forget that door altogether to save lots of money, and just go out through the sliding glass doors into the entrance area, and from there through the double back-to-back doors into the LR or practice area. Either way that would free up both rear corners of the CR for bass trapping. I'd then think about where to install the HVAC system and ducts, as well as the special technical power, wiring and switches for the lighting, and also routing for the mic, line, and other low voltage cables. I'd also think about treatment for my first reflection points at this stage, and probably other basic treatment for the room. That's what I would do, it if were my room. That would at least give me a fighting chance of my CR being a successful room where I actually could mix well and expect the mix to translate well. If you post your detailed accurate SketchUp model of your rooms, then I'd be happy to show you what I have in mind. - Stuart -

I like your Idea about putting the back wall on a 6 degree angle and also the cealing at 12 degrees. thats good stuff!
Nope. You do NOT want the back wall at a 6 degree angle! You want the side walls splayed by six degrees EACH. Which means that the rooms gets a bit wider from front to back. It starts off slightly narrower at the front (where the speakers are) and gets slightly wider towards the back. This helps to keep first reflections from getting back to the mix position, and also helps to reduce flutter echo, among other things. Ditto the ceiling: It starts out lower over the speakers, and rises slightly towards the rear of the room. Same reasons. The side walls are splayed 6 degrees each, for a total of 12 degrees, and the ceiling is angled 12 degrees all by itself. The above assumes that the speaker acoustic axes intersect at 60 degrees, but that is NOT the case with your room. In your case, you have them intersecting at what seems like 90 degrees, so you'd need to splay your side walls by 9 degrees each, or more, instead of 6 degrees. But you do not want to splay your rear wall! If yo did that, you would mess with the room symmetry, which is very important. The rear wall must remain parallel to the front wall. You can lean it forward or backward as part of the treatment, but you do not want to change the angle otherwise. In any case, you say that all the walls are up already, so the above is all moot. You can't change it. You are stuck with what you have, so there can be no splayed walls or ceiling.

So I would still suggest that you build a proper MSM wall around your CR. It will eat into the space, sure, so build it inside-out. Or alternatively, consider what you already have framed as your "inner-leaf", and build your outer-leaf on the outside of that, so that it eats space out of the live rooms, not the CR. But whatever you do with the walls, you really do need to need to get your mix position away from the 50% mark. Theoretically, it should go at the the 38% mark (38% of the distance from the front wall to the back wall), or somewhere in that region. Never at 50%. Every single mode has a null at the center of the room, as does every single second-order harmonic of a mode. It's the worst possible place to put your head while trying to do a mix. Your EQ will be all over the place, and your mixes will translate terribly to the outside world. Move your console closer to the front wall, or get a smaller console, or cut a hole in the wall, or move the wall, but do SOMETHING to get your head out of that null. that will be one of the major issues that prevents you from doing a usable mix. If you do nothing else, then at least do that. Next: Do nothing with the floor. Just leave it as plain old flat cement for now. That will be your final avenue of treatment, if the room still ends up too live after doing everything else. If you absolutely HAVE to do something to it, then just lay laminate flooring on it directly. But leave it hard and reflective for now. Next: Measure the response of the room, as a baseline to compare against later, and also to see what special treatment you might need. Measure it accurately, with something like ETF, and keep careful records of exactly where and how you took each measurement, so you can repeat the final measurements using the exact same locations and methods. Next: install copious amounts of bass trapping. The room is short and fat, nearly square, and small. It will need voluminous bass trapping in as many corners as you possibly can. There are twelve corners in a normal room, but you already eliminated two of the best ones by chopping off those strange diagonal shapes at the front of the room, so you are down to ten possibilities. Your door kills another one, so you are down to 9. The sofa and console take up the front and back floor-wall corners, leaving you with 7. The rectangle "thing" (keyboard?) opposite the door seems to be taking up another one, so you are down to just six possible bass trap locations. :shock: So your bass traps will have to be HUGE! All that you have are the four ceiling-wall corners, the right rear corner, and the floor-wall corner next to the door. That really isn't much space. Make them even BIGGER than huge! I reckon you might need to do your entire ceiling as a bass trap, and include the ceiling-wall corners as angled additions to that. If you build your ceiling inside-out, then you can do that easily, and you wont' waste any headroom. Next: Install good absorption at your first reflection points. You seem to have glass at some of your first reflection points, so this is going to be a major problem. If you can't get rid of the glass, or move it to a better location, then just hang absorbers in front of it. Either way, it kind of defeats the purpose of the glass, and its a shame that you seem to have wasted money by putting in thick expensive laminate glass at your first reflection points, only to cover it up again with the treatment that absolutely must go in that location if you hope to get a decent stereo image. But you say that you can't change it now, so you will just have to cover the glass with the absorbers, and do without the incorrectly positioned sight lines. You can probably figure out a way to hang them from above the frame, or maybe put them on stands. Next: place absorption behind your speakers, on the front walls and the strange angled walls. It would have been better to build soffits instead of those angled walls, so unless you are willing to convert them into soffits (which would be relatively easy and cheap), then you are stuck with having your speakers right up against them, so just slap some nice large, thick absorption on them, right behind the speakers. It might help a bit. Next: Place absorption on your rear wall, above the couch, at least four inches deep: You do not want reflections from your speakers coming back at you from that wall, and muddying up the direct sound. Next: Measure the response of the room, to see what other treatment you will need. The final treatment will depend on those measurements. That's about all you can do, that I can see. Maybe someone else has other suggestions. In such a small room, you probably don't want a diffuser at all: Experts here will tell you that you need at least 6 meters or so between a diffuser and your head in order to avoid "lobing". A panel diffuser doesn't really exhibit lobing (like a skyline would, for example), but in a small room any kind of large flat reflective surface is probably not a good idea anyway, if you can avoid it. I think I'd rather use the space for absorption, or maybe for some of John's great slot resonators, or maybe a combination. But you also don't it entirely dead. You want the bass traps to totally throttle the low end, but not kill the top end. In order to not absorb too much of the highs, one common recommendation here is to wrap your mineral wool or fiberglass in plastic. That will keep the highs in the room while still absorbing the lows. Worst case, you could even put thin laminate in front of the wool, for the same purpose, but then you have the danger that it might act as a panel trap, which you probably don't want. But you should only do that once your room is finished, and tested. The plots from your testing (and your ears!) will tell you if you need to return some highs to the room or not. So make it easy to be able to get access to your wool even after the room is finished, so you can wrap some of it in plastic, if needed.

That's not a good idea. Commercial carpet is a selective absorber. It absorbs different frequencies in differing amounts, and unless you can identify the exact make and model of carpet, and obtain accurate acoustic absorption coefficient charts for that exact make and model, you'll never know what you are getting. It might absorb frequencies that you need to keep, or not absorb frequencies that you really want to get rid of. Thin carpet absorbs mostly in the very high end, and doesn't touch the mids or lows at all, which is the opposite of what you want. Thick carpet does a bit better in the mid range, but still doesn't get anywhere near the low frequencies that you need to treat in a small room. Carpet and egg crates pretty much belong in the same category: acoustic myths and legends. In reality, and contrary to popular belief, true "acoustic carpet" (just like true "acoustic cloth") ,is designed to be transparent to sound, to NOT affect it, and allow it to pass through without changing anything at all, neither absorbing nor reflecting, refracting, or diffusing. Many people think that "acoustic carpet" must be good at absorbing sound, or good at blocking it. Wrong. It is good at not affecting sound at all! Skip the carpet, and either go for proper manufactured acoustic treatment, or build your treatment yourself, based on the numerous recommendations and examples that you can find all over this forum.

Once again, a 3d SketchUp model of what you are planning to build would be useful. If you build it wrong, you can actually make matters WORSE, instead of better. For example, building a three leaf wall by mistake, instead of a two leaf, means that your isolation in the low frequencies (such as the rumble of Harley's and trucks, as well as kick drums and bass guitars...) will be WORSE than if you had built it correctly. And if you build a four-leaf wall, it will be worse still. This is not intuitive at all, but is solid, sound, hard scientific fact. If there are already two leaves there, and you build something that adds a third or fourth leaf, then you will REDUCE your isolation, instead of making it better. Most people don't realize that any time you have an air gap with mass on both sides of it, you have a tuned circuit. If you change anything, such as modifying the size of the air gap, or the amount of mass on one or both sides, then you change the resonant frequency of the system. If you add another mass and air gap next to it, the you also change the resonant frequency, and no matter what you do, in that case the resonant frequency will go UP. Meaning that the wall not only becomes transparent to sound at a higher frequency, it also BOOSTS transmission of sound through the wall at the frequency of roughly 0.714 times (reciprocal of the square root of 2) the frequency where it is merely transparent. Simple physics. MSM: Resonant systems. etc. So, building a wall the wrong way in the wrong place can indeed make sound get through BETTER than it would otherwis, since firstly you might drive up the resonant frequency of the existing wall into the normal hearing range, when it was down below that before, and secondly you might actually now be boosting those frequencies, instead of attenuating them... Food for thought. Acoustics is mostly science, but it isn't intuitive at first glance. Things might not react the way you expect them to, but only because your expectations are not based on the underlying principles.
