Hi Bob.
bwilkinson wrote:
"Since I live in Michigan, air conditioning is nice, but not absolutely necessary. However, I am also considering an HVAC system for the apartment, and may be able to leach from that system."
Actually that may not be as easy as you think. When heat and air systems are installed they are designed for specific room/volume so having a heat and air that the installers designed for one place and then attempting to pull from it into another part of the building, effectively cutting the systems ability in half +/- will create problems in both parts of the building. Moisture extraction comes to mind as does return air. I have a small room but when equipment is turned on, amps, black boxes, computer(s), guitar amps, keyboards, overhead lighting et. al., it gets hot, any time of the year. It's Spring here @ Xit4 and my room is warm even now ;) Think about that HVAC as it is often one of the biggest oversights and can prove to be one of the bigger room to room, machine to room, air-borne leaks you may encounter.
bwilkinson wrote:
"(2) If I use two layers of half inch drywall on the ceiling of the primary studio, will this be sufficient to block sound to the garage/apartment? "
Depends on several things. The air gap makes a difference on how low the frequencies go that the assembly can isolate but this area(ceiling) has to looked at just as if it is a wall assembly. The best results will be from decoupling the ceiling of the studio from the rest of the structure. If this is what you are already inclined to do, then let me move forward. When you take into account the levels of sound that this room will be subject to this determination will allow you to better understand if this idea of 2 - 1/2 inch sheets of rock will do what you need. Based on the dimensions you mentioned above, you are going to use wood I-beams or floor trusses to make this span? You have several options with a new build, weight restrictions permitting. You can add the mass directly to the top of the floor trusses which is the easiest of any solution...bar none. The alternative is that you will cut out strips of whatever you use for mass and install in-between the ceiling joists. Which will prove to be both time consuming, frustrating and tedious...trust me ;) If you are not thinking about using floor trusses or wood I-beams to make that span, reconsider. If you place a pole(s) it will complicate the build. It can be done, but there again, easy is what you are going to want after about three months into the studio build, whenever that date finally comes for you.

Maybe I'm really dense, but I still don't understand what you are saying. How can both sides of a sheet of drywall both be facing the attic space? I really suggest that you should post a 3D SketchUp model of your design (.skp), since what you are describing is ambiguous, and could mean several different things.
it will not create an effective "spring" (as in the mass-spring-mass) to cause resonance in the frequency range of audio, so I simply don't worry about it?
Not really correct. Any time you have a pair of large, flat, massive surfaces facing each other across an air gap, you have an MSM system. The spring is there (the air space) and the two masses are there. Therefore you always will have an MSM system, and it always will have a resonant frequency. Or rather, an MSMSM system in yoru case, since you seem to be describing a 3-leaf system. What André is saying, is that for a very large air gap (several feet) the resonant frequency will be so low that it doesn't matter. Because the air gap is so big, the combined resonant frequency will be less than half of the lowest frequency that humans can here, so the 3-leaf effect will not be increasing the resonance dip to an area of the spectrum that is high enough to be of concern. That is assuming that you actually are describing a three-leaf system with a very large air gap. Your description MIGHT be talking about that, but it also might not. Only a clear, detailed 3D model can tell for sure... - Stuart -

OK, well I'm not seeing a three-leaf in the roof at all! I'm only seeing a 2-leaf: You have the roof itself, (which you show only as shingles with no deck underneath but I guess in reality there will be an 18mm plywood deck, and probably some beefing underneath): that is one leaf, your outer leaf. Then you have 2 layers of drywall under the joists, for your ceiling: That is the other leaf, your inner leaf. There's no 3rd leaf there, that I can see. BUT!!!! that's the roof. You have a major issue with your walls: What I DO see is that you have no inner leaf walls at all! :shock: Both your roof (outer leaf) and ceiling (inner leaf) are resting on the one-and-only same single stud frame wall! So you have zero isolation there, basically!!!! :shock: :shock: :!: To fix that, you need to build a SEPARATE stud frame for your inner leaf wall. You correctly have your roof resting on the outer leaf, and that's perfect. But you need to build a totally separate, decoupled stud frame just an inch or so inside of that, and your ceiling joists will rest on that inner frame, NOT on the outer frame. There must be no contact at all between the two frames. Nothing can touch, not even a single nail. I think that might be the source of your confusion. Once you fix that, it should all fall into place.
The "pocket" I was talking about is the space between the studs, covered by drywall both on the studio side and the apartment side.
That should never happen: With a proper MSM build, you should never, ever have a single set of studs that has drywall on both sides. If you do that, your leaves are coupled and your isolation is severely compromised... You need to use separate frames, with drywall on only ONE side of each set. - Stuart -
