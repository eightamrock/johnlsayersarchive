It should go better over here, I reckon! You are in the right place now. :) First comment on your new desk: I really would not put the monitors on the desk. They are much better off sitting on stands behind the desk. Really, really heavy massive stands. And the speakers should be decoupled from the stands. There are several reasons: 1) If the speakers are on the desk, or on the console, then they transmit vibrations to the structure, and make the desk/console vibrate too. 2) If the speakers are on the desk/console, they most likely are not at the correct height. Recommended height is 1.2m above the floor (that refers to the acoustic axis of the speaker, not the top or bottom!). 3) The stands need to very massive and decoupled for the same reason: so that they do not transit speaker vibrations into the floor. The whole idea here is that ONLY the speakers transmit sound into the room, nothing else. Their vibrations must be stopped at the speaker itself, and not transmitted into anything else, especially not the floor, desk or console.
this will let me keep the desk reasonably close to the wall yet pull the monitors a bit further away from the wall than they are now. Currently I think they’re a bit too close.
Actually, its the other way around! The speakers should either be right up against the wall, if possible, or if not then at least a couple of meters away. The reason being that you get all kinds of nasty stuff going on when a speaker is close to a reflective surface, such as comb filtering, early reflections, etc. If the speaker is REALLY close to the wall, then those issues are up in the higher end of the spectrum, where they aren't too noticeable. If you have the speaker a few tens of cm away, up to a couple of meters, those issues are in the mids and lows, where they can really screw things up. The best option, of course, is to soffit mount the speakers, which totally eliminates those issues, improves clarity and stereo imaging, and tightens up the bass wonderfully too, but it doesn't look like you have much space to do that.

Soundman202017 April 2011#9
Stuart. You mentioned the monitor axis at 1.2m high. My understanding is that you should aim the tweeters at you ear. Maybe I'm misinterpreting what you are saying, I'm interested on your thoughts on this.
Yup. When seated, most people's ears will be roughly 1.2m above the floor, so if your speaker is mounted so that the acoustic axis is also 1.2m above the floor, then it should be aiming directly at your ear. It's not the tweeter per sé that should be aimed at your ears, but rather the acoustic axis of the speaker, which is at some point between the woofer and tweeter. Generally it is closer to the tweeter, so if your speaker manufacturer does not provide any indication of where the axis actually is on your specific speaker, then as a general rule of thumb you can just aim the tweeter at your ears, and you won't be far off. Having said all that, if you set things up as outlined above and then discover that you will have some kind of issue with that arrangement, such as a computer monitor in the way, or reflections from hard-to-treat surfaces, or whatever else, then you can raise your speakers a bit higher and tilt them slightly to compensate, so that the axes are still aimed at your ears, provided that you don't tilt them more than about 7 or 8 degrees (some say max. 10°, some say as much as max. 15°, but that is too much, IMHO). If you do that with soffit-mounted speakers, then you have to tilt the entire soffit to that angle, not just the speaker. (Nice looking desk, by the way!) - Stuart -

gullfo26 April 2011#11
yes, same 4" rigid insulation (around 48kg/m3) is your best option in most cases. this helps balance the mids as well as culling off the HF on reflection points. the superchunk and absorbers shown on John's site is the approach - don't butt them together the spacing helps. same for the ceiling. on the super chunk - make it a 2'x2' (600mmx600mm) from the corners (about 34" (800mm) across the front) and fill behind with pink (15kg/m3) insulation. lower cost, more LF absorption.

sayyousayme27 April 2011#16
With regard the alcove corner, I have something similar in my room. Also like you, my instincts told me that because it is a corner it would benefit from bass trapping. However, when I asked on forums I was told that this is not the case and that the corner created by the fireplace is not to be considered a corner in the same way the other 12 corners of the room are (i.e. wall - wall, wall - floor, wall - ceiling). What might be worth considering though is putting some absorption on the bit of wall that sticks out (i.e. the bit that faces the front wall). This might help treat first reflections (MF and HF) bouncing in the corner and reaching the listening position, smearing the stereo image and affecting clarity. That's what I will most likely be doing in my room, although at the moment I'm holding fire and learning how to use REW properly so that I can take relevant ETC measurements and find out from those whether the reflections from the chimney/fireplace are causing issues or not. At the end of the day, only measurements will tell you whether there is a problem or whether you are trying to fix a problem which does not exist. Cheers Max

making the face dense can help attenuate on the 1/4 wave velocity and the lower density with the pressure zone behind the face. the denser face material (esp covered with plastic and cloth) can help preserve HF. putting a limp membrane behind the rigid insulation but in front of the light insulation can also improve LF absorption but increases complexity and tends to narrow the Q.
