Soundman20208 July 2010#6
about the ceiling, do you think it would work to R-channel over the existing two layers of drywall?
Nope! that would leave a very thin air gap between the old and new drywall, which would give you a really high MSM resonant frequency, and your isolation would be lousy. You need an air gap of at least four inches (10 cm) to have any hope of a reasonably low MSM resonance. Take off the existing drywall, and even use that to beef up the other side, between the studs / joists. Then RC plus new drywall. That will give you enough mass and enough depth for reasonable isolation. - Stuart -

Ahhh! OK, that's better. But since it appears as just a slot wall with no leaf behind it on your diagram, that was confusing!
I was under the impression that John's inside out wall accomplishes both of these, with its double layer of drywall being the first 'M' in 'MSM'?
Yes, but they are still two SEPARATE things, acoustically, even though John's design places them on the same structure, to save space. I think I cans see where your confusion is coming from...
The SAE plan does not have the layer of mass behind the slats- ie. slats/insulation/air/mass, as opposed to slats/insulation/mass/air/mass. So they are different. I've attached the two plans to clarify.
Actually, they aren't different acoustically. A slot resonator consists of an open gap between two slats of wood over a sealed air cavity, with insulation in the cavity. Nothing more. Both diagrams show that. One of the diagrams ALSO shows the SECOND leaf of the MSM wall, which is not part of the slot resonator at all! It is part of the wall. So the only difference between those two diagrams is that one shows both leaves of the MSM wall, while the other side shows only one leaf of the MSM wall. In fact, you don't even need an MSM wall at all to make a slot resonator work: a slot resonator will work over any sealed cavity, regardless of what is on the other side of that cavity. You could build it up against the rock face of a cliff, and it would work just fine. The only thing that matters to the slot resonator is the dimensions of the air gap between the slats, the slats themselves, and the depth of the cavity. Whatever else might be around has no bearing on the resonator. (OK, before some perfectionist jumps on me; yes, other things do matter, like air temperature, pressure, humidity, porosity, sealing, etc., but I'm trying to simplify!). So both of those drawing are equivalent, except that one shows the complete wall (both leaves, plus even the floor), while the other shows only a section of the inner leaf, which is the one that matters for the slots.
Ethan's Bass Trap plans: http://www.ethanwiner.com/BTPlans.gif It appears that his plans all incorporate a sealed cavity with different arrangements of 703 covering it,
Yep, that a panel trap all right... but it is a TUNED trap. It is designed to operate over a narrow band of problematic frequencies. You tune that type of trap by changing the surface density of the plywood front panel (thickness) and/or the depth of the cavity. But that's not what you need for your room, initially! What you want for your room is basic broadband bass trapping. Once that is done, you then measure the room response to see what problems are remaining, and in that case you might well then want to make a trap like Ethan's, tuned to the specific problem that you see in your analysis. But you probably wouldn't start out with one, unless you foresee a major problem due to your room ratio. I'd suggest that you would be better off starting out with superchunks in all four vertical corners, and maybe some of the horizontal ones as well, then measure the room and see what still needs to be done.
I just want to have the location of the traps firm at this stage.
