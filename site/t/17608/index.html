3) Any suggestions? If I am missing anything please ask. Thanks for any help. I want to do this right the first time.
I would suggest that a good place to start is by defining how much isolation you need, as I mentioned above. That means answering two basic questions: 1) "How loud are you/they?" and 2) "How quite do you need to be?". The first one is all about the loudest thing that will be going in INSIDE your room, that you want to make quite outside, as well as the loudest things going on OUTSIDE your room that you want to make quite inside. Number two is about how quite that loud thing has to be. For example, if you plan to record drums, then you know that you will be at least 115 dB loud in the live room. So how quite does that have to be on the other side of the wall? That means in legal terms; there are laws about how much of your noise you can allow to get into your neighbor's office. Or going the other way, how loud is the "stuff" going on around you, over which you have no control? Eg, the manufacturing business next to you, wind, rain, thunder, aircraft flying over, trucks, cars and other traffic, radios, phones, plumbing, talking, etc. (I'm guessing that heavy trucks come and go to that pallet business next door, bringing raw materials and taking away product). So how low do you need that to be inside the studio, so that it does not wreck your recording sessions? The only way to answer those questions, is by actually measuring the levels with a sound level meter. So that's where I would start. Find out just how loud things are in the "future studio" right now, from all of the noise sources around you. Also find out how quite you need that to be, by measuring in other studios, or even in your own home at a very quite tome of day, when you feel that the level is low enough for good tracking. The difference between your highest number and your lowest number, is how much isolation you need. Based on that you can look in several of the research documents that are linked from the forum, and see what type of construction will get you that level of isolation. That will define the type of building materials you need, the techniques, you need, the thickness of the walls and ceiling, etc. So that's where I would start. You say you want this studio done right, so this is one good strategy for doing it right: follow a logical sequence that leads you to the design that you need, starting with the isolation. - Stuart -

Then you should probably cut holes in the walls, since you already have more than 20 dB isolation with what is there already! :) Just so you can get an idea: the average cheap house wall (2x4 studs with 1/2" drywall on each side) will give you about 30 dB of isolation (TL, of course: not STC). On the other hand, if you mean that you need another 20 dB beyond what you already have (increasing your total isolation from 30 dB to 50 dB, for example), then that implies that you need to block one hundred times as much energy as is already being blocked. You say that you understand decibels, so you already know that this is true. An increase of 10 dB in SPL is the same as an increase of one order of magnitude, or ten times the power. A 20 dB increase is another 10 times that. That's a total of 10 x 10 = one hundred times more energy. Or in other words, you have to improve the current isolation by a factor of one hundred. When you do the math, it doesn't look so attractive and simple any more.! :!: :shock: More math: If you wanted to get from where you are now to a level that is 20 dB better, and you decided to do that by adding mass to the existing walls, nothing else, then let's figure that out. Mass law (the principle of physics on which single-leaf isolation is based) says that you will get an increase in isolation of 6 dB each time you double the total mass of the isolating leaf. So let's say that right now the isolation consists of a normal house wall, as described above (2x4 studs with a single layer of 1/2" drywall on each side. Let's assume that is getting you 30 dB, and you need to get to 50 dB. So you double the amount of drywall, and add another two layers (for a total of four layers). That takes you to 36 dB. Now you have to double again for the next step, taking you to 42 dB, so you need to add another four layers of drywall (total of eight layers now). And you need to double once again to get you to 48 dB, so you need to add ANOTHER eight layers of drywall (total of sixteen now), but you still are not there. You still need another 2 dB, which implies about 50% more mass (at least it wasn't double this time!). You already have 16 layers, so you only need to add 8 now. So you'd need a grand total of about 24 layers of drywall to get where you want to go, based on what you have, and using only mass law to get there. That's not a very heartening exercise in math, but the numbers do not lie. Most people do not realize just how complicated it is to isolate a room, acoustically. Obviously, mass law is not the answer. That's why nobody even considers single-leaf mass-controlled drywall partitions for studio isolation. Fortunately, there are other methods of construction methods that give much better isolation with much less mass. The very cheapest method is called "fully isolated 2-leaf MSM construction". That's what you need. Nothing else will work on your budget. Not a hope.

I don't think you understand what a proper floating floor is, since you certainly don't have the budget to do one! A floating floor is NOT what you see on YouTube and some other places on the internet, where some amateur hammers together a couple of 2x4s set edgewise on the subfloor (on top of some bits of rubber), stuffs fiberglass in the spaces between, then plops down a sheet or two of 1/2" plywood or OSB. That's not a floating floor! That's a resonant cavity, also known as a "drum". :shock: It's probably the best way I can think of for wrecking the acoustics of your room. Stretching a membrane across a sealed air-filled cavity is exactly how drums are made, and drums are designed to resonant. So do floors built the same way. The equations for calculating that resonance are very easy to figure, and normally the resonant frequency of such a floor is way up there in the low mids, right where you do NOT want it. The entire floor acts as a tuned membrane trap, and the chances of it being exactly tuned to one of your modal issues for the room, purely by chance, are practically nill. A proper acoustic floating floor involves carefully tuned springs, elastomers and pouring a new reinforced concrete slab on top of the existing one, then jacking it up to the required height (calculated, not guessed): You probably have just enough budget to do that for your live room, but once again you'd have nothing left for walls, ceilings, windows, doors, HVAC, electrical, etc. Floating a floor properly is no easy task. I have seen alot of splaying in CR designs for the purpose of reflections etc for the best sound in the CR.
Yes, like I said: Splaying is done for one of three reasons, all of them involving reflections (of different types). So you should figure out if any of those are what YOU will be dealing with in YOUR room, not what you see in other rooms. Every room is different, and you can't use a "cookie cutter" approach to building a studio. Nothing happens by chance in a studio: it happens by design and calculation. So if you calculate that flutter echo will be a problem in your control room, then that would be a good reason to splay your walls by a total of 6° on each side, symmetrically, and your ceiling by 12°. But you can also treat flutter echo with suitable acoustic absorption or diffusion panels (except that your studio is way too small to use diffusion, so you only have absorption as an option), so you could do that instead of splaying, if flutter echo is your only issue. Or if you wanted to base your room around the well-known RFZ design concept, then you would certainly need to splay the side walls, so you'd ray-trace to figure out what angles you needed. Or if your calculations showed that you were going to end up with major modal issues on the 0,1,0 axis, then you'd also want to splay the walls to help deal with that, displacing the modes up or down a bit to get a better spread. There are many reasons why you might want to splay, but they have to be calculated, not guessed. And splaying your walls just because you saw someone else do it is not a good reason: You could end up making things worse like that, not better.

Yes, like I said: Splaying is done for one of three reasons, all of them involving reflections (of different types). So you should figure out if any of those are what YOU will be dealing with in YOUR room, not what you see in other rooms. Every room is different, and you can't use a "cookie cutter" approach to building a studio. Nothing happens by chance in a studio: it happens by design and calculation. So if you calculate that flutter echo will be a problem in your control room, then that would be a good reason to splay your walls by a total of 6° on each side, symmetrically, and your ceiling by 12°. But you can also treat flutter echo with suitable acoustic absorption or diffusion panels (except that your studio is way too small to use diffusion, so you only have absorption as an option), so you could do that instead of splaying, if flutter echo is your only issue. Or if you wanted to base your room around the well-known RFZ design concept, then you would certainly need to splay the side walls, so you'd ray-trace to figure out what angles you needed. Or if your calculations showed that you were going to end up with major modal issues on the 0,1,0 axis, then you'd also want to splay the walls to help deal with that, displacing the modes up or down a bit to get a better spread. There are many reasons why you might want to splay, but they have to be calculated, not guessed. And splaying your walls just because you saw someone else do it is not a good reason: You could end up making things worse like that, not better.
