"The main problem in this room is airplane traffic every 2-3 minutes !. The the Noise Floor then goes up to an average of 51db." This is a serious problem and cannot be underestimated. Most of what happens when an airplane flies overhead has nothing to do with the interior of the room. Everything on the exterior of the building becomes more important. Things like: Are you on an upper floor(above ground level)? What kind of exterior does this building have...brick, wood siding, vinyl, etc? Do you have access to the ceiling...your going to need it in effort to circumvent air borne sound? You are going to lose temporarily, with help of a window plug, the use of that window due to the LF from the overhead sonic disturbance. The noise from the airplanes, airborne, is hitting the exterior of the structure, this is what you hear after it flanks through the building and takes that always available path of least resistance. This is a fight that can be lost if it is looked at from the inside of the building. I have a few links to documents related directly to this, but my server is not cooperating at the moment. If you want them, PM me or just reply here. Brien

The elevated wood floor...did you build that? I am curious haw you can be so certain about the makeup of this floor/building? Being in the middle of this structure is better than the top floor, so you have that going for you, still, the bottom floor would give you a better start position. The window...you are going to >have< to plug that up. When all the energy from the overhead sound is spreading across this building it is taking the path of least resistance, and it is well known that residential windows( on the average) are just not designed to stop sound. Especially a "slider" type window like you have. Granted having all the concrete, especially if it is as thick as you say, all around you is a big help in reducing the air borne sound of the planes. I think I would plug that window, even if it is a temporary fix just to get an idea of how poor the window is at sound isolation and then I would have to consider that floor as a possible flanking agent. If you can tap on the floor and it sounds hollow this is not a good sound for you. when the airborne sound makes it's way into this structure it is getting into and under that wooden floor. But not knowing exactly how this floor is constructed, I could not be certain without more information.

xSpace3 March 2010#14
Sound insulation testing, that is outside of my ability, but I will explain to you what I understand of it. To establish what the existing ambient sound levels are and what the maximum offending sound levels are you have to take several measurements in several places on both sides of the wall in question. I would recommend using a stand with your microphone so you can get fixed readings with no interference from your body either moving or blocking the signal path. You will want to pick a time of the day when you are certain to some degree that all you are going to be recording will be the ambient levels. I would pick my first place to be as far away from the building as the building is tall. Example being, if the top of your roof measures 25 feet tall then establish a position 25 feet away from the building for your first reading. Make a note of your distance and position relative to the room. As you do these tests you will want to make notes of what the exterior of the building looks like in terms of means of egress or windows, any type of opening that may exist above or below the room you are considering doing your build in. These "holes" or leak areas can contribute to the isolation loss. As a matter of a learning experience, I would continue to move closer towards the room with my fixed microphone in increments of measurement related to the building/room in question. If I had measured my top of ceiling in the room on the second floor to be approximately 16 feet from the ground level, then I would move the mic inward to 16 feet. Take a reading and make notes...making notes that you can keep up with cannot be over stated. Moving inward to a position measuring 8 feet from the building would be my next reading position. My last readings would be maybe from 3 feet and again at 1 foot from the structure. This type of measuring will give you enough information to average all the readings to get an average ambient noise level. The last two readings might help to determine just how much sound the building is actually standing in the way of. Then I would repeat the whole process with the maximum offending sound levels. At the end of the process you will be able to establish what is the ambient noise level and what is the maximum noise level and be able to correct your readings accordingly. Then do the same thing on the inside of the room you are wanting to build in. You may find that being in the dead center of the room is the best starting position. But you do want to take several readings in several different places, and especially within 12 inches of that three slider glass window. ((reference example)If you noted there was a window above the room that measured in from the corner 10 feet and this opening had a total square footage of 15 feet (3 feet wide by 5 feet tall). You can expect levels to raise at these positions in the maximum sound level periods.)
