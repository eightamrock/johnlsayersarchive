I don't really expect we will be able to achieve this level of isolation required for full out all-night operation. I'll just turn down at night as required.
That's good, but you should still check what the legal limits are in your area for noise. Call your local municipality and ask for a copy of the noise regulations. That's a good starting point for understanding how much isolation you will need.
but some of the details of construction can be tweaked to suit a better build acoustically, such as thicker materials, and more layers, to improve isolation where affordable and practical.
You might already know this, but isolation is a complete "surround" concept: you need to get the same level of isolation in all directions, not just in some places. For example, if you do your walls to 60 dB of isolation, your windows to 50, your ceiling to 40 and your doors to only 30, then what you get is 30. Period. Sound takes he easiest path out, and once it is out it expands from that point in all directions. Especially low frequencies.
For example, there will be a lot of glass involved in the outer walls, but my dad is prepared to splurge on 10.5mm HUSH glass, which should have a better sound isolation character by a fair bit, compared with standard windows, if we install them correctly. (open to tips in that area)
From what I found on-line, that seems to be laminate glass with a specially designed acoustic interlayer. That should be good, but you'll need two layers of that (one in each leaf) with an air gap between them, so this could get expensive!
I won't be working with any really really loud muso's, but I'd like to be able to rehearse with a rock band at reasonable levels for an afternoon without bugging the (also quite tolerant) neighbours too much
"Rock band" implies two problems: drums and bass. Those are the two loudest instruments, the ones that put out the most low frequency energy, and are therefore the ones that are the most difficult to isolate.
I think the key to the project is probably going to be about lots of various panels on wheels,
That will be fine inside the live room (tracking room) to help shape your acoustic environment in there, but movable panels are totally useless for isolation. The ONLY way to isolate loud sounds is by construction a complete air-tight "shell" around the sound source. It has to be air tight: If air can get through, then so can sound. And that shell has to be massive, as in "very, very, VERY heavy". The only ways that you can stop sound are with mass, rigidity, decoupling, and damping. That's it. There are no other methods nor magical materials that will stop sound. If you don't build a massive air-tight room, then you don't have isolation.
but I am up for looking into building some internal wall structures semi-permanently if my dad will allow it!
Let's hope that you can do better than that! Soundproofing a studio with drums and bass guitar inside it is an expensive proposition, so the walls that you build should probably be considered as "permanent", not temporary. It doesn't make sense to spend thousands of dollars on building an isolation wall if you think you might have to knock it down again in a few months.

This is the space that will be available for the two spaces: LIVE ROOM & CONTROL ROOM.
First problem! Your control room is square. That's bad, since all the natural room modes from the width will line up exactly with the natural room modes for the length: You'll have massive peaks and dips in your modal response for that room. The only shape worse than a square room, is a sube room, where all three dimensions are the same. So you might want to change that, and choose dimensions that give you a better ratio.
Now, something important to mention immediately, is that the three new walls are to be glazed floor to ceiling, with over three metres of glass (!)
:shock: :ahh: I hope your dad owns shares in the glass company! That is going to be expensive. Two layers of laminate glass, floor to ceiling and wall to wall, is going to cost a small fortune. Do you really need that in both leaves of your room? You could save a lot of money by only using glass in some places on the inner leaf, even if your dad wants to do the entire outer leaf in glass, for aesthetic reasons. Just a thought
I know this is certainly not ideal for acoustics in many ways, but I'm hoping I can find some effective ways to make the most of it! (10.5mm HUSH glass, as mentioned in my initial post)
The glass won't make much difference to the acoustics of the room: the acoustic reflectivity of glass isn't a lot different than that of drywall or plywood. But it will make a difference to the isolation. Glass is several times more dense than drywall, so you can use thinner glass to get good isolation, provided that you also support it rigidly and get at least some damping in the cavity. It sounds like that wont be easy to do, if you want wall-to-wall and floor-to-ceiling glass. The drawing shows both steel and timber framing, so that will have to be done carefully, to ensure sufficient rigidity. The damping is going to be a problem. Large panes of glass like that will most likely have some low frequency resonance that you'll need to deal with.
The LIVE ROOM would normally be entered from the south, from off the driveway, where a ramp access will be installed, leading down off the drive to the double doors (for loading gear in & out, such as drums, piano etc).
It is really hard to isolate double doors, since there is no central pillar that you can seal to, reliably. Air-tight seals are critical for good isolation: No seal = no isolation.
There's an existing (was external) brick wall on that side, and stud & plaster wall on the garage side of that.
So if I understand, then the studio area will be divided from the garage area with a stud-and-drywall partition? If so, that needs to be done right: you can put drywall on only ONE side of those studs, not both. Since you still need to build your inner-leaf wall in the studio, the drywall will probably go only on the garage side of the studs. It is very important to NOT put drywall on both sides of those studs: if you do, then you'll have a 3-leaf system system when you add your inner-leaf, which will reduce your low frequency isolation.
Directly above are living areas, and a bedroom, so treating the ceiling / floor above in the best way will be one of the upcoming challenges. That's the basic idea for your two-leaf ceiling, yes, but that will only work if you do the inner-leaf walls correctly, and also tie in the ceiling to the walls. Doing just the ceiling by itself will not accomplish anything.
The plan is to grind and finish the slab for the finished floor surface, and use some floor coverings as part of the internal treatment.
When you say "floor covering", I assume you mean laminate flooring, or hardwood flooring, or ceramic floor tiles? If you don't want concrete (which is a good surface, acoustically), then those would be your best options. Carpet would be your worst. But plain concrete is also just fine, as long as it is in good condition. Since it is new, it should be just fine.

In a square room like that, it doesn't matter: all orientations are equally bad! :) Seriously, you need to fix that square shape and get it closer to one of the many good ratios.

Soundman20209 August 2012#22
not to mention the additional expense of getting the rather expensive dedicated monitors.
I'm not sure what you mean by "dedicated monitors". You can soffit mount pretty much any monitor, as long as it is not rear-ported/side ported, and as long as you have some method of rolling off 6 dB of bass. The vast majority of studio monitors fit both of those requirements, since they are either front ported or un-ported, and practically all monitors worth considering for a studio have room correction controls on them. There are a few that cannot be soffit-mounted easily, sure, but most can. What monitors do you have? (brand and model).
But it also seems like quite a daunting task, to get it right,
Not really. At its simplest, a soffit is just a large sheet of very thick wood, with a hole cut in the middle for the speaker to poke through, and some supporting framework behind it. It can be made a lot more complex, sure, and John often combines his soffits with bass traps below, which is a great idea, but it can also be kept very simple.
I admit I'd be quite daunted by the prospect of choosing the right monitors to suit the job, and building the wall and mounting arrangement just right to do it all justice.
It's not as complex as it seems at first glance: You still set up your speakers in the normal way, according to ITU and EBU recommendations, then you get your piece of wood, cut a hole in it in the appropriate place, build the 2x4 framing to support it, and poke the speaker through the hole. That's about all there is to it, in theory.
I would like to construct the corner areas so that I have the option to use free-standing speakers with great results as well..
Free-standing speakers can never sound as good as the same speakers correctly soffit mounted, for one simple reason: SBIR. That stands for "Speaker-Boundary Interference Response". It refers to the way the speaker interacts with the nearby walls, floor and ceiling, but mostly the front wall of the room. It's a confusing term, and some acousticians include many artifacts under that name while others only include a few, but ALL of them still happen: reflections, comb filtering, phase cancellation, baffle step response, power imbalance, room loading, reflections, diffraction, diffusion, etc... All of those ugly artifacts associated with the front wall will always be there with a free-standing speaker, regardless of what speaker you use, where you place it, or how you treat the room: But if you soffit mount the exact same speaker in the correct location, then all of those are gone. Eliminated. Don't exist. Buh-Bye. Not there. Or at the very least, they are greatly reduced. Of course, you CAN still use free-standing speakers if you want, as long as you carefully locate them to minimize the artifacts, and many people do that. But it could always be better if you soffit mount them. If you want incredibly tight bass response, a crystal clear sound stage, accurate stereo imaging, plus pure, clean direct sound, and you DON'T want SBIR, phasing, comb filtering, phase cancellation, power imbalance, et. al., then soffit mounting is the way to go.
more viable for me to use regular triangular type corner treatment (if there is an eloquent solution that would work around the brick pillar)
The easiest would be to just stuff those "gaps" full of mineral wool, floor to ceiling, then build superchunks in front of them, and more superchunks in the angles nest to the side wall. But that sounds like it would be rather expensive. That's a LOT of insulation! And to answer the question in your drawing above: Yes, you probably would need to replicate that pillar on the other side, to get acoustical symmetry. Even with the deep insulation around it, that single pillar will still have some effect on low frequencies, so the other side of the room should be done the same. Of course, if you do soffit mounting, then you do NOT need to duplicate the pillar, since technically the pillar would no longer be in the room: it would be inside the soffit, which is not really part of the acoustical space of the room. - Stuart -
