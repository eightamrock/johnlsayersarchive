.. but you aren't showing that in your SketchUp model! You show only walls between rooms, but not the walls around the edges of the space, that should be there to complete each of your rooms. 2-leaf isolation refers to the entire room, not just some of the walls. It has to be a complete shell, which is why it is often referred to as "room-in-a-room" construction: picture that you are building a complete, separate structure for each of the new rooms, inside the existing room. each new room consists of the four walls (or maybe more than four walls, if you have an unusual shape) AND the ceiling. You aren't showing the new ceilings, either. So I would suggest adding those corrections to your model, then posting it again.

floating the drums and maybe amps on plinth if necessary.
Not sure what you mean by "plinth", but take a look at Glenn's design for a drum riser, and do something like that. Very effective, low cost, looks good. You can do the same for amps.
We would love to take advantage of the huge windows and incorporate them into our build for natural light but struggle to see how. I also think the windows are being replaced by double glazed PVC ones before work begins.
Bad idea to use ordinary double glazing! That is not meant for isolating studios, and will not isolate low frequency sounds. Double glazing is meant for isolating typical city and residential sounds, which are mostly in the mid and high frequency range, and they are pretty good at that, but they do not isolate low frequencies, such as drums, bass guitars, the low end of the keyboard, electric guitars, etc. Instead of buying typical double glazing units, what you need to do is to replace the existing windows with one thick pane of laminated glass, then install another similar pieces of glass in the inner-leaf, in front of each of those. And of course, none of those windows can be operable: they all have to be fixed and sealed.
2/ Is the 2 leaf air sealed cavity design going to give us the best possible isolation between our rooms?
Absolutely yes, if you do it right! But you aren't doing it right.... :)
3/ Does floating the control room give us greater isolation between our live rooms?
Yes it does, but you very likely do NOT need to do that, since a concrete slab on grade is already pretty darn good. Floating a floor is a major task, and expensive, and really hard to do right. It's nowhere near as simple as just plonking down a few 2x4s on a couple of rubber pads, then slapping some OSB on top...
4/ Would building our frame to angle into the brickwork around the windows be a good way to incorporate them in our design?
The brickwork is your outer leaf, and will not be visible at all once the inner leaves are in place. The windows will end up as two-leaf as well., not single leaf as you show them currently.
I was thinking maybe a stage curtain or shutter/slot resonator doors built into the frame with fabric covered insulation secured to the back of each so when opened we get the natural light and when closed they become a functional part of the room.
Curtains do not provide isolation, and are only useful for treatment of high frequencies, not mids or lows, so that wouldn't work. Slot resonators require sealed cavities to work: If the cavity is not sealed, then all you have is wood slats, not resonators. And the entire inner-leaf itself must be sealed air-tight too, which isn't possible with a curtain or a slot resonator that opens.

do you think 10k would be enough to build this studio or are we asking for too much?
The best way to find out if your budget is realistic, is to call up a couple of local building contractors and ask about their flat rate per square meter for converting an unfinished basement or garage into a home theater or "granny flat". Add about 20% to that rate, and you are in the ballpark. You have about 720 square feet and 10k to do it: that makes for about 14 pounds per square foot: I fear that is very much on the low side. I have no idea what building costs are like in Belfast, but I would suspect that the going rate is much higher than that. Of course, if you do all the work yourself (or most of it) that can reduce the costs substantially, and if you look around carefully for sales on building materials, overstocks, second hand materials (from demolition sites), and such like, you can reduce your costs even further. Another option might be to just build the control room now, then build the other rooms later as more funds become available. However, on the down side, you are also building very complex shaped rooms, which will greatly increase labor costs and material costs too, as well as slowing down the construction. If you stick to just plain rectangular shaped rooms, and plan carefully (so that walls are always multiples of 1.2m long, and exactly 2.4m high, for example, thus matching standard drywall, stud, and plywood sizes) then you can reduce costs, minimize material wastage, and move much faster.

In the video above he mounts the track to timber, i wonder if the same can be done to on concrete? Hope so!
No, it can't. Not to timber, and not to concrete or anything else. That video is just plain wrong, if you want isolation. Each room is a separate, independent, self-supporting structure! It does NOT attach to anything else at all: not to the existing ceiling, or the existing walls, or to other rooms: it stands all by itself, and there are NO mechanical connections between the framing for each room and anything else. Not even a single screw. As soon as you have even one tiny mechanical path between the inner-leaf of a room and anything else, you destroy your isolation. This is easy to understand: get an old-fashioned tuning fork, tap it so it rings, then hold it up in the air, far away from you. Can you hear it? Very vaguely, at best. Now put the base end down on your desk: can you hear it now? :) You just created a mechanical connection, or "flanking path" between a vibrating object and a resonant surface. The exact same thing happens when you have one room inner-leaf connected in any way with some other construction. There can be ZERO mechanical paths between the inner leaf of a room, and something else. The ONLY exception is the floor, but since that is a hugely massive, well damped concrete slab, that doesn't matter: it won't flank until way beyond the flanking limit of your walls,doors, windows, HVAC and electrical systems.

even though we are four floors up
:shock: Ooops! I was assuming you were building on the ground floor. Didn't realize you were up high. So your concrete slab floor is NOT damped, and there may be structural issues with what you are proposing to do. Your first order of business should be to hire a structural engineer to examine the building and tell you how much extra live load you can put on that floor, and where you can put it. If you don't know that, then you cannot proceed. You are proposing to add thousands of pounds of load to that floor, and you cannot do that without first getting a qualified expert to confirm that you can do so safely. So before you do anything else, get the structural engineer to give you a written, signed report about that. Get him to take a very close look at that major crack in the chimney: there's a reason why that cracked, and hopefully it is not related to the building structure...
some tests will be needed first.
Right. Set up a full-range system in that room, play typical bass-heavy commercial rock music at about 115 dB in the room (wear hearing protection, of course), then measure that on a proper sound level meter (not an iPhone app!!!!), with "C" weighting and "slow" response for the real readings, and also "A" weighting for the legal readings. Measure at several locations in other parts of the building (other rooms on the same floor, next floor up, next floor down, lobby, stairwells, etc.) as well as outside the building in the street on all sides around, and perhaps even inside neighboring buildings, if you can get permission to do that. That will give you a good idea of how much isolation you have at present. Then turn off the music, go back to each of those points and measure the ambient level: that tells you how much isolation you will need in order for your noise to not disturb others. Finally, measure the ambient level inside the room, to get an idea of what the NR rating is right now, so you can figure what you need to do to get that down to your target NR rating inside the finished studio.
until the landlord puts in the new pvc windows
Once again, that's a bad idea! If he's going to do that on his dime, tell him that you way prefer to just have the existing windows replaced with single fixed panes of 12mm laminated glass (6+6). What else is he going to demo for you? What will come out, and what will stay in?
so we have our space and would love to start the build asap,
That's understandable, but that would be a big mistake: Designing a studio takes a lot of very careful planning, of multiple aspects: isolation, structures, geometry, electrical systems, ventilation systems, acoustic treatment, permits, codes, inspections, etc. realistically, you are many, many weeks away from that. A wise studio designer I know has a motto: "Studio construction is 90% design, 10% actual building". I think he's an optimist, too... :) - Stuart -

Then turn off the music, go back to each of those points and measure the ambient level: that tells you how much isolation you will need in order for your noise to not disturb others. :) This is actually a common misunderstanding, and the guys that sell resilient channel and resilient clips don't see to be too worried about trying to fix that.... Their products are good, and they do work, but only when used for the purpose for which they were designed. If you have a stud wall with drywall on both sides, and you want to increase the isolation a lot without spending too much or losing much space, then RC or clips + hat channel is a great way to do that. Take the drywall off one side, but the clips-and-channel or RC in place, then put the drywall back again. Both systems decouple the drywall from the studs, so that vibration cannot be transmitted through the studs to the drywall on the other side. That's the goal: the path has been broken, so vibration can't get through. But if you need even more isolation than that, then you have to create a bigger air gap, and the easiest way to do that is to by putting up a second stud frame, an inch or two away from the original one, and put the drywall on that. But in this case, the simple fact of have a separate stud frame also means that the path is broken! There is no mechanical connection between the two stud frames, so they are already decoupling the drywall. Thus, no additional decoupling is needed. The "broken path" has already been attained. If you are sitting on the bottom of a swimming pool, then you are already fully wet, and taking a hosepipe down with you is not going to make you any wetter! If your walls are decoupled, then they are decoupled. Doing it again is like taking that hosepipe down with you: pointless! :)
