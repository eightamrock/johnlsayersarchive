barefoot12 January 2006#8
Hi Don, I got your email. Well, I've been out of the studio design loop for a while, concentrating on the speaker business. However, I haven't stopped thinking about all this stuff. I don't have a lot of time to write a detailed post, but I'll just throw this little bomb into the water. -I'm seriously starting to change my position on the efficacy of soffit mounting.- The more I talk to recording, mix and mastering engineers, the more they tell me they just cant get good results from soffit mounted monitors. They mainly just use them for impressing the clients. In other threads I've discussed the benefits of soffit mounting. And those principles haven't changed - less diffraction, reflection free zone, and a flat power response. And those are great in theory, but the major problem is that the vast majority of end listeners do not have soffit mounted speakers. They listen to freestanding speakers, which have that +6dB low-end power response step I've described. Likewise, I've learned that car audio designers often tune their systems to emulate the power response of freestanding speakers. So from a translation perspective a flat anechoic response combined with a +6dB low-end power response step is superior. All this is leading me to the conclusion that a freestanding system is better. Of course, you still have the problem of wall reflections. Therefore, you have to think very carefully about wall and window angles, the liberal use of diffusion, and the judicious placement of absorption. I haven't had time to think about the details of such a design, but I'm definitely leaning in that direction now. Hope this doesn't cause you a headache. ;) Thomas
barefoot12 January 2006#9
Oh yeah, if you choose to take my words to heart and have already commenced with a soffit design, all is not lost. The solution is to tailor the ambient room absorption so that it emulates that +6dB low shelf response; or more exactly in the case of absorption, a -6dB high shelf response. In fact, this may turn out to be the best overall design. However, there are issues with reverberation time and such that need to be considered, so it definitely needs to be investigated further .

Sandersd9 February 2006#19
The whole house is wood framing on a concrete slab - no cinder block in Texas. Termites, but no cinder block. The interior walls will not be true walls - simply frames to support 3" mineral wool batts and are covered with cloth or slats, depending on where they are located. The rear wall and ceiling will be absorbent, the side walls will have variable depth slat resonators, and the front walls will be partially reflective (upper half) and partially absorbent (lower half). The existing drywall will all be removed, the insulation replaced w/ mineral wool, and two layers of 5/8" type X drywall will be installed on RC on the walls. The ceiling will be covered in fabric. I'll add additional 2x6 rafters alongside the existing to carry the weight of the drywall. The existing rafters are already stressed enough with the composite roof. They were designed for a lightweight cedar shingle roof, but those have been outlawed due to the fire hazard. The floor will be 1/2" fiberboard over a concrete slab on grade, topped with two layers of 3/4" plywood and 3/4" hardwood flooring. I don't think I'll need more isolation than this, as I'm in a residental neighborhood on a cul-de-sac. I just need a quiet place to be creative - no drums or metal bands. I think it will be adequate for the intended purpose. I've angled the monitors down ten degrees to remove the computer monitors from the sound path and to provide optimal coverage whether I'm sitting at the desk or seated in the chair relaxing. Two absorbers to the right and left of the mix position cancel any side reflections. I have a design for a window baffle I'l try to post this weekend along with design for the other recording room.
