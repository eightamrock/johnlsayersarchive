RobK wrote:
It seems one of the biggest alternatives to this approach is GREEN GLUE, with standard drywall layers, but compared to Quietrock 525 it's about the same price. I'm figuring that Quietrock will be easier for my contractor to work with also.
I disagree -- I think Green Glue would be easier to work with, once the instructions are understood. Be sure to carefully compare the lab reports between Green Glue to QuietRock. QuietRock emphasizes STC, but Green Glue is at the forefront of shedding light on the myth that STC is the "be all, end all" number to look for. Study Green Glue Company's Recording Studios and Home Theaters and Understanding STC, what it means and doesn't mean to understand why STC is a very misleading metric when it comes to reducing sound transmission in studio construction. Quiet Solution's marketing materials are focused on STC. Granted, Quiet Solution does publish the detailed third-party lab reports that show the full spectrum, but they'd just a soon you stay focused on the STC number because it's sexier. :roll:
RobK wrote:
One of the many things I'm unsure about, is that the Quietrock assembly example diagrams (except of course double wall installations) all include insulation packed inside the walls. Searches on this site indicate that interior AIRSPACE is needed within walls. Can anyone comment on this. (EG Quietrock single stud assembly with R-13 packed into the walls as opposed to Greenglue with 2 layers of 5/8 drywall with NO insulation placed inside).
Understand that most insulation is breatheable, so it counts as "air" in the "mass-air-mass" equation you read so much about here. Technically, complete fill of insulation is superior to an actual air pocket or a combination of air and insulation. However, it gets to be more expensive to do a complete fill, depending on the depth of the air gap, and the increased isolation relative to the cost can sometimes be negligible.

I have a sample of that 525 stuff -- it's really thick! It's like two layers of 5/8" with a thin sheet of metal plus the constrained dampening layer substance. Cutting that stuff requires power tools! :x Beware of the "marketing claims" they make as you have to study the reports to know the truth behind the truth.
RobK wrote:

You bet it is -- in fact, you need to address the ceiling with as much vigor as the walls, because if you don't, your ceiling will be the "weakest link" in your isolation chain. Welcome to the forum -- I hope this info is some help to you as you continue to absorb stuff here. :) Resist the urge to just "get'r done" because you need to become sort of your own expert before embarking on the construction. --Keith :mrgreen:
