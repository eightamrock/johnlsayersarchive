I have been told seen and read a lot to tell me 12x12 room with wood floors is bad.
12x12 is bad acoustically, because both dimensions are the same. So you will have room modes reinforced in both dimensions. You need to get away from duplicate dimensions, and choose a known good room ratio, or at least get close to one. Wood floors are NOT bad. In fact, they are GOOD. . . . when done properly. Many designers recommend wooden floors (or rather: hard, solid rigid floors in general), for a variety of reasons.
We are planning to use a low density insolation.
If "insolation" means rock wool or fiberglass insulation, then low density is no use. Optimum density is 48 kg/m3. Higher density than that does not buy you much additional benefit. Lower density does not do the job.

Then again something I dont get how adding the new walls makes a product that would help useless.
Adding mass to a wall will get you "mass-law" increases in isolation. Mass law says that you get 6 dB extra isolation each time you DOUBLE the mass. So if you need 48 dB more isolation than you have now (quite probable), then you can double the present mass 8 times over, and you will get there. So if you currently have 2 layers of drywall, you can increase that to SIXTEEN layers (add SEVEN new layers to EACH side) and you'll get the isolation you need.... That's what you will get with mass law. On the other hand, if you build a proper MSM wall, you can accomplish the same level of isolation with just 4 layers of drywall in two leaves. But you have to do it right. You can't just guess. How much more isolation do you need than you have now? What are your numbers? How loud are you now, in dB? How quiet do you need to be? Without that, you don't even have a goal. If you don't have real numbers to work with, you cannot possibly design what you need. And if you don't design it, it will not work! The probability that you'll get the right results by pure luck and no design, is somewhere around zero.

I know I have read R13 has no sound proofing/isolation properties.
That's correct. Partly. By itself no insulation has sound isolation properties. When used AS PART OF A SYSTEM it has amazing sound isolation properties. That is the issue. For example, hold up a 4 inch thick piece of rock wool in front of a speaker, and you'll see that it doesn't do a lot to stop the sound at all. But put that same piece of rock wool inside a correctly designed and built MSM wall and it will increase the isolation of that wall greatly, by as much as 16 dB. It does that for several reasons, but the two mains ones are that it drives down the resonant frequency of the MSM system, and it damps resonance inside of the cavity. When the rock wool is out in open air, it cannot do either of those. It has to be inside the wall to do that. By itself it does nothing. As part of the MSM system it is amazing. Like Andre said, acoustics is not intuitive, and not easy. You cannot look at individual materials and guess how they might act if you put them together. There are underlying principles at work, laws of physics and equations that dictate how things interact. You need to understand those principles yourself, or to trust people that do understand them. An MSM wall is a tuned system, specifically designed to resonate at a certain frequency that is way below the human hearing range, and to block all higher frequencies. If you do ANYTHING to that wall that changes the principle on which it is based, then you will INCREASE the resonant frequency, and it will no longer isolate well at the low end of the spectrum. That's what folks here are telling you. If you make a 2-leaf wall ("MSM") into a 3-leaf wall ("MSMSM"), then the laws of physics dictate that the resonant frequency of that system will be HIGHER, and it will isolate LESS in the low end. Anyone who tells you otherwise does not understand the principles of acoustics.

Soundboard is more expensive for the same amount of mass. The sound waves don't care how much you paid for the mass: all they see is mass. If you have LOTS of money, then buy lead sheeting! That has stacks of mass in a very thin dimension, and is great in acoustics, for some applications. But a thick sheet of drywall will isolate practically the same as a thin sheet of lead with the same mass, and only costs a small fraction of the price. Mass is mass: buy the cheapest you can get.

We are waiting trying to firure out how to remove the three leaf. Will it still have the effect if we blast insolation into all cavities so there is not air space?
If you just blow insulation unto the cavity, you will still have an air gap since insulation is mostly air anyway. In order to turn the existing walls into only one leaf, you have to take off one of the two leaves. The normal way of doing that is to remove the drywall on the room side of the studs, so that you can see the framing inside the wall, and also see the leaf on the outside of the wall. Then you use the drywall that you just took off to "beef up" that outer leaf. You do that by cutting it to the shape and size so that it fits in between the studs, up against the existing outer leaf. You hold it in places with cleats, and you seal it all around the edges with caulk. Do 2 or 3 layers, depending on how much isolation you need. You didn't tell us how much isolation you need (you didn't give any numbers, in terms of decibels) so it's hard to guess how many layers you will need their to beef up your outer leaf. Two should most likely be enough, but you might need more.
Yes echo fllutter caused by the room being 12x12.
That's not flutter echo. Flutter echo is due to walls being parallel, flat, rigid surfaces. The issue with the room being 12x12 is a problem with resonance, not flutter echo. Two different things. If your walls are parallel sheetrock then you would still have flutter echo between them even if your room was based on a perfect Sepmeyer ratio. And you could very well have a 12x12x12 room with no flutter echo at all, even though you'd have huge resonance issues in such a room. It seems to me that you are a bit confused about the terminology, but don't worry about that: I was pretty confused when I first came here too. 've learned a huge amount since then, but it takes time and diligence.
I was meaning with the birds that I dont need STC rating in the high 60s, 50-55 would be fine.
OK, that's a start! At least you have a goal in mind. STC 55 is certainly doable with what you have, if you do it right. But beware about STC ratings. STC does not take into account the frequencies where you will have the most problems. The STC rating system is designed mainly for speech, not for music. You can have a wall with a great STC rating that is no good for a studio. A better measure for studio walls is "TL", which means "transmission loss".
