INTRODUCTION Greetings! I live in Bozeman, Montana, USA. I recently built a room to use as a combination recording space/control-mixing space. Construction is finished and the room is in use. I barely finish construction of the room before I had to immerse myself in a recording project with a tight deadline. I hastily threw up a hodge-podge of sound deadening materials and finished the CD, but the results were horrible - I'm hoping I can at least partially blame the room acoustics for the poor sounding CD. ROOM DETAILS/FRAMING DETAILS: The room sits in the corner on the second (top) floor of the building. The floor consists of 11" engineered I-joists, covered with 5/8" OSB (chipboard), glued & screwed. This is covered wall-to-wall with a carpet pad and then Berber carpet on top. There is 6" of craft-faced fiberglass insulation between the floor joists, and the underside of the joists has 5/8" sheetrock attached, which is the ceiling of the room below. The interior walls and ceiling of the room are all sheetrock (gypsum board) except for the doors and windows. Here are overhead and elevation sketches of the room, pretty much to scale:

The left wall separates the room from my wife's office. If I understand that correctly, then that is a three-leaf wall, which is not good. It means that you will get poor isolation in the low frequencies (kick drums, bass, etc.). But you did say that isolation is not a concern, so that's not really important for solving your acoustics issue! However, it is something that you should be aware of if you ever do decide to work on your isolation problems! You'd need to take off one of the layers of sheetrock, preferably the one INSIDE the wall, on the staggered studs. Just taking that off will improve your isolation by about 6 dB (maybe more) even if you do nothing else at all. Your walls must have two leaves, and only two. Not one, three, four or anything else. Anyway, on to the REAL problem:
I believe the acoustical properties of this product are not substantially different from regular sheetrock, except that it reduces the sound transmission through the walls. In other words, I'm hoping we can consider it regular sheetrock for our purposes here.

The room (sadly) is roughly square, 13' 9" wide by 14' 6" long.
Like xSpace said, this is your main problem. It's not quite square, and I haven't run the numbers, but that looks close enough to me that you are going to have some pretty bad mode issues. Also, at some point along it, the sloping ceiling is exactly twice the width dimension, and at another point is is exactly twice the length dimension, so you have additional mode issues right there too.
there is carpet pad and berber carpet on the entire floor.
Probably not a good idea. Most people here seem to recommend keeping your floor live, with concrete, wood, laminate, etc. You can always put throw-rugs or similar if you need to deaden a bit, but it's hard to "liven" a carpet floor. Ethan Winer argues that your brain is used to hearing reflections from floors your whole life, so it's better to have a live floor and dead roof. Any chance of replacing that carpet with laminate flooring?
I feel the room sounds too dead. I know it need better bass trapping. I think the square geometry and small size of the room are working against me.
Bingo! That's it, basically. First question: Any chance you can move a wall, or build a new wall, to get rid of that squareness? Based on your budget, I guess this is out of the question, but I had to ask! But bass trapping, and lots of it, is probably where you need to start.
Question 1: I'm pretty confident the corner bass traps in my plan drawing are a good idea. Is 4" sufficient, or should I go 6"? How about more? Perhaps another one in the center of the rear wall where it meets the ceiling?
As Ethan always says: it is impossible to have too much bass trapping in a small room. In your case, with a square room, maybe Superchunks in all for vertical corners, floor to ceiling, would be a good place to start. Think BIG!
Question 3: I feel the left and right walls are probably the main source of my flutter echoes (right?) Not too sure about that! "Seems to me like parallel walls are the disease, absorption is a band-aid,". Absorption seems to tbe the cure I always see touted for flutter echo. "diffusion a possible cure". Diffusion is a cure, but for a different problem, AFAIK! Andre once explained to me why diffusion is a bad idea in small rooms anyway, and the general consensus here is that small rooms need absorption more than diffusion, (except on the floor). Maybe Andre can offer you advice in that area.
Question 5: I've heard carpet is bad: as it only blocks certain frequencies, makes the room sound too dead, etc.
Yep, carpet is bad. It is selective, and will give your room a dull "cavey" sound.
How about a couple sheets of plywood on the floor?
Sounds like a start. But just one or two sheets, and behind your mix position? I on't think that's enough. I think I'd go for the entire floor! And laminate flooring would be better than plywood. Plus just laying plywood on top of your carpet might not be a good idea, acoustically, as it will leave an air gap in between, and that might do strange things. I think I'd be inclined to remove the carpet entirely. Once again, Andre would be the one to comment on that issue.
Question 6: The ceiling is currently bare. I think its slope helps, but I'm guessing I should put some absorption up there, right? Where, and how much? Given the slope of the ceiling, would putting alternating patches of my foam squares up there be sufficient, or do I need a cloud of 703? Is there a particular spot on the ceiling I should focus on?
Search this forum for "cloud", and I think you'll find about 50 different ideas to pursue! Basically, the spot you need to focus on first is right above your mix position, as well as all first-reflection points. You need absorption in both of those places. Search the forum for "first reflection point", to find out how to treat that. It seems that you have your room acoustically "upside down": reflective ceiling, and somewhat absorptive floor! You could try doing your mixes standing on your head! :)
I use near field monitors, with the centers of the monitors spaced 4' apart, and each speaker about arm's length away from my head. The tweeters are about ear height while seated in the chair. My head is located about 66" from the front wall.
That might be another issue: You should aim to have your head roughly 38% of the room length back from the front wall, which is roughly where you are right now, but 38% is not a magic number: just a really good starting point. Try moving your head! You also should try to get your speakers and your head at the corners of an equilateral triangle, so they will be at 60 degree angles. If your speakers are 4 feet apart, and at arm's length from your head, then that probably isn't equilateral (unless your arms are 4 feet long?) And most important of all, you need to position the speakers correctly in relation to that front wall. That might be a large part of your problem. You might have them in a location where the bass interaction with the front wall is doing all kinds of funny things to the low end. I know I have a paper somewhere on positioning, but if I recall correctly, you need to either get them smack bang up against the wall, or at a distance of a couple of meters away, such that the first wavelength where there are comb filtering issues is at a low enough frequency that it doesn't matter. I'll see if I can find that paper. Worst case, you might want to consider soffit-mounting your speakers. But that might be outside your budget, and isn't easy to do right anyway. It sure would tighten up your bass, though!

You might not need 705. I read an excellent explanation by either Ethan or Andre about why 703 can do pretty much the same job. Even plain old fiberglass or mineral wool piled up in the corners will make great bass traps. In your case, you might need to cover at least some of it with plastic, to get back your highs, but I'd leave that until you have the bass traps in place.
but I can post it, and/or pics of the studio if requested.
Photos would be good! As would an accurate SketchUp model. In summary, I'd say that your priorities should be to make your room non-square and a good ratio, followed by huge bass traps, followed by a cloud and treatment at first reflection points, followed by getting rid of the carpet and getting wood on your floor, followed by careful speaker positioning. All of those are first priority! Then you can start looking for specific issues that need individual treatment. That would be my suggestion. If it was my room, that's what I'd do. But that's just me. Maybe one of the real experts here can give you much better advice. I ain't no expert on treatment! - Stuart -

Hello Chuck.....!, the Advice you have received and the Idea's detailing your plan are indeed correct as Far as I can tell from a Room size I have worked with before though another 2 feet longer and 1 foot wider. Bass-trapping will indeed make a great difference and you really need to use a TON of this stuff, absorbing as many frequencies in the low modes as you can is going to help you to every End. I would suggest 703 and not the denser 705 (See Ethan Winer's site for 703 tests with FRK facing etc) (http://www.realtraps.com) as you want to capture the long, low notes and transfer them into energy via heat, so Corner Super-Chunking / And or a John Sayers Corner trap , (See Acoustics Section) & Stickie's will help here, I would say super chunk, air gap and large Floor to Ceiling 2 foot /120-cm traps 4 inches wide in the location you pointed to will tame things down as will a massive back wall bass trap or absorption. In this type of room situation reflection will not be of use, unless you wheel it in when recording, as for mixing you need to go dead and low, adding your ambience via electronics, as the Room size is actually working against you so you are.... going to have to live within those dimensions unless you so some serious amendments to walls etc as suggested, though I don't see that as a practical solution for you. the Cloud will help, though basically absorption is going to be your friend and not diffusion or reflection. The floor Idea may also help as you will need that upside/down room (Ceiling to Floor) reconfigured, as this will help you translate better, you said you have moved position though in rooms like yours the 38% rule is not a rule to be set in stone and used as such, (See Wes Lachot's 38% Mix room placement) on this site, as their is much debate about it, I would say you need to find a position neither in a major Peak or Null (there will be many around the room though some golden places will be better then others, so find those by moving your chair & speakers around - test for what feels best, and then do the actual tests with an Omni Mic and program if you require hard evidence, though for a room like yours mixing in feel and in knowing what the rooms limitations as far as mixing is concerned - this will help you go a long way to making things better. - by no means is your room size the worst known, though it's a toughie...! You also might want to block those windows - see the forum on that. I do hope to see some pics soon or hear if you have been successful with the plan you devised and advice offered.
