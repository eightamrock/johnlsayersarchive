knightfly27 September 2005#204
The steel you're referring to is just called Angle; older term was Angle Iron, but it's not iron any more, steel is an alloy. Least size angle that might be effective would be 3x3, 3/8" thick; that weighs about 3 TIMES what a Doug Fir 2x8 weighs, and wouldn't be noticeably stronger - a lot of bending resistance comes from DEPTH of the piece. For a simple example, support a 2x6 across two blocks off the ground, with the 2x6 held on EDGE; try standing on it (sober :wink: ) and see the deflection. Now, lay the 2x6 on its SIDE and repeat ('nuff said) A 12' length of 3x3x.375" angle would weigh about 94 pounds, and steel has tripled in price in the last two years to around $1 a pound - so each rafter's sister, even based on ONE side only would cost around $200 - this doesn't include the collar, or finding someone to weld it that won't burn your building down. Yes, tubing is stronger in some ways - again, cross-section counts. if you were to take the amount of steel in that 3x3x3/8" angle and form a hollow 2x6 with it, placed on EDGE it would have about TWICE the support capability. But it would still cost about 10 TIMES as much as doing this beef-up with Doug Fir. Insulation - I doubt HD will give you a break on any quantity you would be able to use. And for anything EXCEPT acoustic treatment INSIDE the room, the "fluffy stuff" will get you within 1-2 dB of the high price stuff; for walls, you need complete fill + slight compression. For ceilings, you need either complete fill or, if there's room, at least a FOOT. Under NO circumstances do you want to compress insulation FIRMLY, with the possible exception of a wooden floated floor. I'm still trying to get caught up on more than a year of neglect on my own life before bad weather hits, so I've still not had time to "suss out" all the ramifications of unvented space; If, in all your postings you've included a labeled sketch of your existing roof layers, please point me to it and I'll try to finally clear this up - I also need to know for sure whether you'll be heating or cooling the space more of the time. Read this through before answering, and try not to miss any points I need answers to; hopefully I can get to it in the next few days... Steve

Mike, don't shoot me for asking this question. It's a legitimate question, even if the answer shows that the option is completely impractical. For the answer to this question becomes the extreme upper limit of what you should consider doing. What if you had that entire garage of yours bulldozed and removed, and you had the freedom to construct from the ground up? How much would that cost? What benefits would you realize? I can think of several benefits:
  • It would be built to today's code, which is much more structurally sound than the current building, particularly from a seismic point of view.
  • Ceiling height would no longer be an issue -- "sky's the limit."
  • You'd have no choice but to do it "by the book," and it would be easier to do so because you wouldn't have to worry about retrofitting.
  • Your design would no longer be hindered by "the nook" or "the original doorway thing" or anything else. You'd have a clean canvas from which to work.
Another option would be to reclaim your garage as a garage (which, I realize, means redoing or undoing much of the work that's already been done in that 4' extension), and entertain the possibility of taking advantage of all that land behind the garage. (I don't think Mike has mentioned it, but he's on an especially deep lot.) I know that you've talked about this with me/Dan in the past as sort of the "ultimate" and possibly future plan -- what I'm suggesting you think about is doing the "ultimate" now. This may be way off-base, I realize... But I think it's worth at least looking into. Find out what the costs would be. Do the math. Maybe, just maybe, you'll discover that it is more expensive but the value of the investment makes up for it. Consider resale in your equation. The worst that can happen is you'll do the research and the math and conclude that it's too expensive to execute. Then at least you'll know and it won't be hanging over your head all the while. Just think... No beef-up effort. No retrofitted collar ties. No worrying about unvented attics. No worrying about "nooks." No vacillating over "to pull permits or not to pull permits" (because you will pull them). Think about it. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward8 October 2005#224
Whether or not you place the unit in the attic isn't the issue. (I would imagine that in the case of the unit for the attic, it wouln't matter much, if at all.) The fact that you need to ventilate the attic itself is. You don't have to worry about air quality or comfort up there. The advantage of HRV is that it saves energy by warming the incoming air when it's cool outside, and warming the outgoing air when it's hot outside (thereby, in theory, cooling the incoming air somewhat). These are things that you care about for as far as habitable spaces are concerned. Bottom line, you don't care to make the air in the attic comfortable. What you do want is a flow of air to prevent moisture build-up. The cheaper unit should be able to do that for you. --Keith :mrgreen:
