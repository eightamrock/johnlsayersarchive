knightfly23 November 2005#260
OK, finally stole enough time to go thru this thread from the start; also re-did calculations for your existing rafters, and must have used "ceiling joist" instead on the first go-around. According to the span calc, 2x6 #2 douglas fir will span 11'11" as a rafter, when placed on 24" centers and loaded 20 live/10# dead load. Bottom line is, no sistering needed with what is ALREADY on the roof (2 layers of 3-tab/felt, plus 5/8 CDX ply) - IOW, you're good to go on the outer roof if you don't add ANYTHING to it. Your inner, vaulted design seemed to be OK as is. For inner walls, with vertical frames you don't need 2x6 - 2x4 is fine for 3 layers. Bed your frame's sole plates with either 2 layers of 30# building felt, or Sill Seal for a thermal break, and I would use Pressure Treated for the sole plates - normal 2x4's for the studs and caps. For insulation inside walls and ceiling, use plain fiberglass with NO BACKING - no paper, no foil, NADA. For your inner stud frames, get some fiberglass strapping and run it at least every 24" horizontally across the NON-wallboard edges of your studs, then insulate and drywall. This will leave an air gap between frames, but still keep the insulation against the wallboard for damping. Better for breathability of walls. If code demands a fire stop between frames, use rockwool ONLY where the firestop is needed (at the top of the wall) - this will allow you to maintain physical separation of frames (no HARD contact) and still pass code (hopefully this is also true in over-legislated calif.) Continue your outer leaf at the eaves by filling in between rafters with those beveled 2x6's we talked about, caulk well, and you're set. I would get a decent humidity meter or two, you can safe a few $ buying two of these http://www.natlallergy.com/allergy_reli ... meter.html I just ordered two MORE, for a total of four; trying to control stuff more these days (allergies, mold, etc) - the two I already have both read exactly the same in the same conditions, which might not mean they're accurate but at least they tell the same lies :? - Reason I'm going on about the humidity is that you should keep yours below 40% year 'round, so even if your climate/construction isn't perfect the low humidity will keep things from staying too damp. You're probably OK in summer, but maybe not in winter. Those particular gauges also store min/max readings, so you can see just how high/low the humidity got since last time you checked. BTW, humidity isn't necessarily constant within a room; it's called RELATIVE humidity, because the percentage is RELATIVE to the TOTAL moisture the air can hold; at cooler temps, the air can hold LESS moisture so, for example, in winter the humidity near walls would be higher (cooler temp) than near the center of the room; so for best mold control, the sensor should be near an outside wall. Any acoustic instruments will also thank you for doing the humidity thing; pianos, guitars, fiddles, etc, all like 40-42% humidity. Hopefully I covered any remaining questions - if not, :cry:
