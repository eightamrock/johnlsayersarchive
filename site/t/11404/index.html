The way I see it, the baffle doesn't count as a leaf really, since it is part of the speaker, acoustically speaking. The baffles (soffit front surface) is just an extension of the speaker baffle itself, and acts like an infinite baffle. But if you have something solid on the front wall between the speakers, then that isn't really part of the speaker, acoustically, as far as I can figure out, so I would think that it would be a third leaf. Remember, a leaf doesn't have to be sealed airtight in order to act like a leaf. It just has to be close to another leaf, with an air gap between. so if you have a solid panel there, then maybe you could consider changing that to cloth?
unless the proximity of the center board between the speakers to the front control room wall is the issue. It is approximately one foot away from the control room wall.
That would be my concern, yes. At one foot away it might not be an issue. depending on the mass/material/density, it also might not be an issue. But you should do the math to figure it out, or ask one of the experts what they think. I may be wrong (hopefully I am wrong on this!), but it might be a problem.
As far as the speakers being in the center of the baffle. I was not aware they had to be exactly centered. My understanding was that the flush mount just allowed me to extend the baffle.
Yeah, you really need to have as much surface around each side of the speaker as possible, or you won't really have an infinite baffle. I don't recall the figures right now, but I think the surface is supposed to be at least a quarter wavelength of the lowest frequency that the speaker can produce, with the speaker in the middle. So if your speaker goes down to 65 Hz, then the soffit should be 52 inches wide, or 26" on each side of the speaker. I think that's it, but I cant find my reference for it right now. Of course, you have to live with the space you have available, so if you can't make it that wide, then just get it as big as you can and understand that there will be some roll-off on the low end and maybe other consequences, but even so it still beats leaving the thing on a stand! --------- EDITED TO ADD: I have since learned that the speakers should NOT be in the middle of the soffits, but rather offset to one side or the other, in order to avoid creating peaks and nulls in the frequency response. It seems that a position about 2/5 of the width is good. They should also not be centered vertically, for the same reason. - Stuart - ----------

Then I have some good news for you! 60° is not written in stone! If you have to angle the speakers tighter to make the geometry work out, then you can go up to as much as a 90° intersection angle, if you really need to! What you will be sacrificing is kind of "squashing" your sweet spot a bit, and your stereo image will be a bit wider than it should be, but that's a small price to pay in order to get the speakers positioned right and aimed right. It's a trade off that is well worthwhile. The 38% number is also not written in stone: It's just a starting point. In fact, most engineers end up sitting a little closer to the front wall than that, just becuase it sounds better there. In any event, the 38% point is the position of your EARS, and not the position of the intersection point! The speaker axes will intersect about 25cm BEHIND that point, since your head is round, not flat, and your ears stick out to the sides... Think about it, or draw a rough sketch on a piece of paper, and you'll see what I mean. The 38% point is at your ears, and the intersection of the speaker axes is quite a few inches behind your head, since the axes graze through your ears, not through the center of your head. And like I said, none of that is absolute. It is just ideal starting points. You can change it as needed, just recognizing that you might be sacrificing something in order to get something else.

Well, Andre and I did a lot of research in this a while back (he did most of the research, I just read what he found), and we came to the conclusion that there are several different "maximum angle" recommendations, ranging from 7 degrees to 20 degrees. But NEVER beyond 20. There are multiple reasons for those different angles. The 20° limit is due to reflections and comb filtering coming off the desk, and the 7° limit is due the way the hum ear works to figure out directions and frequencies. The best recommendation seems to be the 10° limit, which comes from the EBU. If you go beyond 10°, you start messing with the way your ear perceives sound, and you also start getting comb filtering effects and other artifacts from reflections. So I really wouldn't go beyond 10° So, there might be a way you ca re-work the geometry to still get those windows in and still have no more than 10°. Perhaps you can fiddle things around to do that. But if not, then I'd chose acoustics over aesthetics any day, for a studio. If you can't get the speakers decently positioned, then you won't have a good studio, period. But if you can't see some places, that can be solved with video cameras and flat screens. Not so elegant, but it works. I'm not going to tell you what to do, but if that were my room and I couldn't make the geometry work without losing the windows, then I think I'd yell and scream a bit in frustration, bang my head on the wall, then bight the bullet and replace the windows with video links, in order to get my speakers in the best possible location. To my way of thinking, the entire room should be designed around the speakers, since the speakers are the whole point of having a studio! All the rest is just "fluff". The speakers are what matter most. That's what I'd do. But I'm hoping you can find a way to fiddle the geometry, since those windows do look cool! Here's a real curve ball for ya: Have you considered mounting the speakers IN the glass? I mean, using the glass itself as part of the infinite baffle? xSpace5 January 2010#23
Joe, In order to get the best performance out of a mass/spring/mass wall assembly the spring has to be fully enclosed to do it's job. That enclosure is provided by the >unbroken< wall partitions that actually create the air cavity that the spring will exist inside of. Your wall lines have breaks all over, therefor you will not have an effective high transmission loss wall assembly which is what you are wanting to do. Ideally you will re-draw your design to utilize the exterior of the basement to the best you can to maintain your mass/spring/mass. My hack paint re-touch only show the areas that I see that are in question. I have seen people draw and then build such things that have potentially a broken air cavity but could get away with this if the doors or what-have you remain closed during rehearsals or tracking, etc. It isn't ideal, but it works for them. But your walls are simply not sealed in so many areas as to make all the work and expense of little importance based on the potential of what you can actually gain with properly constructed wall lines and air tight wall assemblies.
