Soundman202024 October 2009#9
Depending on what you want to do (which you didn't really say much about), there are probably many ways that your design could be improved. The equipment list doesn't really mean much at all here, and won't have any real impact on how your studio turns out acoustically. Except maybe for your speakers, which I didn't even see on the list. The guitars, amps and mics that you have will not have any effect at all on the acoustics of your room. And if you ask me, I'll prefer to record with mediocre mics on a lousy console but in a great room, over great mics and fantastic console, but in a lousy room, any day of the week! If you have a lousy room, then no amount of equipment n the world is going to make your recordings sound good. Put a U47 on an SSL and try to record vocals inside a concrete pipe.... ? Never going to work. But give me a cheap Behringer mic on a lousy Roxy console at Abbey Road, .... Hmmmmm... It think that will work a bit better! My point is that your equipment list has no bearing at all on your room design. Posting your equipment list here is pointless. It's also way too early to be worrying about redesigning slot resonators for corners and bass traps for walls, I think. Like xSpace said (in his own way!), it's not a good idea to try playing around with acoustic treatment if you don0t understand the underlying concepts, and just think it might sound good. If you haven't seen your ideas for treatment used on any successful studios, and you can't find any references to anyone ever having used your way successfully in a prestigious studio, then that should be a major red flag already! There most likely is a very good reason why nobody else did it. Probably because it doesn't work well. Just like I might think it is a great idea to design a car made of paper that has seven wheels and the seats facing sideways, but when I look around I don't see anything like that at all on the streets, I should start suspecting that I'm probably not smarter than all the guys who have designed cars over the past several decades. So if none of them built one like that, then I should probably suspect that it just isn't a good design for a car. If it was, then someone most likely would have built one like that already. So if I wanted to design a car, I think I'd look around at existing successful car designs, and base my design on those. Maybe a seven-wheeled, side-seated paper car really would be a wonderful vehicle, but I'd be all on my own trying to make it work. So if you want to design your studio around untried principles that you think might work, then there's no reason why you shouldn't do that (and it might even work out great), but you do have to realize that you'll be on your own. You could make your room pyramid-shaped, put spherical bass trapps in the center of the room, speakers in the ceiling and slot resonators on the floor, and maybe that would work fantastic. But you'd have to figure out all the new acoustic theory for that yourself, and do your own experiments on your own lab, because nobody build studios like that. So there is no expertise and no research in that area. OK, so I'm exaggerating a lot, compared with what you are talking about, but you get the point. So from my point of view, unless you are an ace acoustic designer already, it is far better to stick close to designs that other people have already worked through theoretically and practically, people who do understand the theory, and rooms they actually have built and found to be successful. It makes sense to do what the experts are doing, since they know what they are doing, and don't have to guess. The rest of us who don't know too much can be sure that our rooms will work if we just get a good solid understanding of the basics, and stick to proven design concepts. Trying out stuff without understanding the theory, in the hope that it might work, doesn't seem like an effective way of building a studio. So from my point of view: bass traps go in corners, slot resonators go on the walls, speakers get flush mounted in infinite baffles at 60° and 1.2m, ceilings and walls splay away from speakers at 12°, mix positions go roughly at the 38% mark, ceilings are soft, floors are hard, and first reflection points get absorbers. Why? Because that works! Because the theory behind it all is relatively well understood, stacks of published research on it is out there, and the concepts are not too hard for the average non-acoustician home-studio builder to get his head around. But that's just my opinion. Others might disagree. - Stuart -

Soundman202024 October 2009#12
Thanks, and yea, the acoustic treatment that are in there are just thrown in on the design.
OK. So I'd suggest that maybe it would be an idea to leave out that stuff, and re-design the interior more along proven styles that are known to work. Yes, you really should wait until the room is finished to actually measure it before deciding on the final details of the treatment, but there are many rules-of-thumb that you can already include in the basic design. Things that you DON'T need to wait for until the room is built. This would be my approach to re-designing your studio: For example, you mentioned your doubts about floors and ceilings... General rules of thumb: 1) hard floor, soft ceiling. 2) High ceilings are good, low ceilings are bad. 3) "Acoustic" tile isn't. 4) Isolation between rooms is important. 5) MSM ceiling construction is a good way to get isolation at low cost. So that would imply ripping out as much as you possibly can of the existing ceiling until all that you have is bare roof above you, to get as much height as you can. Then build a new MSM ceiling at least 4" below the existing roof. And since you want good isolation between rooms, that new "inner-leaf" MSM ceiling will have to rest on new matching inner-leaf MSM walls that are totally decoupled form the existing outer-leaf walls. To maximize height while also optimizing acoustics, that new ceiling could be built inside out, sloped at a 12° angle, and filled with wool. There you have your high, soft, acoustic, and isolating MSM ceiling. Or you could make it straight (not sloped) for even more height, and hang clouds under it. That too would work. Pro's and con's either way. The floor is a no-brainer: if you have a soft ceiling, then you want a hard floor. So bare concrete, or maybe laminate-wood. Done deal. For the shape of the room, figure out the best possible room ratio that you can get within the dimensions of the space available to you, and what you actually plan to do with the studio. Now figure out what speakers you will be using for that shape/size of space and that purpose. Now figure out the correct room geometry for those speakers in that space, to get a wide, clean, symmetrical RFZ at your mix position with tight bass and perfect stereo imaging. That will dictate the angles and locations of walls, doors windows, etc. (Or at least, it will dictate where some things can and cannot go.). Now consider other major systems, like HVAC and electrics. You need cool fresh air coming in, warm stale air going out, lighting, power to run it all, ducts for all of this, insulation to retain isolation.... Now consider your budget and building skills. Putting lots of doors and windows in a studio might look cool aesthetically and architecturally, but it is going to cost you a fortune, is going to be really hard to implement, and is going to be an acoustic nightmare. Acoustic doors are thick, heavy, massive, double-and-triple sealed monsters: = expensive. Windows are thick, heavy, massive, laminate glass, double-sealed monsters: = expensive. Glass is reflective, acoustically, and you don't want any more reflections than you already have in a small room. Doors take up space, and usually the only place you can put them is the best place for treatment. So, Like xSpace said, minimize the glass and doors, unless you have huge bundles of dough to splash out on it, and stacks more to spend on treating the acoustic issues that they will create. Do you see where I'm going with this? There is a method that you can use, based on proven principles, that will practically get the room, to design itself. Not much experimentation or wondering "will that work?" is needed. The concepts for this method of design are well understood, and you'll find them all over this forum, as well as extensively covered in numerous good books on the subject, such as Rod's book, and the MHoA. Like I said, that would be my approach to designing and building your studio, if I were you. This isn't the ONLY way of designing a studio, of course. But it IS a way that works, and is relatively easy for those of us who aren't acoustic engineers to follow. You don't have to be an expert acoustic engineer to design a studio like this, since the expert acoustic engineers here have already figured out all the hard stuff, and laid out the basic rules for us dummies to follow. Learn the basics, do what they say, build it carefully and with understanding, and you are practically guaranteed that your studio will work. Or ignore what they say, try to do it your own way that you think maybe, possibly, could be, perhaps, might sound cool or look spiffy, and chance are it wont work. - Stuart -
