gullfo7 January 2008#6
the biggest challenge is the sides of your mix position have two different boundaries - one is very close and the other far. this will alter the way you hear things because of time delay differences in reflections, comb filtering off the near wall, and oddities in the low end response. if you could turn the mix desk and couch so the desk faces the vox booth and the couch is nearer the kitchen counter, you might be better off, then add absorption on each side and overhead, and get your monitors on stands behind the desk. you'll still be able to see both the vox and the drums.
bassman8 January 2008#7
I think that what you are seeing in larger pro studios is deceiving. The visual "middle" of the room might not be the acoustic "middle" of the room. Very often, in a larger studio, there will be much more space hidden behind false walls that hide acoustic treatment. In Masterfonics in Nashville, the back false wall in their mastering rooms is 6-8 feet away from the actual back of the room but you don't notice this unless you get a peek behind the curtain.... Being in the middle of the room is usually bad since a higher amount of room modes will culminate there, altering the bass response in a bad way. -ashley

gullfo11 January 2008#10
nothing wrong with targeting problem frequencies. but start with broadband treatment. if you target a small set of frequencies with your trapping, whats the chances you'll actually hit them? plus the treatments may need to be in other places in the room to work effectively. if you have space to spare, then build some nice deep broadband bass trapping. if you don't have floor space, use the ceiling-wall corners. build soffits around the wall-ceiling corners and make those do some work.
