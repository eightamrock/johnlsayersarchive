Hi Tim. Welcome! :) A couple of comments, no particular order:
For phase 1, I will have a poured concrete foundation wall.
And a separate poured concrete slab too? In other words, the foundation wall, a small gap, then the slab? Is that what you have in mind?
I am planning to use that wall as the first layer of mass,
Then maybe I'm misunderstanding what you mean by "foundation wall". To me, a foundation wall is just that: a low wall built as part of the foundation, on top of which you then build your actual walls. But it seems you are referring to an actual entire wall of poured concrete, floor to ceiling?
The size of the air gap can still be changed at this point if that helps,
If you are talking about the air gap in a two-leaf MSM structure, then yes it does help to increase the gap. It lowers the MSM resonant frequency, and improves isolation.
am I generally better off using fiberglass batting or wet/damp sprayed cellulose between the concrete wall and the first layer of dry wall ?
I reckon I'd go with the fiberglass batting. Especially after reading Rod's comments on the potential problems with flanking. It doesn't take much to cause a flanking path, and I don't see how you can avoid it with the sprayed cellulose, no matter how careful you are when spraying.
it is my understanding that its ok to have fiberglass batting completely fill the air space between the two mass surfaces in this scenario.
IT is more than OK! It's an excellent way of increasing your isolation. The difference between two identical walls, one with an empty cavity and the other with the cavity filled with insulation, can be as much as 16 dB. That's huge! (Partly filling the cavity will give you around 8 to 10 dB over the empty wall.)
Is it ok to do the same with wet spray cellulose, or is it better to only spray one wall surface and leave an air gap ?
I don't know how you'd be able to do that! How can you prevent it from creating flanking paths? If you were only looking for thermal isolation, then that cellulose would probably be fine. But your issue is acoustic isolation, which is a whole different ball game. And I don't see why using ordinary fiberglass or mineral wool would not be a good option for thermal insulation anyway. - Stuart - - Stuart -

rod gervais9 February 2012#5
Tim, I assume that if you install the netting properly - and it's fine enough - you might be able to maintain the air space - at which time the bridging issue would be gone - however I do not see any real added benefit from an isolation point of view with this material - although there is a huge insulation benefit - so if you want to spend the extra cash just go for it - although I don't know that in a basement you will realize enough of a energy savings to ever pay for it - but there is no advantage related to isolation. I made a factory rep meet with me on this issue once - and when they claimed that it was better than simple fiberglass insulation I made them produce the actual test results in their entirety - so not only the TL values but all of the other pages that go along with it - and imagine my surprise when I found that they use a very small wall sample - one without seams in the field (a 4'x8' sample versus the 10'x8' samples tested in IR761) which skews the results in their favor. If you examine IR761 you will find this insulation used in a number of their wall tests - no advantage...... What you do is your choice -but me - I would never throw my money away on this material for isolating walls........ Good luck with your build, Rod

Rod, thanks very much for your response. I guess I'll need to look at the cost numbers and determine whether there is sufficient thermal insulation benefit to justify the additional cost if I were to go this way. tim
Tim, just remember - the majority of the thermal loss in a basement wall is in the top couple of feel of the wall - the backfill against the wall outside is a pretty decent thermal barrier all by itself (which is why even here in New England where we have pretty cold winters - the basements in heated structures tend to remain at or slightly above 50 degrees F even if they aren't heated.) So the real question when it comes to something like this is exactly how much additional energy am I going to save using this material versus standard insulation. The difference between the 2 on a framed wall exposed to the elements is large enough that the payback (of the difference in insulation costs) in energy savings is made in a relatively short period in time. However the biggest bang for the buck there is the total seal that creates not only an efficient thermal barrier - but a complete air barrier as well - and a backfilled wall is one hell of a major air barrier. You have to look at these things very closely - have your insulation contractor provide you with a long term energy analysis - to determine whether or not like this makes sense. I have a project going on right now in a large commercial building (nothing to do with studios - just new boilers/ heat pumps/air conditioning and replacement of light fixtures for new energy efficient lighting) and the building has just 5 1/2" (so R-19) insulation in the ceilings..... we looked at beefing that up to R38 and found that the owners would never save enough energy to pay for the upgrade within the life of the equipment we were installing - so even though it would save quite a bit of energy it just doesn't make sense financially. Good luck, Rod
