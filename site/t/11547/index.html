Soundman20207 January 2009#2
Hi Nick, and welcome!
Directly behind/underneith the proposed control room there is a privately owned garage where they do rev up car engines and I'd need to be able to keep that out of the control room too.
Ouch! That sounds like your number one public enemy right there! "Engines" are loud. They might be causing the entire building structure to vibrate: What kind of engines are we talking, and how loud do they get? Car? Truck? Motorbike? If they do something like Harley Davidson bikes, fore example, then you have abundant low frequencies in that characteristic "thumping roar" of the Harley, and I don't think I'd want those things revving loudly just a coupe of meters from my studio.... Even worse if they do work on heavy diesel truck engines. Apart from actual engine noise, to me "garage" also means heavy equipment, such as compressors, pneumatic tools, hydraulic lifts, drills, winches, pressure-washers, garage door mechanisms, and various other loud noises, such as hammering, dropped tools, doors slamming . . . Before you decide on how to treat, get out your sound level meter and measure the level in that garage, while they are working on something really noisy. Ask them to be as loud as they possibly can, use every tool and piece of equipment they have all at once, and measure the level. Measure with "C" weighting and on the "slow" setting. Make several measurements in different places, and note down the highest peaks. If your meter has a "peak hold" function, use it. Now go up to your proposed new studio space, and with all the doors and windows closed, measure again while they are making their same "worst possible noise" down in the garage. Once again, take several measurements at several places, and note down all the peaks. Also, while you are up there in your proposed studio, gently touch the walls, floor, and any exposed structural beams / joists with your fingertips: Can you feel them vibrating? Put your ear up against them: Can you hear them vibrating? Once you have those SPL numbers, post them here so the acoustic experts (I ain't one of them!) can figure out what it would take to get enough isolation to make that space into a usable control room. I can tell you one thing: you are talking about lots of mass here, and I dread saying this as I always say the exact opposite to everyone, but you might even have to commit the terrible, dastardly, wicked sin of floating your floor! (Gasp! Did I actually say that? Did I really suggest that somebody might need to float their floor? I must be going nuts...). Floating a floor on the second floor of a building is complex and expensive. . . . But before going any further at all, you need those real numbers. - Stuart -

Soundman20208 January 2009#4
Do you think it might be a good idea to bring a kick and snare drum down there and see what sort of bleed there is currently between the live area and the other rooms too?
Sure! The more data you can gather, the better! In fact, why not take a good speaker, a good microphone, and a laptop with some type of spectrum analyzer software. Play pink noise loudly in your future control room, and test in several locations in each of the other future rooms. That will give you full bandwidth graphs of where your issues are. - Stuart -
Soundman20208 January 2009#5
A couple of other points, Nick. How is the structure around/underneath where you plan to build your rooms? You are going to need to add several TONS of mass to get decent isolation (even assuming that the downstairs garage only deals with model airplane engines, and perhaps much more, depending on your SPL readings). Is the structure up to that? You might want to get a structural engineer in to look at it. Tell him that you need to store hundreds of sheets of drywall up there, and dozens of bags of cement, plus a small truck! That will give him an idea of the amount of weight you plan to have up there. (OK, it's not actually THAT much! I'm exaggerating a bit, for effect. But if you just tell him "I'm going to build a studio" he might not understand the weighty issues that are involved in the kind of isolation you are going to need, plus people and equipment.) Regarding your rough plan sketch: Does that show the current layout of the rooms? OR is that the idea of how you would like to lay them out? Are there any load-bearing walls there, or walls that cannot be moved for whatever reason, or other structures that you should be telling us about? Such as columns, pillars, staircases, chimneys, ducts, bracing, etc. Anything that can't be touched and might get in the way. - Stuart -

Yeah! Pictures would be great. Like they say here: no pics means it didn't happen. Also, an accurate SketchUp model would be excellent, especially if you can show the location of the "revving-engines" and the structure of the existing building. Include all major structural members of the building, and be accurate with your measurements. I know I baulked when they first told me to do a sketchup model here of my room, and I almost quit when I saw that the learning curve is a bit steep to start with, but once you get the hang of it, it is really powerful, albeit quirky.
