cadesignr30 December 2009#3
Looks pretty good. Except for a few things. Here is what I'd do. Use all 100mm. Treat room as Control Room first. Use either Nested 100mm panels or stacked diagonals to make Superchunks, floor to ceiling with lightweight film over absorption material for reflecting Mid/Highs. . Use 100mm Panels at front, front sidewalls and ceiling for Early Reflections. Treat rear wall with either 1'x1' x100mm Patchwork Panels, or a 4'x4'' Panel centered between Corner absorbers same as front. Or use a Polycylndrical bent plywood "diffuser"(actually a "scatterer"), with patchwork absorbers between corner bass traps. Use Ceiling Corner bass traps of either diagonal panels or Superchunk style. Here is what I mean

Now for my disclaimer. I ain't no expert. But for rooms where construction is not possible, these are commonly accepted solutions for basic small room treatment. However, there are many schools of thought and alternative views. These are mine. Remember this though. Small rooms are difficult to tame in terms of room response. Furthermore, one room studios suffer the limitation of headphone monitoring while recording. But recording is as much art as it is science, and when push comes to shove, many recordings are successful even though the artists and engineers had a less than perfect environment to do so. However, the key goal here is TRANSLATION. Once you treat your room whereby you are satisfied that the recordings "translate" to a variety of systems and environments, then your room is doing its job. This means you get to the point where you TRUST what your monitors/room tell you. This takes time and effort to test your recordings as I mentioned. Anyway, good luck with your room.

cadesignr31 December 2009#5
When you say "with lightweight film over absorption material for reflecting Mid/Highs." Im assuming thats just the common burlap etc that everyone uses for their panels
No, the fabric is another thing entirely. What I'm refering to is a 2 to 4mil thick plastic film, like painters use on floors to protect them. Or landscaping films. Usually comes in rolls, or folded sheets. You staple it over the insulation prior to fastening the fabric. Used like this, LF easily transmitts within the insulation, while mid/high frequencies get reflected. This keeps the room from getting too dead Try it before fastening fabric. Let your ears tell you when it sounds good in the room. However, I wouldn't use it on the sidewall and ceiling units. Those are for absorbing early reflections, which are basically mid/highs, as LF modes terminate in corners.
