"I try to be "design technique agnostic" but it's safe to say I'm pretty much sold on 2-leaf MSM, Green Glue and the other myriad techniques and materials put forward on this site." It is like that for a reason, because it works. So you made the proper adjustment. The only thing I can add about square or rectangle would be this: If you build a square building you can still install a working recording studio. But if you have goals like a bathroom, kitchen or other extras, then rectangular may be better, depending on the footprint of the structure and how it fits into the environment. If it comes down to one thing, then you may want to design a sketch of what it is you think you will require as far as control room/tracking/machine room/iso booths, etc and then see how that fits into either a square or rectangular building. I think in the end, that will give you the answer all though someone else may come along and prove this incorrect. But I doubt it ;)

symmetry will work, but sn0t necessary for an LR. And if you do make it symmetrical, then stay away from parallel walls, and also from ceiling parallel to floor, just to make your life a bit easier!
Re. ambient noise, integrated average measurements at ground zero, 5:45 PM, C weighting, fast sample rate, pointed at the noisiest direction SSW: Minimum 56 db, average 62 db, max 70 db
That sounds about right! (By the way, the direction you point the mic shouldn't have a lot of effect on the numbers. The mic is supposed to be omnidirectional). So now you have basic ambient noise figures. Do you need to add anything to that (apart from the airport)? For example, are you close to a railway line? Or a hospital (ambulances and med-evac helicopters make a lot of noise)? Or a fire station (ditto fire engines)? Noisy neighbors that like to party loudly on Friday night? Or any other possible intermittent sound source that might not have been active when you measured? Any of those might affect your numbers. You might even want to consider things like heavy rainfall, or thunder storms, if they happen to be common events in your area, and might trash that one-and-only once-in-a-a-lifetime perfect take... Just some things that might be worth thinking about. I wouldn't sweat those numbers too much at this early stage, but it is something that you need to keep in mind. You also need to think about how quite you want it it to be inside, usually represented by choosing one of the noise curves, and designing with that in mind. This includes not just blocking outside noise coming in, or inside noise going out, but also noise going between rooms. Right now, I reckon your best bet would be to throw together some rough layouts of how you think you might want your rooms organized. If nothing comes to mind, then take a look at some of the many designs John has done: maybe one of those will strike you as being close to what you need, so you can use that as a basis, then modify and refine it as time goes on. Then post your basic plans here for comments, and with specific questions. So far, you sure seem to be doing everything right! - Stuart -

xSpace31 January 2010#13
"You know how with splays you can take the average (measured at the center point) as the ratio number. " Ratios apply to rectangular boundaries. Averaging may have been submitted as a method to keep in the parameters of a ratio, but it really doesn't apply. Once you splay your walls, properly, the need for ratios as a strict boundary guide are no longer required. As a reference to develop a well dimensioned room, sure, it's a better starting place then guessing. But when splayed walls are introduced, then the ratios do not matter. The reason is that you are adding simple diffusion and by virtue of the splayed walls are reducing standing waves, which are by and large the reason good room ratios were looked into in the first place...I think :) Anyway, start with a good room ratio to obtain your room size then splay the walls as they should be and your room will need less treatment and sound more even and better overall. As to getting the maximum air space you can, get it all. But there is some support that once you actually hit a 12 inch cavity that this is as far as will be helpful. I think the document I am thinking about was using sheetrock, so this applies to your build, essentially. Certainly everything matters. Like if you had a double leaf with each leaf being 24 inch concrete, then a 3 foot (36") air space might improve the isolation of the assembly. edit: a few typos.
