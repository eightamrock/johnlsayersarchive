kendale22 March 2006#8
Aloha mika, Andre is right. With a 6.5" woofer, your monitors are not producing a significant amount @ 50Hz. Not that it isn't at all, but it's just not a significant amount to fill your room at that freq. What you are probably hearing at the sofa location is a buildup of 77Hz, 116Hz and 155Hz. (see attached. Note green outlined area.) What you could try is moving your sofa out to where the listening position is about 11’ at your ears. If this sofa position interferes with your door, you could try either a smaller sofa or two chairs, or possibly reorienting your door to open out. Next, for the most non-invasive test, try repositioning a couple of your wall panels across the two rear corners of the room – floor to ceiling. You will most likely hear an improvement in the low end absorption at the rear of the room, similar to what you hear up front. If that works for you, you might want to finish treating the back wall with a couple of floor to ceiling corner absorbers like those found here: http://www.johnlsayers.com/HR/index1.htm If you need more low freq’s @ 50Hz and below, try adding a sub after the above suggestions.

So what are you planning to cover the front portion of the wall with? Cloth or more wood slats? And the area around the monitor face? Here’s an excerpt from the “Speaking of speakers section:
http://johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=718 Here is a method for a flush mounting that I developed a couple of years ago. It provides for a simple modification method when upgrading your monitors. It also helps isolate the speaker cabinet vibrations from the control room. The picture below depicts the basic design. The front bezel is clear in the picture to help visualize the whole setup. Here is an outline of the method: 1. Create an opening that is large enough to accommodate your monitor or any monitors you might potentially use in the future, plus some extra room for sound absorption material. Building a larger space than what you presently need gives you the flexibility to upgrade. Avoid building any structures that directly couple the inner and outer framing. 2. Rigidly mount an adjustable shelf to the inner framing. Mounting to the inner framing isolates the cabinet vibrations from the control room. I've depicted small wooden rails attached to the shelf to maintain the monitor's lateral position. A belt clamp thens hold the monitor firmly to the shelf. The face of the monitor should be flush with the front wall. 3. Screw mount a rigid bezel to the outer framing. The bezel should be flush with both the front wall and the front of the speaker. The more massive and rigid the bezel the better. The opening should not contact the monitor cabinet, but the gap should be no more than 5mm (3/16 inch). You might have the idea of filling the gap with some sort of gasket, but this is both unnecessary and detrimental. Any gasket will increase the transfer of vibrations to the bezel, therefore, effectively increasing the cabinet radiation surface area. Gaps less than a half wavelength will have little effect on wave propagation. 5mm is less than 1/3 wavelength at 20kHz, so audible diffraction induced by the gap isn't an issue. In any case most tweeters radiate very little energy 90° off axis at 20kHz. When you upgrade your monitors all you need to do is remove the bezel and cut a larger size opening, readjust the shelf, then mount the new speaker and screw the bezel back in. 4. Fill the extra space in the mounting cavity with sound absorbing material like fiberglass. This will help absorb sound radiated from the monitor cabinet. You might have noticed that a big part of this mounting method centers around isolating the monitor cabinet energy from the control room. Loudspeaker cabinets are almost invariably under-constructed and can radiate a significant amount to energy, resulting in coloration. If you follow these design principles, I think you'll hear a noticeable difference from a simple flush mount.
Also, be sure to provide adequate cooling for your monitors as this may pose an issue with overheating/shutdown/fire hazard. Final question: Is that a radiator pictured on your back wall below the curtains? Be careful of any fire hazards that it might pose. Hope this helps, Aloha 8)

Aloha mika, Yes, it is important to feel at home in the studio. Is it as important as "making the bass consistent everywhere in the room?" It's your choice. Most of the guys here are trying to help each other achieve the best results possible by the advice/questions offered. After all's been said and done, it pretty much comes down to what do YOU feel is more important for YOUR studio.
A way to fight the axial waves in the back is maybee to build aborbers and hang it cross the backwall and the celling. Do you think it can give the same effekt like absorbers cross the corner?
In order to absorb the low frequencies your room is producing, I doubt you'll have the same affect as corner absorbers across the corners using the kind of panel absorbers you have pictured. To optimize your room at both the mix and sofa positions, you need to position (or reposition) yourself in the optimum listening positions (avoiding peaks and nulls) and treating the room with the proper absorbers in the right places and in the right amounts. Anything else would be a compromise, which usually results in less than optimum results. Oftentimes, compromises are unavoidable. I am currently in a compromised situation. And while the advice and acoustic treatments that have been done have vastly improved my mix position, there are still areas in my room that fall short of what I would have hoped for. Had I been/when I am able to build from the ground up, I would definitely make the choices to make the changes necessary in order to optimize the whole room. Ultimately, the choice is yours. Choose wisely, my friend. :wink: Aloha 8)
