The BEST place to put them is flush mounted (sometimes also called "soffit mounted") diagonally in the front corners of the room, at an angle of between 30° and 45°, such that the acoustic axes intersect at an angle of between 60° and 90° a few inches behind your head, with your ears located roughly 38% of the room depth (distance from front wall to rear wall). They should be mounted with the acoustic axis at a height of 1.2m above the floor.

Soundman20202 November 2011#6
Actually, it won't "cause" reverb! How could it? Reverb is a function of surface reflectivity and room size/shape. In fact, angling the ceiling (or a wall) helps to break up some forms of "reverb", such as standing waves (if you want to call those "reverb"). So I'm not sure where you read that angling the ceiling will "cause reverb", but wherever that was, you can probably safely ignore it in the future. Reverb depends on multiple reflections from hard surfaces. There are several reasons why you might want to angle your ceiling. The two most common are to eliminate flutter echo (which is a form of reverb too, if you want to stretch the term a bit), and to help create an RFZ design. A third possibility is to change the modal behavior of the room (yet another form of reverb!) But angling at 6° wont accomplish any of those. For flutter echo, you need an angle of at least 12°, apparently, from empirical evidence (there doesn't seem to be much research on this point). Creating an RFZ will most likely require a rather larger angle, but that depends on the geometry of the rest of the room. And messing with modal response? Put it this way: if you have a rectangular room, it is easy to predict the theoretical modal response of the room. If you angle one or more walls (floor, ceiling), it becomes very hard to predict. So if you are like some people who swear by "room ratios" and "golden dimensions" and elves and fairies, then you would probably want to keep the room perfectly rectangular with an exact 10 foot ceiling (according to some), and base your other dimensions on one of the holy grail ratios. Or if you don't put too much store in ratios, and prefer to just stay away from the bad ones and approximate a good one, then by all means angle the ceiling as much as you need to, and forget about room modes. Room modes are a fact of life: you cannot get rid of them, and all rooms have them. You can change their spread by changing room dimensions and shape, sure. But you cannot make them go away: all you can do is try to tame them a bit, with treatment in the room. And since theory is one thing while reality is another, it makes sense to actually build the room and test the the actual modal response before deciding on the best treatment for the modes you are actually having trouble with, rather than finding out that the ones you predicted would be worse are not actually an issue, while others are. So that's a long way of saying: angle your ceiling if you want, and if you understand why, but 6° isn't enough: it should be at least 12°
I also read that it depends on my back wall.
The relationship between ceiling angle and rear wall is very unlikely to cause issues. I'd be far more concerned about speaker reflections off your FRONT wall and two side walls, which is why you need absorption on your first reflection points.
If the back wall is a difuser, they I will def have more reverb which is not so good for recording. Can anyone confirm this
Nope! Not me! I wont' confirm that. In fact, I'll deny it! :) Diffusers BREAK UP reverb! They do not CONTRIBUTE to reverb. They help to create a more diffuse resonant sound field, which is definitely a good thing. Hard surfaces produce specular reflections. Diffusers produce, well, emmm, "diffuse" reflections. It seems you have been reading in the wrong places, if that's the kind of information you are finding. Do yourself a huge favor, and read the Bible. Not THE Bible written by God (although that one is highly recommended too), but rather the Bible of Acoustics, which is correctly called "Master Handbook of Acoustics", and was written by F. Alton Everest. It teaches you all the basics of acoustics, in simple, easy to understand terms. It talks about many of these concepts, and gives you the theory and the basic math behind them, so you can figure out for yourself what will work and what wont. And one thing that won't work, is most types of diffuser on the rear wall of a very small room, such as yours. Most types of diffuser (especially QRD based designs and similar) tend to create lobing close up, an that needs some distance to even out. Small rooms don't have enough distance. So a much better idea for your rear wall is thick acoustic absorption, such as mineral wool or fiberglass insulation. That helps for several reasons. - Stuart -

OK, let me turn those around and re-state them positively, instead of negatively: 1 ) Square rooms have problems with the even spread of room modes, but that can be changed by moving one of the walls. The ratio of height to length to width is somewhat important, and this is called your "room ratio". There are several good ratios, and several bad ratios: Stay away from the bad guys, and get close to one of the good guys. 2 ) I is best to have your speakers facing down the longest axis of the room. 3 ) The best place for the mix position (theoretically) is to have your seat and console set up such that your ears are at roughly 38% of the room depth (front wall to back wall) and 1.2m above the floor. That's not carved in stone: it's just a good starting point. 4 ) Absorption is more useful that QRD diffusers in small rooms: diffusers are more suitable for large rooms. 5 ) The corners of the room are very useful: rear vertical corners are ideal for bass traps, and the front vertical corners are ideal for speaker soffits (flush mounts) 6 ) Small rooms need lots of bass trapping. The best idea is to put "superchunk" style traps in the rear vertical corners, and if possible in several other corners. Other forms of bass trapping might also be needed, such as tuned traps of one type or another. 7 ) You have enough room to soffit-mount (flush-mount) your speakers, so you probably should do that, for all of the benefits it provides. 8 ) The acoustic axes from the speakers should meet a few inches behind your head when seated at the 38% position, sat an angle of 60° to 90°, such that the axes just graze your ears. 9 ) Try to minimize the issue of first reflections. That includes placing absorption at the first reflection points wherever possible, and choosing a desk where there will not be reflections. :) - Stuart -
