gullfo19 April 2011#2
welcome! nice first post! it is possible to build some components - soffits, slats, traps etc to fit your space and leave an air gap around the periphery. on the ceiling - it might be possible to add a layer of drywall get the mass up although it would be more useful to add some greenglue although your budget prohibits that. on the door, adding some MDF and sealing it will help. the cloud will definitely be a necessity over the desk and you might find you need a hard backed cloud angled over the mix area to get things right. the window - you might consider a small frame to sit in it and then some MDF or glass to beef it up and seal it. the glass would let in some light it that is desired plus if it's hinged, allow it to be opened. on the back wall treatments, a framed wall with access panels or doors for the plumbing should be used to isolate the noise and heat. you might consider some way to vent the heat off into another room - small flex duct / fan. plus factor all this into any need for fresh air exchange with your space. maybe a baffle box hung on the outside of the door could do it? or in the window opening?

gullfo20 April 2011#4
if you need isolation and adding the drywall layers will help, then that should be step one. the isolation wall on the plumbing side is to help with the system noise getting in, not your sounds getting out... obviously leave whatever elements you need exposed to provide the necessary heating. the hard backed cloud is a panel with an absorptive front (and/or back) which runs interference with the floor-ceiling modes. in your room, it will be helpful. my preference is generally angled slat resonators for the side wall treatments, cloth covered absorption front and back and scattered as needed to get the frequency and reverberation levels balanced. the angles help keep the front alive and reduce interfering reflections off the sides. soffit mounting is good and using John's component style to build up the necessary units will mean you can build up over time.

BriHar21 April 2011#8
The cloud remains a broadband absorber, the hard, mass backing alters the (local) floor ceiling room mode and the angling serves additionally to deflect the soundwaves away from the listening position (RFZ). Without the hard backing it is a simple broadband absorber and the angling varies the distance of the absorber to the hard ceiling thus helping to extend the frequency range (in the lower registration).

gullfo4 May 2011#14
in general, your soffits should have enough space to let them work. the baffle step effect will increase the speaker LF response about 6db. so the trade off between the rear port and the baffle step will help equalize and the venting in the soffit as well as the overall configuration of absorption in the space will not necessarily prevent the rear facing port from contributing (or detracting depending on the time involved) to the LF response. so having enough absorption in the soffit is more important than less to ensure a balanced response. the HP6B are fairly small speakers as are the BM5 so you might be able to mount them both (top to top - to match the acoustic center as closely as possible) in the soffit.
