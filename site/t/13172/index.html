1. What's meant with "mud and tape", regarding drywall assembling? Also how am I supposed to install the foam backer rod in the perimeters? Is it a bad idea to just caulk under(if facing floor) every drywall sheet instead of using foam backer rods?
How do they finish drywall in Sweden? Here in the States we apply a layer of joint compound over the joints - we then bed a material (called tape) into the wet drywall........ this is sometimes paper - sometimes a fine mesh material...... Next we seat the material into the wet compound, smooting it out and removing any excess compound. We then apply more layers of coumpound over the application until we finally reach the level of finish we desire - some light sanding and we're ready for pain - wall paper - what ever finish you want to apply. Foam backer rods are easily installed - you can push them in place using your fingers - a pencil if the joint is to small for fingers. You want then setting about 1/4" back from the face of drywall before applying caulk. I explain in the book the importance of backer rod - it stops caulk failure caused by 3 point caulk applications...... so yes it is important to do.

rod gervais15 June 2009#4
Osse, the whole intent of building seperate walls inside the building are to decouple them from the exiting frame. The are many different isolation clips you can use to make your walls rigid (utilizing the existing frame) without creating flanking paths between the old and the new. And these clips will cost you much less than RSIC clips and hat channel once all is said and done. If you do this right - the inner wall is already decoupled - how can you then gain from decoupling yourself from the already decoupled wall? This is like working for the "Department of Redundancy Department". In fact - if you frame with 2x6 @ 2' centers - you will actually have a slight advantage with low frequency without the RSIC clips. But - in the end - you will never gain by trying to isolate yourself from an already isolated member.... isolated is isolated...... Rod

Just adding to what the others said: The reason why you wont gain anything is because, if you build a proper 2-leaf room, then the second leaf is already decoupled from the first leaf. It makes no sense to decouple it again. In other words, you have to build the frame for your second leaf a couple of inches away form the brick wall, such that it does NOT touch the brick wall, nor the other walls, nor the ceiling. It only touches the floor. So it is decoupled. Adding RC or clips will not gain you anything additional, since what they do is to decouple, but your wall is already decoupled, so they don't do anything. Of course, if you were planning to build your second leaf by attaching it to the brick wall, or to the other walls, or the ceiling, then that isn't a proper 2-leaf wall anyway, so that would be a different situation. Like Javier said: if you don't build all fives sides of the room fully decoupled, then you didn't build a 2-leaf system, so you'll have coupling and flanking going on, and you won't be well isolated. - Stuart -

So what DO you need then? Without numbers, nobody can even guess at the construction that you'll need to get to that number. Especially when you say that you don't need it anyway, and that you also already have it! :) The isolation in your room will only ever be as good as the isolation through your weakest link. If your "weak link" happens to be the ceiling, or the floor, or one of the side walls, or through the windows, or the doors, then that is as good as it can possibly be, no matter how well you build your dividing wall. Sound does not travel in straight lines, so a single wall cannot stop it, no matter how thick that wall is. Sound travels as waves, and waves simply wrap around walls, especially at low frequencies. To stop waves getting in, or out, you MUST build a sealed box. There is no other way. If the box isn't sealed, sound WILL get in, or out. Simple basic physics. Think of it like an aquarium: To build a good aquarium that holds water, you need five pieces of glass: One for the floor, and four more for the walls. If you take out any one of those, then it's useless: it will not hold water at all. If you replace four pieces of glass with cloth, cardboard, newspaper and a sponge, then it won't work either: The water will still leak out. In order to work as an aquarium, it must have water tight walls on all five sides. But what you are saying you want to do with your room, basically, is that you want to build an aquarium that has only one really good thick glass wall, and with the others made from sponge, cloth, etc. The simple answer is: that won't work! If water leaks out through ANY of the walls, then it is a useless aquarium. If sound can get in or out through ANY of the six sides of your room, then it is useless. The same for your room: The isolation will only be as good as the worst wall, whichever that one happens to be. And since you haven't told us anything at all about the other walls and the ceiling and the floor, we can't even tell you how bad the isolation on those will be. How quite does it have to be outside? How loud are things around you? Consider things like your neighbors, roads nearby, airplanes flying over, trains, mechanical noise in the building, wind, rain, etc. Also consider your municipal bylaws, and the legal limits they impose on sound levels. Etc. Nobody here can help you with good advice if you don't provide this type of information. Basically, right now its like you are saying "I have a piece of rope here. I guess the perimeter walls will conduct some of the sound from the live room to the control room
Exactly. That is called "flanking". Sound will get around your divider wall, by flanking through the side walls and ceiling, and floor. If the floor is a concrete slab poured directly on the ground, then that won't be too much of a problem, but the walls and ceiling will be: No matter how good you make your divider wall, sound will ALWAYS flank around it. Unless you build a room-within-a-room, then you will not have good isolation. You cannot make the laws of physics go away just by wishing and hoping that they won't apply to your room. They will apply to your room in the exact same way that they apply to every other location in the universe. And there is another major issue here: My guess is that the space above the ceiling is shared by both rooms. If so, then your weak point is right there: sound will flank very well around your wall through that air space. And once again, you still have not given us any numbers or information about those rooms, such as how loud you will be in there, how quite it must be outside, what type of music you record, what instruments, etc, etc, etc. All of the stuff that is asked for in the most important post on this forum.
