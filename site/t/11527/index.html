Many others who are very helpful, will not reply out of respect for the moderators' wishes. Having said, that I have a comment on your plan for the floor: You said:
What I am hoping here to do in regards to the studio flooring is to elevate it with two beams as shown below and firstly put in moisture protection, followed by the sound insulation and that wooden flooring? Than carpet.
That sounds suspiciously like it will resemble a floating floor, which is not a good idea. Read this: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8173 Yes, I know your intention is not actually to float it, but your description says that you will be building a thin, low-mass floor on top of an air gap, with another floor underneath, and it will bring you the same kind of issues as a floating floor. I'd suggest you re-think that floor. Resonance is a good thing inside a kick drum, but a bad thing inside your floor...
That’s all I have in terms of ideas for my studio at the moment, hopefully others can add in some input as to what else I am going to need to do for construction.
Well, it's really hard to say anything at all about your design, since you didn't actually post a design! And it really hard to say anything about your available spaces, since you didn't actually post an accurate detailed fully dimensioned SketchUp model of your space! You also didn't provide any of the basic details regarding what you want to achieve in terms of isolation: What are the sound levels that you expect to have inside the studio, what levels you are aiming to have outside the studio once it is built, and also the other way around: What is the loudest exterior sound that you absolutely must prevent from getting into the studio, such as; do you live next to a noisy street with heavy traffic / have an airport, freeway, railroad close by / have noisy neighbors with loud lawnmowers / dogs barking / parties / garage band... Etc. You need to put actual numbers to these concepts, with a sound level meter. Measure the levels inside and outside at the noisiest times of the week, and also the quietest times of the week. Play music / pink noise in your garage as it is tight now, and slowly turn it up until your wife / family / neighbors / police tell you it is too loud, then measure the actual SPL at that point: Etc. It is impossible to design your isolation if you don't have the numbers on which to base the design. It's like saying "I need to build a road", but not knowing where the road has to start, where it has to end, or what traffic it has to carry.
width is 5.390m. with depth 5.440m & height is 2.6m.
But you say that only half of the depth is available, so your available space will measure 5.39 x 2.42 x 2.6. Chop off 20 cm (minimum) on each edge for isolation, and you are going to end up with a room that measures about 4.99 x 2.02 x 2.4. That's pretty small. You have to put equipment and people in there, not to mention HVAC. And that's just the dimensions to interior bare walls, without even considering the acoustic treatment that you will undoubtedly need. Is there no chance that you can make it a bit bigger? You didn't say, but I assume that you will have some form of isolation booth in that space, either for the equipment, or for the announcer, since you can't have equipment in the same room as the microphone. That shrinks your space by another 40 cm (at least!) in one of the dimensions, just for walls around that booth.... But once again, without seeing the layout that you have in mind and the numbers, it's impossible to say. Soundman20208 January 2009#4
The loudness around my area may reach up to around 80-100db and that is from numerous machinery and noises, at times it may even reach 120db but that is rarely with neighbors having parties...
Wow! That sounds pretty loud! Are you sure your numbers are correct? How did you measure them? 120 dB is roughly the sound level you normally find standing a few meters in front of the speaker towers at a large rock concert. Do you live next door to Pink Floyd?
I really need the studio to be isolated from the sounds of outside.
If you really do have levels of 120 dB SPL right outside, and you need to block that enough so that you can record vocals inside, then you have a problem! You are going to need some pretty massive isolation do accomplish that! I don't think anything other a couple of thick concrete walls with a large air gap is going to tame ear-splitting 120 dB party music down to a level where you can record vocals. Even if the level is only 100 dB, you still have a big job ahead of you, but killing 120 dB is going to be really tough. I think you should re-check those levels with another sound level meter, one that is correctly calibrated. Use the "C" weighting, and "slow" response.
I did plan to have all the equipment in the same room except for computers which provide the real noise problem. I will do this because I see numerous radio stations which do have equipment in the same room. I will have all network communication equipment in a server cabinet to hold UPS, music server, network connection equipment etc...
Well sure, you CAN have equipment in the same room as your mic... provided that the equipment is specifically designed to be used in that kind of situation! ie, no noisy cooling fans. It has to be totally silent. Some equipment is built like that, some isn't. Anything that makes a noise has to go in another room, or a closet, or an isolated rack. And don't forget that when you put equipment in a small enclosed space, it needs ventilation air flow (air conditioning). So you need to consider that when you design your HVAC system. - Stuart -
