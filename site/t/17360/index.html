"1. You can use area rugs...you do not have to modify the entire area just to satisfy this issue. In any event this floating floor stinks as an option, and here is why. Typically the air in an air space is made up of air and the included insulation. So if you have insulation, then you have air, which is in effect the decoupling mechanism of this assembly. Now if you add wood supports, what does that do? And then you aid another air gap? And then yet again wood supports? Why not just the compressed glass then cover that with 2 layers of plywood(dense) running one direction and the next layer running perpendicular to the first layer? Wouldn't that be easier? But, to be fair, not knowing what kind of sound issues you are up against, even the decoupled floor might not help at all. If the structure is making a lot of noise then you have to attack that since making this decoupled floor only stops some of what is happening with the floor. It does not even remotely change or offset the large glass windows, the hard reflective overhead. And having basically cubicle partitions, well, there again, that is going to suck the sizzle right out of your sound, like if you installed carpet on the walls, same type of effect. I guess in the end, I would go slow...area rugs, since all you are looking to address is the hard surface of the floor concrete.

Soundman20202 February 2012#5
Hi Matt. Welcome! :) Could you be a bit more specific by what you mean with "there's simply Too Much reflection"? Do you really mean "reflection", or are you talking more about reverberation? Or resonance? Or is it modal issues? Would you classify it as "boomy" or "honky" or "tinny", or something like that? As Brien already pointed out, your room is nearly a cube, so I strongly suspect that what you are hearing is related to modal behaviour. The Bonello diagram for your room is all over the place, and really ugly, so I'm not surprised that there are issues in there.
1. The concrete floor seems a bit too much reflection for drums - particularly overheads
Concrete is actually a great surface, acoustically, for too many reasons to go into here. It's far more likely that your ceiling and walls are giving you problems. And if your overheads are sounding to "live", then like Brien said, a common solution is a large rug on the floor under the drum kit. Yes, that only really deals with highs, but that's usually what the issue is with overheads and floors. If you do that and still have problems, then it most likely isn't the floor that's causing it.
I was thinking about constructing a floating floor of concrete->compressed fibreglass->wood supports->air gap of 8cm (I have room)->wood supports->plywood.
That sounds rather like a drum riser to me. Those are normally used to keep impact noise out of the the building structure bu isolating the kit from the floor. But it won't help much with resonance or reflections: plywood isn't much different than concrete in that respect, and you run the risk of creating yet another resonant cavity in the room, tuned to an unknown frequency and with an unknown Q.
2. I'm going to be putting 2 or 3 bass traps on the ceiling - would you recommend higher or lower freq traps in this case?
Bass traps on the ceiling would not be effective. Bass traps normally go in the corners, where they are fare more effective. That's a small room, so it would be a good idea to put some traps in at least a few of the corners, but with care: if you already like the sound of the room, you run the risk of making it too dead by adding plain bass traps. You need to design them to keep the highs in the room, and only kill the bass.
3. To further reduce overall reflection without changing the tone of the reflection significantly, what would be the best treatment for the walls?
Before deciding on that, I reckon your best bet would be to actually measure the room acoustics, with REW, and post the results here so we can see what is going on in there, and then figure out what to do about it: Most likely you'll need a good sized cloud above your kit (probably hard backed and angled), some absorption on a couple of walls, and perhaps a couple of slot walls at shallow angles on the other two, to help damp the modal issues and resonance a bit.
The issue I'm asking about - which I made clear in the OP - was simply reducing reflection across all frequencies.
:shock: That's actually a pretty tall order! For some of the frequencies you can fit hundreds of wavelengths across the room between two walls, but at the other end, you can't even fit a tiny fraction of one single wavelength! Reducing "reflection" across the entire spectrum evenly, is pretty tough, and you don't have anywhere near enough wall area to do that: Some devices would have to be dozens of square meters, others relatively small. Different parts of the spectrum behave differently: high frequencies are very specular and highly directional, lows are highly diffuse and not at all directional Long waves wrap around small objects, mids diffract at the edges, highs just bounce off. It sounds to me like you might need maybe some polys or tubes to that mix of treatment I suggested. That might help too keep the room live while still treating the modes. Anyway, start by downloading REW if you don't already have it, then run the tests and post the results. - Stuart -

Soundman20202 February 2012#7
Well, reverberation is reflection,
Actually, no it isn't, technically. Yes, people use the two terms interchangeably, but acoustically they are different. Saying reverb is the same as reflection is like saying running around the block is the same as a taking a single step... :)
If you're using a glynn-johns or recorderman setup, which I tend to do, then the balance of lows in the overheads is critical.
If you use Recorderman (my personal favorite) then your overheads are pretty low to the kit anyway, so the kit itself is blocking the mic's view of the floor and therefore most of the high reflections, so that doesn't seem to be your problem. And you also don't have any phasing or timing issues on either lows or highs in the overheads, so what you are getting is mostly clean, direct drum sound without too much "room" anyway. So if you are getting too much room reverb in your overheads with Recorderman, then you really do have a major room issue!
but I thought you were against traps on the ceiling?
A cloud is not a bass trap. I'm not against putting bass traps on the ceiling: It's just that it doesn't make much sense, acoustically, to put them on the ceiling if the corners are available. Why put it in 2-pi space if you can get the huge boost from pi or even 1/2-pi? More "bang for the buck", for free. But apart from that, I'm not talking about a bass trap: I'm talking about an angled, hard-backed ceiling cloud, which will do several things. The hard back will help (marginally) with any vertical room modes and flutter echo in the vertical plane, the angling re-directs and breaks up any standing waves that happen to involve the ceiling (axial, tangential or oblique modes that have the ceiling as one of their vertices), and the absorption damps those as well as damping first-order reflections coming back at your overheads from above. It won't do much at all for bass, since it isn't meant to be a bass trap, but will do wonders for the rest.
Or are you differentiating between hanging traps and traps flat against the ceiling.
A bass trap flat against any surface is pretty ineffective anyway! (Unless its a membrane trap, in which case that would be the best place for it). Absorption works on the velocity component, which is zero at the wall surface, so absorption directly against the walls won't do much for normal incidence sound. That's why superchunks in corners are so effective: There's pretty much NO angle where sound can be normally incident to a flat surface behind it.
I don't actually get the point of that. As I said, I'm happy with the current reverberation freq balance. It's just a matter of reducing it - across as much of the spectrum as possible - to about half of what it currently is.
That IS the entire point of it! You don't even know what your room response looks like at present in the frequency domain or the time domain. Everything you know about the room is purely subjective, but acoustics is a science that deals with objective data. What you call "too reverberant" I might consider "too dead", or vice-versa, but REW will reveal exactly what it is, objectively. How can you design treatment to treat the room evenly across the spectrum and over time, when you don't even know what the room is doing? If you don't know, objectively, what the RT times are for each area of the spectrum, how can you treat the room as a whole to keep those in the same relationship? You also seem to be confusing frequency-domain with time-domain. Your issues in that room have very little to do with the actual basic frequency response of the room itself, and very much to do with how that changes over time as the standing waves release their energy, and the reverberation dies out. That's what a REW waterfall plot will reveal.
It's just a matter of reducing it - across as much of the spectrum as possible - to about half of what it currently is.
In a room with those dimensions, you won't be able to do much at all for the part of the spectrum below 154 Hz, which is the Schroedinger frequency for your room. From 154 to 616 Hz, you stand a chance of at least affecting things to a certain extent, and above 616 Hz it should be relatively easy. But from 616 up is not where your real problem are anyway. Theoretically, you need something like 260 sabins of absorption in there to achieve ITU recommendations, but you don't want to go that far, and we have no idea how much you already have (not much!) so you need more like maybe 100 or so, and you need most of that to be installed in such a way as to not kill the highs and concentrate on the lows. Put maybe 20 of that in the cloud, 50 or 60 in the corner bass traps, and the rest on the walls, in patches. In fact, I'd put the rest all on one single wall, checkerboarded. I'd start on that basis, so how it goes, then decide on what more to do, (if any). A couple of photos of your room would help a lot to understand what you are dealing with. (And so would a REW analysis! Without REW, it's all just guess-work, based on subjective comments). - Stuart -
