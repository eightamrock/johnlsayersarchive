rod gervais20 June 2008#3
John, as far as the humidity goes - it's all relative (pun intended). NO big deal - the MDF should remain stable just like it would anywhere else in the building - it's only when it gets wet from an outside source that it becomes a problem - but not at RH inside a space. The fire issue is a seperate thing. In a lot of commercial applications fire seperation is not an issue - and it would be fine i those cases. It would not be fine where fire seperation was an issue. But - In THOSE cases - it is no big deal to construct a box with the same inside dimensions with an outside frame that is a rated assembly to work with - OR - if the seperation requirements are only one hour then you could make it a metal duct plenum - same details as the MDF chamber - line it - and then surround it with any other assembly (MDF OK in this case) just to stop the possible transfer of ambient noise in the space from making it into the duct chamber. Rod
kevicoll2 August 2008#4
I built plenum boxes of mdf lined with duct liner board. The duct liner is foil-covered, both sides. I sealed all the edges with foil tape, so I would say they are, by definition, vapor-sealed. Foil is considered a good vapor seal. And I used acoustic sealant to filet all the edges, too, which applied mass to the board and gave further stability to the seal of the corners and edges. On top of that, the edges are bound together with drywall corner bead, to keep if from further drifting apart, stitched together with my little 1-1/4 in nail gun. As long as the air is moving and there is no condensation, the moisture in the plenum should remain constant. If you made it of 3/4 in plywood, it would be meet the requirements of a one-hour fire stop. In my case, the wall cavity is enclosed with 5/8 in drywall and stuffed with fiberglass which meets the prescriptive requirements for fire-spread, in case they should ask. This box is made to stand up inside the wall cavity, the only place there is room.
