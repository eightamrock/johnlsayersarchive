If I understand this and the pictures, the square ducting runs perpendicular to the joists across the length of your basement, but there is at least one flex duct that connects to the top of it and runs parallel to and between the joists, across the basement to the room above. Correct? If so, and given your 7' 10" clearance (from the bottom of the joists I assume) with which to work, I think you'll have to do one of a couple of things... If you attach your ceiling to the existing joists, using a resilient channel (or hat channel on RSIC clips), you'll have to box in the "parallel" duct with several sheets of drywall, or maybe even MDF... Then have a "drop down" in your inner ceiling to accommodate the thickness of the bottom surface of the box. Or maybe you can remove the duct and make the box serve as a duct, covering its inner surfaces with insulating duct liner -- and perhaps you could attach the bottom surface of the box to 2x3 cleats on the inside of the joist, above the bottom of the joist by a few inches. Since this description is hard to follow, I posted an illustration below that hopefully clarifies this idea. Yes, it is still 3 leaf, which is something you want to try to avoid doing. However, there are times that you can't practically escape a third leaf, at which point you do the best you can and try to make up for it. Using 3 layers of mass along in the middle may diminish some of the third leaf effect. It's up to you to verify that this will meet code in your area. Don't miss [url=http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4442 ]my "http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4442" thread[/url]. You also need to be careful not to overload your joists. Chances are that you aren't able to add much weight to them at all -- builders are notorious for using "the least amount necessary" to get the job done, which often gives you very few options. Here's an excerpt from [url]my "Safety During Demolition, Construction" thread[/url]:
    Be sure not to overload your joists. Adding just one layer of gypsum to your ceiling could transform an otherwise safe ceiling to one that can kill. This span calculator (courtesy the American Wood Council) is helpful in determining the maximum span of a joist under a variety of weight parameters. For more information about loads and span tables, see Understanding Loads and Using Span Tables by Paul Fisette, as it appears on the American Wood Council Web site. When in doubt, consult a professional structural engineer.
You should really consider using [url=http://www.audioalloy.com/b10-00_products.html[/url]Green Glue[/url] between your mass layers. Check out my "Would Green Glue Save Lot of Work/Materials on My Ceiling?" and the "Definitive word on Green Glue?" threads over on StudioTips. Also, there's my post regarding my experience so far with Green Glue. I know you inquired about QuietRock recently -- The Green Glue concept is similar, but you assemble it yourself. Have you found out what your minimum height requirement is in your town, along with any other restrictions that may be in effect for basement conversions? You'll want to find these things out very early in your planning process. You'll need mechanical fresh air ventilation, and lots of it, to make up for your lack of windows in this living space you're building. Code requires it. Windows are more than a luxury -- they supply the room with fresh air on demand. No windows, no fresh air on demand -- unless you have a fresh air ventilation system. You need "a window that isn't a window." That's what mechanical ventilation will give you. The mechanical ventilation must be capable of exhanging all of the air in the room(s) a certain number of times per hour. In my town it's 2 ACH, which stands for 2 Air Changes per Minute, or once every 30 minutes. It's up to you to determine what code calls for in your town. I ran into this obstacle myself. For my project, I ultimately chose to use a Heat Recovery Ventilator (also known as a Heat Exchanger). Here is the point in my project thread that I decided to incorporate the a Fantech HRV. That's all the time I have for now -- maybe others can chime in with other ideas.

Does the flex duct shown in the pic Vent to Ceiling.jpg supply air from one of the metal ducts across your studio to the upstairs room as it appears? I'm trying to find a way to eliminate that flex, because as shown you're right; there's no practical way to get a 2-leaf barrier between you and the upstairs otherwise. You would need to build a serpentine duct between your trusjoists by dividing the height of the joist cavity in two, putting heavy mass layers halfway down from the top, more heavy mass at the bottom, and forcing the air to do an "ess" curve on its way to/from the metal ducting. This would require that the upstairs register is NOT really close to your studio wall, which it appears to be :cry: - only other thing I can think of is if you do the double leaf thing as above, line with duct board ABOVE the upper mass (next to the register), insulate BETWEEN the two masses, run this construct at least 2/3 of the way across the studio, and THEN either adapt from the upper half of this plenum BACK to flex duct, or find a different, higher mass way to attach to whichever duct it feeds from. Something like this... According to "Choosing Electrical Junction Boxes" on the Better Homes & Gardens Web site:
    Electrical codes require that all wire connections or cable splices be inside an approved metal or plastic box. And every box must be accessible -- you cannot bury it inside a wall. This protects your home from the danger of fire and makes it easier to inspect and upgrade your wiring in the future.
I don't think your electrical panel relocation idea is going to fly. You may be better off making a closet in that rear corner where the electrical panel and window are... NOT normal, YES problem... :cry: Sounds like they poured your basement floor on un-compacted earth and it settled afterward, or else there WAS water problems and part of the earth under the slab was washed away.
