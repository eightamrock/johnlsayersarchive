some mid high absorbers (rockwool on 6mm mdf framed with a material cover) at points down the room, these are from an old build and hang from hooks about an inch off the wall so are very easy to move and shift around as needed.
Those sound good. But you'll probably need more than just the treatment you mentioned. Theoretically, you need 295 sabins of absorption in that room to meet ITU/EBU recomendations. That implies 295 square feet of open window, which is about 28 square meters. The entire surface area of the full room (all four walls, plus ceiling and floor) is only 54 m2, so in theory you need at least 50% of that covered in absorption. Your floor takes up 11 m2, so the remaining surfaces (wall and ceiling) only give you 43 m2 to play with. The window looks like another 3 m2, the soffits are maybe another 4 or so. Allowing for doors and other thins, you probably don't have much more than about 30 to 35 m2 available, so practically all of your room surfaces will need absorption, in one form or another. I'm looking at my crystal ball, and I see a lot more panels in your future! :)
At the rear of the room I have a 2 seater couch and planned on using floor to ceiling traps using the superchunking method (had great results in the past) and some form of high end absorption on the back wall (auralex, studio foam or a broadband absorber if needed, etc.).
I'd go with a very thick layer of absorption across the entire surface of that wall. At least 4" of fiberglass or mineral wool, spaced at least 4" away. Or maybe angled, so it is closer to the wall at the bottom and further away at the top. I'd also cover both that and your superchunks with plastic, thin wood, or perf panel of some type (maybe), to not kill the high end too much.
I will also be hanging a cloud consisting of a timber frame cradling studio foam above mix position
Make that cloud hard backed, and angle it, to help break up the vertical modes a little. Also, studio foam is expensive: plain old fiberglass or mineral wool insulation is much cheaper and just as effective. Of course, if you have the foam already then you might as well use it (assuming that it really is proper acoustic foam, and not the typical worthless "foam by mail" type, or packing foam, or mattress foam, or upholstery foam, or any other type of foam that won't work).

Hangers: Being a small room I don't have much space to place some big hangers as suggested in micmic's thread and John's suggestions (viewtopic.php?f=2&t=12365) but my thoughts are that by keeping the panels the same mass and just changing the dimensions to fit the space (namely under the screen), would this have the same/positive effect?
Hangers are a poorly understood subject, with little science to back them up but plenty of evidence that they work. Nobody doubts that they are effective, but there just isn't much published research out there on HOW they work, or the theory behind them. That said, based on John's comments, it isn't the mass that matters, but rather the spacing and size. They should not be heavy: homasote is about 1/4 the density of MDF or plywood, and is very soft, self-damped, etc. It's not resonant at all, so that's not how they work. It seems to be something to do with impedance, and something to do with waveguides, so what matters is the size, spacing and arrangement, not the mass. In other words, make them roughly the same size as John does, and use his same principles for spacing. Your image shows a lot of very thin hangers placed very close to each other, but that's not the way John builds them, so I wouldn't do that. The way you have them in the cavity uner the video screen between the soffits looks much better.
I wanted to board this somehow and leave a reflective finish to further treat in symmetry with the opposite wall... Any suggestions on this?
You can board it up if you want, but you don't need to do that to get symmetrical reflections. You say the opposite wall is drywall, so there isn't much difference in how reflective the two sides are. Glass and drywall are pretty similar in reflection for most of the spectrum, and only get different at very low frequencies, where stiffness (rigidity) starts to matter. So there's no need to cover that window, especially considering you aren't interested in isolation. However, if you so still want to cover it, then one option would be to build a window plug that can be slipped into place and removed as needed. There's quite a few examples of plugs on the forum. One reason you might want to plug it, is to gain back that surface area for absorption.
I've in the past boarded a window using 12mm mdf with rockwool and a material finish, basically creating a massive mid high absorber that was a little too much to my ears in the end, kinda deadened the room a bit too much!
Yup! You should have made the rockwool thicker (at least 2") and covered it with plastic, thin wood or maybe even perforated panel (as needed), to make it more of a low and mid absorber, but not touching the highs to much. It's easy to have too much high frequency absorption in a room, but practically impossible to have too much mow frequency absorption, especially in a small room. So the trick is to put stuff in front of the low absorption to reflect the highs back into the room, and only let the lows through for treatment.
I know I could use a reflective surface under the finish material but here I'm trying to find as much symmetry as possible. The edge of the window is around where the first reflections are so hanging absorption half over the wall/window would look a little funny.
If the whole unit is going to be covered in cloth, then you could put deep absorption across the whole panel, and only cover it partially with the reflective "whatever". In other words, leave the section at the first reflection points uncovered, but cover the rest. You won't see the difference, since the cloth will hide it. Or you could make one half into a slot wall and leave the other half purely absorption, and do the exact same on the other side of the room. That would probably work rather well, actually.
I feel I'm on the right track
It sure looks that way! :) About the only other thing I'd add is to try to use the wall/ceiling corners for superchunks, to give you the extra bass trapping that you are going to need. Overall, looks like a fun project. - Stuart -

Soundman202023 June 2012#4
Can I ask... Which calculator are you referring to?
I like Bob Gold's calculator ( http://www.bobgolds.com/Mode/RoomModes.htm ) as it gives you a large amount of very useful information. It's a bit intimidating at first, but very good. There are several others out there, that are also very good and easier to use. But beware of the ones that only show you axial modes: tangentials and obliques are also very important. Even though they are lower in energy and harder to excite, they still exist and still affect the room. So whatever calculator you use, make sure it considers all three types. - Stuart -

What helps a lot is to group related geometry together into "components", and to duplicate those as needed. So select the six sides that make up a stud, for example, do "make component", and they now act as one. Then duplicate that as many times as needed, and if you adjust any one of the duplicates, they all change in the same way. Lots of other things that you'll figure out...
For both the left and right and top sections of the wardrobe I'll be filling these with a standard insulation ... covered and sealed with 9mm Plasterboard where the doors would normally be. My aim here is to make sure that these spaces don't resonate.
Ummm.... if you cover them and seal them, then you are FORCING them to resonate! You are creating a tuned resonant cavity, basically a membrane trap, and the tuned frequency will be set by the surface density of that plasterboard and the depth of the cavity. This is not a good plan if you want to stop it from resonating! Just leave off the plasterboard, and it will be fine like that.
