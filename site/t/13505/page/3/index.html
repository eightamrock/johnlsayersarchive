Hello Rhino. Well its been a long time since I felt like posting here. But since no one else has bothered to answer your questions, I'll throw my .03 in here :mrgreen: Ok, lets see...
One states that the entire soffit structure should be completely rigid; ie the frame, shelf, and front plane of the soffit should all be directly coupled to the speaker enclosure, as an extension of the speaker. The other states that the frame and speaker support should be independent of the front plane of the soffit, allowing for a few mm around the edge of the speaker enclosure so that it doesn't connect with the front plane.
Well, from my understanding, this is one of those "proprietary" design elements that very few pro studio designers will devulge details about. I asked some pro's here.... http://recforums.prosoundweb.com/index.php/t/29580/0/ As you can see, its a pretty "touchy" subject. :roll: As to the "rigid" scenario, I "believe" the term "rigid" actually means that...ie...a WOOD framed front wall can NOT possibly be totally rigid. There will be some inherent "resonance" within the structure. Thats why proponents of this scenario use STONE, CONCRETE, or MASONRY of some type, with the monitors supported by...(now this is my "own" guess...) a masonry enclosure with some type of fastening mechanisim for the monitor support. Since the whole front wall is inherently a massive rigid structure, it provides the resistance against vibration transmission from the monitors and or resonance of the wall itself. At least this is my guess. As to a wood framed front wall, my personal "opinion" is the monitors should be supported by a "decoupled mass", such as a concrete or masonry block. However, this whole mass with monitors attached, should be decoupled from the floor, as well as the wall/soffit framing. I've seen various methods to accomplish this. Even Thomas shows a method on the Speaker forum. Some involve enclosing the complete monitor in rigid fiberglass, which in turn is supported by a massive masonry block or a frame which is isolated from the Soffit face support frame. Here is a few illustrations of what I mean..although I certainly didn't do any "calculations", its the ideas I'm trying to show. Here is one with the monitors surrounded by 703 and supported by a decoupled mass.

Rhino, I'm certainly no expert, but I believe your circumstances require the second scenario. Whether or not you chose to use a decoupled mass to support your monitors is up to you. However, there is an important consideration, as decoupling a Mass supported monitor system requires expert calculations shown here: http://www.earsc.com/HOME/engineering/T ... asp?SID=61
Which way do most people go?
I think for Home studios, very few would have the space, deep pockets, or expertize to design a Massive wall scenario. In the pro studio world, it simply is a matter of the Studio designers school of thought/preference and experience. However, there is ALWAYS a caveat. I've been told personally, that ALL studio design is NOT 100% science, and can be considered a series of compromises, as no one design can encompass nor solve every concievable conundrum. ie..reflections off a console face as an example. BTW, Phillip Newel has plenty to say in regards to this conundrum. Consider your sidewall window scenario. From what I've read in the Master Handbook of Acoustics, it takes an Acoustical expert to predict reflection geometry. I don't have the exact "quote" at hand but using his explanation, here was "my" attempt at designing a RFZ WITHOUT absorption. This has to do with understanding the physics of sound reflections. I make no claims to this effect, and this was ONLY an attempt to understand what Mr. Everest was describing. But here it is anyway to illustrate my point.
External image, not preserved — original: http://httpics.com//is.php?i=989&img=RFZ_attempt_1.j.jpg
What I'm trying to say is, regardless what others "think" about your sidewall window vs an RFZ, the actual prediction of the reflection geometry is quite complex and beyond the scope of what most people here can accurately comment on. This is why for most humble studio project owners, a sidewall ABSORPTION scenario is the easiest way to gain a reasonable early reflection free zone. I say reasonably, because without the benifit of expert design, or post construction testing, there is absolutely no guarantee that you will indeed end up with a TRUE RFZ. At least in my opinion. This whole subject leaves my mind boggled sometimes as I see MANY MANY studio designs where the use of side windows LOOKS like they would be one giant reflector into the engineering position. Even some of Mr. Sayers. Take for example, Manifold Studios. Mr. Latchot chose a standard Polygon shape for the entire studio complex, whereby all angles are subsets of 120 degrees. Whether of not he calculated dimensions to produce a RFZ is beyond my knowlege, and the owner certainly won't say. But first appearances tell me something. Maybe because my knowlege of the physics of reflections are almost ZERO, I am probably spitting in the wind. But it sure looks like these side windows, doors, and even the front sidewalls themselves would produce a HUGE reflection into the engineering position. But I may be totally wrong. However, this is only to illustrate that unless ABSORPTION is used, the sidewall geometry must reflect in a calculated angle or the dimensions from the centerline of the front window to the outer edge of the soffit must have a bearing on this starting point of the reflection. Which is exactly where you are asking about. One thing about this hexagon room, is in relationship to the front wall, the sidwall angles are 30 degrees, vs yours at 6 degrees, which reflect these early reflections directly towards the REAR sidewalls, which are built of Masonry with builtin QRD wells. Whether or not these reflections miss the engineering position and create a RFZ, only the designer knows. Btw, this is why I used a hexagon in my "interpretation" of your space. Although, proving it provided a RFZ was beyond the scope of my abilitys, plain and simple and I'll be the first to admit it. Anyway, take a gander. gullfo17 October 2010#74
it's actually fairly common to build a large rectangular room with all the isolation needs met and built around good ratios. then to build your room treatments inside. the angled treatment walls and ceiling allow for plenty of space to add the bass trapping needed etc. having the hangers upfront as well as the back will help. room modes exist regardless of the room shape so just because you created a low angled front to your ceiling hasn't eliminated room modes or the need to treat them, it just means it's harder to estimate them and left no space for the treatments... that said, if you need to angle your ceiling to fit within the warehouse it's what you have to do, but i'd shoot for more space rather than less and use that space for treatments.
