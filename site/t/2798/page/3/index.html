Summ the questions: what is the 3.6pcf good for? The 3.6 pcf looks good for the floor, as shown in your pic - to be sure, you can cut strips of the 0.75 pcf "fluffy" stuff, about 150mm wide, and lay it centered between each joist before putting the chipboard on top. Try it both ways, you want the weight of the chipboard to compress the insulation enough NOT to keep the chipboard from touching the joists, with JUST the weight of the chipboard. Lay a piece of the chip board over 3 joists (with pucks under them) and the insulation, so you have joist/insulation/joist/insulation/joist, then lay the piece of chipboard on top. Leave it overnight, then check. If the chipboard is touching the joists (or almost) this is what you want. BTW, just because the chipboard interlocks, does NOT mean you shouldn't SEAL it; I would put a light bead of acoustic caulk into each joint, compress the joint, and wipe the excess. You need an airtight seal here. If you've not used this type boards before, you need to leave a bit of slack in joints anyway - so the caulk will ensure a good seal, while letting the boards expand slightly as they reach moisture equilibrium in the room. what is the 2.5pcf good for? This should be your wall insulation; also good for absorbers, ceiling, traps, just about anything. what to do with the layers on the wool (the black and the aluminiumlike)? The black; if you use on ceiling, put the black DOWN as you thought. Walls - doesn't matter, unless it's a SLOT resonator; then, put it closest to the slots, up against them. No need for cloth covering that way. The silver stuff will act as a vapor barrier; so don't use it anywhere that both sides of the wall or ceiling aren't at similar temperatures, or it will cause mold. You could use it in the ceiling if you don't have enough of the other, if you do that the silver part should go UP so you get the absorption. Specifically where you DON'T want to use the silver stuff is on EXTERIOR walls or ceilings, or on the floor. comment the doors and the chipboard Doors look good; if they are "pre-hung", check to see if the metal extrusions they are framed with have any hollow "voids" - if so, these must be filled. Here in the US, a product called "plumber's putty" is good for this. Chipboard - looks fine, see comments earlier. If you have problems getting the boards to go together enough, you can use a scrap edge cut from one of the boards as a PAD - put it into the mating edge of the board you're trying to move, and hit the SCRAP piece with a large hammer. That way you won't damage the interlocking edge of the GOOD piece. should the rubber compress 10% or 20% one time you say 20 on time 10.. i am confused You're not the only confused one - here 'tiz, as simple as I know as of today - any elastomer needs to be slightly compressed at rest, or it can't act as a spring - most manufacturer's guidelines say a MINIMUM of 5% compression, 10% is best, and more than 20-25% will SERIOUSLY shorten the working life of the rubber. Therefore, if you aim for 10% in your calculations you will be right where you need to be, somewhere between maybe 7-12%. This won't seriously affect life of the material, but WILL optimise its effectiveness as a spring. All elastomers "sag" with time under compression, this will not be a bad thing because your insulation will also; that way, you still get contact with the underside of the floor, so it stays damped. What weights to consider for your 10% target - everything that will be in the room (and everything that IS the room) ALL the time. No people though. You have the weights of each type material, now you calculate how many pieces of each, add them up, and divide by the floor area to get total surface area of rubber you need (in actual contact - don't forget that only the part the joist sits on counts) - this will give you your total contact area. If you are NOT floating the walls on top of the floated floor, you're done. If you ARE floating the walls on the floors, there is more to do yet. Let me know for sure, but it looks like you're putting the floors INSIDE the walls. all the rooms are separate.. here is the thing.. now my wall between live and CR will be 55 thick.. with 32 cm of air.. will that be a problem? can there be too much air? can i now fasten the gypsum boards on the wood framing.. since they are decoupled? that would make the wall at least 20 cm thinner.. That much air is fine, although most of it should be filled with insulation (except windows :=) - yes, if your inner and outer frames are decoupled you can mount the wallboard directly to studs on both sides. Just be careful to make sure that NO PART of one half of a double wall is in hard contact with ANY part of the other half. Are you using double gypsum over the joists in ALL rooms? If not, you will lose isolation. Above the gyp you need insulation - if you have enough, you could use a layer of the 0.75 pcf insulation on top of the gypsum, then (if it's not too thick) a layer of that 8 pcf stuff. How thick is the 8 pcf stuff? If more than about 3", this might not be a good plan - you don't want the heavy stuff to compress the light stuff by very much, like 50% or less. Did I miss anything? I need more time. the pucks should be centered at what distance? 24"? The pucks should be under the joists (obviously :=) Spacing is what you are calculating. If you cut the pucks to a specific size, you will know how many square inches of contact surface they have where they meet the joist - for example, with 50mm joists (2") a 1" wide puck would have 2 sq. inches of contact area. If you can depress that puck by 10% by placing a TOTAL weight on it of 20 pounds (1 kG = 2.2 pounds) then the pucks need 10 pounds per square inch of contact space. So, if your total floor weight is 1800 pounds, you would need 180 square inches of contact area. Using the above puck size of 1" wide, or 2 square inches, you would then need 90 pucks total. If you then had 168 lineal feet of floor joist, this would put the pucks 1.86 feet apart. In reality, a puck that tall should be wider; if you double the width, you would then need 45 pucks (in this example), which would be spaced 3.7 feet apart, which is still good spacing for that size joist and loading - Now, if you were building your walls on TOP of this floor, you would want more support under the wall - so you would ADD more pucks under the wall section, calculated on the added WALL weight. If your ceiling were ALSO floated on these walls, that weight would need to be averaged along the wall length and added to the total sprung weight. Spock wrote:

Alarm bells are ringing in my head. :shock: Do you hear them too? You're not using the Neoprene for leveling; you're using it for decoupling/floating. In order for rubber to level things out, you'd have to overcompress the rubber in the high areas and undercompress the rubber in the low areas. That means your decoupling will be failing in those areas or will fail sooner than the other areas. Failing = flanking = leakage = bad news. In my design, I also have an unleveled floor - I will be using wood legs of varying heights with rubber (EPDM) pucks underneath them. The wood legs are for leveling, and the rubber is for decoupling. If you look on my thread that Steve posted a link to above, I had nearly a 3" fall from one corner of the CR to the other. I had to level mine. I used a laser and marked the four corners of the room, chalk lined it, then used another laser ($20 from Sears) that shoots the straight visible line around the room. Found my center point. Measured from the top of the rubber, to where the laser hit the tape measure and cut blocks to fit between the rubber and the bottom of the floor. Some were 2 7/8" and some where 1/4".
