Actually, if the existing ceiling is concrete THAT is your first leaf - this makes the gypsum above joists your SECOND leaf, and anything UNDER the joists would be a THIRD leaf. So, if there isn't room to physically get up there and add a second layer of gypsum on top of the joists, you would be better off if you can find heavier panels for that single layer - However, if the outer ceiling is concrete, and it is SEALED AIRTIGHT, and it is several inches thick, then even a single layer of gypsum with that wide a gap would give enough isolation that it won't be a problem; if that's the case, you could just put lots of insulation on top of the gypsum that's on top of the joists, and consider any acoustic treatment afterward. A lot of people tend to think only of what THEY ADD to a building as being significant; this simply isn't so. The ENTIRE structure has to be taken into account when figuring out what will happen acoustically - so, if there is already a center of mass (the concrete ceiling) then you should only put ONE MORE center of mass in place (with its separating air/insulation gap), and then any acoustic treatment you need inside that. Also keep in mind that some acoustic treatments will WEAKEN your sound proofing somewhat by actually BECOMING a third leaf - panel type bass traps will do this, as will helmholtz resonators. but i can but cloth under the joists and 2 layers of gypsum above joists.. If you can't put insulation on TOP of your gypsum inner ceiling, then you need to use it UNDER - without it, those panels will ring and cause serious coloration of sound. One possible way would be to do a complete, slightly compressed FILL of the joist cavities, then put your boards up - this will cause some isolation loss because of triple leaf, but if your outer ceiling is concrete this may not be a problem. I would go ahead and put the boards up as tongue and groove, like they were intended, for the section over the mix area - Over the rest, you could install the boards keeping a GAP between each, (length-wise, not end-to-end) alternating 1/16", 3/16", 5/16" - this would give a slat resonator/diffusion effect over the range of frequencies from around 100 hZ up to about 250 hZ figuring your 2x6 joists. You would need to cover this area with cloth before putting up the boards, or the insulation may poke through - also, the cloth is necessary for the helmholtz calculations to be close. The doors you show look to me like raised panel doors - are they quite a bit thinner in the center than at the edges? If so, I hope you can return them - what you need for decent isolation is MASS, so a solid core door in this context means THICK EVERYWHERE, but without a hollow center like cheaper doors. Unless the insulation is about 20mm THICKER than the cavity, it will NOT work out. Gravity is NOT your friend here, you need COMPRESSION (not too much though) to keep the insulation in contact with the gypsum or it will SAG, allowing the gypsum to ring. If you use the spaced boards idea (helmholtz effect) and a cloth cover UNDER the insulation before the boards go on, you can cut strips of insulation that are maybe 1/3 as wide as each space between joists, and add these down the center of each cavity before putting the cloth on - this will stretch the cloth a bit, but the boards will take care of that. Using a full, slightly compressed fill with spaced boards will change the helmholtz action slightly so the calculator doesn't work quite right, but that's part of why I suggested to use variable spacing between boards; that way, you get a wider range of frequencies that absorb, so there won't be any peaks at one frequency. Another thing I noticed in a few spots - if you want isolation, you need NO CONTACT between surfaces. knightfly20 February 2005#43
Yes, the rockwool can rest on the asphalt - I would not, however, put any OTHER vapor barrier in the floor - instead, I would do whatever you can to leave air circulation under/around the floated floor, as long as it doesn't hurt your double envelope construction and keep it from having two continuous masses with air space/insulation between them. The asphalt will act as a vapor barrier, which (unfortunately) should be toward the INSIDE of the room instead of on the OTHER side of the insulation. All I can think to do for this is to let the floor BREATHE as much as possible, but WITHOUT violating any of the sound proofing rules you already know of. One layer of particle board and one of chip board will be fine; if you can get construction adhesive, I would use that on the FIRST layer ONLY - you want a solid connection to the joists, and make sure the screws sink at least even with the surface of the boards, so the second layer won't have any "lumps" to keep it from laying perfectly FLAT against the first layer. Stagger ALL seams, caulk all cracks before adding the second layer, and the same for whatever flooring you use over the top layer. The bathroom scale was just to weigh your joists, etc, because I don't have proper weights of the dimensioned lumber you're using. You need to know the weight of EVERYTHING that will be floated on those pucks in order to get the compression right... Steve

knightfly22 February 2005#47
Petar, for the basic room itself you need to find out (weigh) what each type and size of building material that will be supported by your neoprene weighs, keep a running total, then divide by the # of square meters that particular floor covers, to get the weight per square meter that will be supported by the pucks. Then, you need to find out how much weight per square centimeter it takes to compress your rubber by 10% of its thickness - Then, you need to find from that, what your TOTAL contact area needs to be between floor joists and neoprene, and divide that out to find out how large each puck needs to be at acceptable span distances - if you're using those heavy 5x10 cm joists, the bearing area of the joist will be 5 cm in one direction and maybe 2.5 cm in the other, if you make the pucks 2.5 cm wide - they should be longer than it takes to go across the joist, maybe 75-80mm so they stick out past the sides of the joists a bit - they should be glued in place on the joists with contact cement. If you start by calculating the weight of ALL your building materials for each room, I'll help you work out spacing of the pucks - but you also need to experiment to see how much weight it takes to compress one of your double thickness (12mm total) neoprene pucks when that puck is 25mm x 75mm, placed CROSS-wise of one of your joists - Read the Floating Floor "sticky" first page, and then I'll explain anything that isn't clear... Steve

knightfly22 February 2005#49
That's great about the decoupling; NOT so great about using gypsum in this way. You would be OK if you were going to use the gysum BETWEEN two layers of sheet goods, like between two chipboards or particle boards or plywood - but gypsum isn't real strong for "crush", so what will probably happen if you use gyp directly on joists with chipboard over, is that the weight will tend to "squeeze out" the gypsum from between the two layers of paper, leaving a sagging, squeaking floor. If you can't afford the extra chip board right now, you would be better off putting just ONE layer of chipboard and adding a second layer later on.
