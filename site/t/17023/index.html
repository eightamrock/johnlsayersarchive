the walls in my garage are just framing with dry-wall over it, I dont believe any insulation is between those walls since its just a garage. My so called "plans" would be kinda just to build rooms inside of this garage
Unfortunately, that would not be a good idea, and could even end up making your isolation WORSE, believe it or not. This is kind of hard to grasp, until you start understanding how acoustics works, but there is an issue with building extra walls in front of existing walls. This is called the "three-leaf" problem. Basically, an isolation wall is a tuned system. It is tuned to a natural resonant frequency. It does not isolate at all at that frequency, and isolates progressively better at all higher frequencies. Obviously, if you can tune it to a very low frequency, lower than than the bottom limit of human hearing (20 Hz, roughly) then the wall will isolate all of the frequencies that we can hear. That's the goal. For complicated reasons of physics, it turns out that the best way to do that is with a two-leaf wall, called an "MSM" (Mass-Spring-Mass) wall. Basically that means that you have a "leaf" of drywall on one side ("M") an air gap in the middle with insulation in it ("S") and a second "leaf" of drywall on the other side. If you add another leaf, it turns out that it re-tunes the entire wall to a much higher frequency, thus reducing the isolation for low frequencies, not increasing it. Not intuitive, but fact. There's one other condition you need for this MSM thing to work: decoupling. That means that there is no mechanical connection between the drywall on on side, and the drywall on the other side. Of course, in normal house walls, both leaves of drywall are attached to the same set of studs, which "couple" them together, and that's why normal house walls don't isolate very well. To decouple, you build TWO frames a short distance apart, with drywall on only ONE side of each frame. Simple! In your case, you already have two leaves on your existing wall, so if you just put up another frame and put drywall on one side of it, then you would have a 3-leaf wall, which will isolate worse in low frequencies (kick, toms, bass, keyboards, etc.), not better. So you think "OK, well I'll put drywall on both side of my new frame, as well!". Nope. That would make a FOUR-leaf wall which isolates even worse than 3-leaf for low frequencies! So, that's just a long way of saying that what you will have to do to start your build, is to take off the existing drywall, to expose the studs. With that gone, then you can build another frame an inch or so away, and put new drywall on only one side of that, thus creating a good fully-decoupled MSM isolation wall. So you had the basic idea correct, of building a "room-in-a-room", but that important detail of 3-leaf vs. 2-leaf was missing.

I suppose in terms of isolation it needs to be as isolated as i could possibly get it.
You need to put a number on that, in terms of decibels of transmission loss. You can figure that out with a simple sound level meter, by measuring the level of a typical session that you might normally record, then also measure the level of what is "quiet enough" for your neighbors, or for others in your house, or better still, "quiet enough" to meet your local noise regulations. Call your municipality to find out what they are. So once you now "how loud you are" and "how quiet you need to be", subtract "B" from "A" and you have your isolation number. With that number in hand, you can decide on the type of construction you need to build in order to get that number on your budget. THEN you can start designing the shape of the room, positions of the new stud walls, etc. There's a process here that you should follow to figure out how to build your room correctly, and is starts with figuring out your isolation number. So beg, borrow or buy a sound level meter (if you don't already have one), and make those measurements, then we can progress from there. You can pick up a meter on e-bay for around fifty bucks or so. Not expensive at all, and you'll need one anyway for the final "tuning" of the room (installing the final round of treatment inside your finished room). - Stuart -

Soundman202019 September 2011#7
would a good way to determine how loud I a m to my neighbors is to use the meter outside of my space closest to my neighbors and see how loud it is there? that way I could get a grasp on the level it is to them?
Yep! Set up a full-range speaker system in there, and play full-range music at the level you normally expect to produce once the room is finished. That might be anywhere from 85 dB if all you do is mix, up to about 115 dB or more, if you plan to track rock bands in there with acoustic drums. Then measure the levels outside the room right now. that will tell you how much isolation you already have. Now get someone else inside to turn down the volume while you carry on measuring outside, until it gets low enough that you can't hear it above the ambient noise level. That tells you how quite you need to be. Of course, you should measure the "quiet" level at the quietest time of day when you think you'll be making noise in your room, which is most likely something like 3 AM or some such... - Stuart -
