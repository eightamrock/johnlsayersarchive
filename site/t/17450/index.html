Soundman202010 March 2012#2
Hi Eric, and Welcome to the forum! :) Great first post, by the way: All the basic info is in there, complete yet concise. Wish we had more first posts like yours! OK, some general comments on your situation, in no particular order: First, there's a process, or sequence of steps that you can use to approach the design of your studio. Start with the simplest, most basic: How much isolation do you need? But this has to be objective, not subjective. Real-world numbers, in decibels, not guesses. You already partially answered one of the key questions here: "How loud are you?". We know that you'll be around 87 dB, peaking perhaps 95 to 100, in the control room. That's pretty normal. But what you DIDN'T say is how loud you'll be in the live room. You mentioned drums, which is one of the loudest and most difficult instruments to isolate, so assuming that this is your "loudest loud", we're talking about +/- 115 dB. So we have one side of the equation. The other side is "How quiet do you need to be?". There's two possibilities here on the limit for that number: one is other people, as in family and neighbors, and the other is legal limits. Your local municipality certainly has a noise ordinance, so it would be good for you to get a copy of that, so you understand what your legal obligations are at different times of day. You might be surprised at just how quite you have to be, legally, especially at night. Or going back to the first approach, perhaps you don't have a legal problem, but you still might have family/neighbor problems. So the ideal thing is to actually measure levels with a sound level meter, and find out what acceptable levels are for those around you. Now, simply subtract your "quiet" number from your "loud" number. That's how much isolation you need. For example, if it turns out that you need to be no more than 50 dB outside your studio in order to not disturb the peace, and you plan to record drums at 115 dB, then you need 65 dB of isolation. Or if you only plan to
mix
those drums at your normal 87 and never actually record live drums, then you only need 27 dB of isolation, which is roughly what an ordinary wall gives you anyway. But I'm betting that "ordinary wall" is nowhere near enough. So that should be your first order of business: find out "how loud" and "how quiet", so you can put a number on how much isolation you need. So what do you do with this number once you have it? Well, once you have it, you can now start looking for construction methods and materials that will give you this amount of isolation. There's a huge amount of research available on that, so you can simply look at the various options, find one that gives you the level you need and also fits your budget, and you have your first problem solved! The type of construction will also dictate the next step: space. Different methods of isolation need different thicknesses of walls and ceilings, and there are some techniques that allow you to maximize isolation while minimizing loss of height. There are also pitfalls to be wary of, such as the dreaded "three-leaf" wall, square rooms, etc. So now you can start the juggling game! :) Figuring out how to fit the type of construction that you NEED in order to obtain the isolation that you NEED, into the space that you have, without losing too much height, while also minimizing costs. Fun! :) Anyway, that's the basic approach. Put numbers on your isolation needs, and then find a method of getting that level of isolation.
I would like a decent amount of isolation in both the control room and the live/room room, but neither need to be dead quiet.
"Decent amount" is not a number! :) For me, "decent amount" could mean 80 dB, while for the next guy it could be 40 dB. You need to put a number on it.
And again, the acoustics have to be great for mixing/mastering in the control room and good enough in the live/dead room to avoid bad modal and comb filtering problems.
All rooms have modes. Small rooms have big modal problems. Big rooms don't. But "big" and "small" here are with reference to sound waves, so "big" means dozens of feet. So practically all home studios are small rooms, and have modal problems. The trick is to minimize modal problems by careful choice of room dimensions, and careful testing and treatment.
I have reached a point where I am frustrated in trying to decide the layout of the studio because there don’t seem to be any A+ options on where to build the control room or the live/dead room in the space that I have while also keeping the current bathroom and wet bar.
Welcome to the wonderful world of studio design! If you got to the "frustration because I can't make it fit" stage, then you are now fully qualified! That's always the issue with studio design: it's always trading off something for something else.
Main space: 7’11” – from concrete floor to the floor board of above ceiling 7’4” – from concrete floor to the bottom of the above floor joists 6’7” – from concrete floor to the bottom of the ductwork
That's pretty low! Which means you have some bad news coming your way: Trying to fit in a great mastering studio will not be easy! You need at least 1500 cubic feet of volume for a critical listening room. And a low ceiling is not good for acoustics in any case. That's doesn't mean you can't get a darn good room in there: it just means it won't be a world-class mastering room.
First off, the ductwork. From my plans and photographs, you can see how this ductwork goes through the best space sqft-wise of the basement.
So I'd suggest picking a room arrangement that helps you work around that, instead of fighting it. For example, an RFZ design often has an angled ceiling, so duck the front of your room under the ducts, while leaving the rear part as high as possible.
keep the control room on that side even though it would require the ceiling to be asymmetrical as I finish around the ductwork,
Bad idea. Control rooms MUST be symmetrical, and that's even more critical for a mastering room. You need the front half of the room at least, to be absolutely symmetrical. The rear isn't so important, but the front is critical.
unless I put the whole ceiling at around 6’4” which I don’t want to do.
Definitely not! You need to find a design that keeps your ceiling as high as you possibly can. With careful planning, you should be able to get a ceiling height of maybe 7'9" for your final inner-leaf ceiling. Allowing for treatment, you'll be down to around 7'6" or so.
So probably an asymmetrical ceiling for the control room..fine.
Nope. Not so fine.
Because I’m pretty sure I need that space on the West side of basement for the live/dead room because I need the ceiling height.
You need ceiling height in your control room too, if you want it to be a mastering studio! So what is the highest priority? Mastering, or tracking?
I don’t know if you can decouple walls that are already finished
Yes you can, possibly involved some "surgery" to those walls. Especially since those walls will not be your final inner-leaf walls.
Not sure about the ceiling height, because it needs to be decently isolated but then acoustically treated to sound good inside that small room as well.
As I mentioned a bit earlier, there are techniques you can use to maximize ceiling height while still getting good isolation. But treatment is a different matter: The smaller the room, the more treatment it needs.
I’m pretty sure I want a completely absorbent ceiling throughout whole control room AND live/dead room in any of my options,
Not necessarily. Partially absorbent, for sure. But maybe not totally. It all depends on your overall design concept. I'd suggest that you switch to doing your layouts in SketchUp: 3D has a huge advantage over 2D when it comes to trying to visualize how things fit in. - Stuart -
