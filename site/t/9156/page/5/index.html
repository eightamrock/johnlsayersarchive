If you take this 1800 watt tool and run it on a 240v supply it will draw 7.5 amps of current. If, on the other hand, you ran the same tool on a 110V supply it would draw 16.4 amps! EG,
Lou, that's not entirely true. The power (P) is related to U and I. So with lower voltage there's just less power. Same current, same resistant. The tool will just deliver less power when voltage drops, it'll not keep the same power and draw more current. U=IxR P=UxI to use your example: U=240v P=1800W This means: I=P/U => I=1800/240 => I=7,5A R=U/I => R=240/7,5 => R=32 Ohm (just to check: P=I2xR => 7,5(2) x 32 = 1800) When U=110v (I and R stay the same): P=UxI => P=110x7,5 => P=825 Watt So the tool will deliver less power, not draw more current. But, yes, when that same tool CAN/MUST deliver 1800W at 110v THAN I=1800/110 => I=16,4A But I've yet to see that tool ;) It's a highly dangerous one. Great build logging here Lou, when ye'r dun. When U=110v (I and R stay the same): P=UxI => P=110x7,5 => P=825 Watt
Ro, That’s not entirely true my friend, because that does not satisfy Ohm’s Law. :wink: If we’re discussing the same tool, running on either 240V or 110V, then. the resistance is of course a constant ie 32 ohms at either voltage, therefore if the voltage drops then there will be a proportional drop in current drawn.
I=P/U => I=1800/240 => I=7,5A R=U/I => R=240/7,5 => R=32 Ohm (just to check: P=I2xR => 7,5(2) x 32 = 1800)
Spot on correct. :wink: :wink: Therefore working the maths through, V = I x R or as you imply, (but with different voltage), 110v = I x 32ohms I = 110V divided by 32 ohms (resistance is constant) = 3.44A Power = 3.44A x 110V = 378W (not 825!) :oops: To clarify what I was trying to say earlier, in order for a 110V tool to deliver the same wattage, then the resistance would naturally have to decrease in order to allow more current to be drawn, which would clearly require a completely different tool. Right then, so to conclude for this post at least, something that I’ve noticed since last December is that none of us actually ever mention money. I mean, hard cash, or if you kind folks prefer – what our hard efforts are drawing from our pockets :shock: . Now don’t get me wrong, I totally understand why - after all it’s somewhat personal – however, I thought it might be prudent to illustrate to any newcomer, exactly what our sort of ventures can cost. More importantly, in the words of one Mr Len Morgan, – ‘The hidden costs’. With this in mind, here then follows an accurate breakdown of the total cost of my isolated slab, (not including drainage, footings, brickwork etc). I fully appreciate that this may only apply to UK builds, but I sincerely hope it will provide something of an eye-opener for anyone considering the same. Please bear in mind that, rightly or wrongly, I have site-managed the job thus far, and am a total stickler for landing a deal. It’s what we call over here being – ‘Self-employed’ Here goes: Jack-hammer/compressor hire. £88.03 Labour for excavation of original floor. £160.00 Grab lorry to remove spoils. £180.00 Labour to prepare oversite. £400.00 6 Tonne 40mm down rejects. £80.00 1 bag bulk builders sand. £35.80 1 roll 300 grade membrane £54.60 14 sheets 2400mm x 1200mm x 70mm Celotex £379.53 Gaffer tape £7.04 6 sheets steel mesh + binding wire £144.21 Audio ducting (green tubes) F.O.C. Mains conduit £27.62 1 roll 2.5mm twin and earth mains cable £42.92 Labour for pour £270.00 Concrete for slab £490.00 Labour for screed £380.00 Screed £450.00 Total £3189.75 Floor area – 8.12m x 4.77m = 38.73m Cost/sq m (for properly engineered isolated slab) £82.36 There you have it, guys. Thanks for reading. Break out your wallets. Stay tuned Regards Lou 8)
