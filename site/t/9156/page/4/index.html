Morning guys – loads to tell you and show you this week, but first some response to the electrical questions! :wink: Eric – I have to concur with Keith – I have never regarded you as simple, Sir. Ro – I’ve read many of your posts as I consider you to be a fine contributor to the forum, always helpful, supportive, and with a sense of humour. OK folks, well the jury is still out on this one, but I would like to stick my neck out and clarify a couple of points if I may. I was pretty useless at maths and physics at school, but one thing I’ve always remembered is ‘Ohm’s Law’, this outlines the fundamental principles of just about everything to do with electricity. It simply states: V=IR where V is voltage, I is amperage, and R is resistance. This could be played with to establish that I=V/R (voltage divided by resistance) and alternatively, R=V/I Now for the definitions folks: Voltage is best considered as electrical pressure and is of course measured in volts, Amperage is current flow (amps) and resistance, (measured in ohms), hopefully speaks for itself. Whichever way you look at it, voltage is not power. (Sorry Ro – not trying to be pedantic honest!) :oops: Power is in fact wattage measured in, believe it or not Watts, – If you take all this and put it into maths terms you would get the following: Wattage (power) is Voltage x Amps. Spot on Ro, nice one again Dude. Current drawn (amperage) is Wattage divided by Voltage, and Voltage is naturally wattage divided by amperage. So then, if you take an 1800w power tool – it will perform just as efficiently on either 240v or 110v, however running on 110v it will draw more than double the current! If I’m waffling on, sorry - just trying to help. If you take this 1800 watt tool and run it on a 240v supply it will draw 7.5 amps of current. If, on the other hand, you ran the same tool on a 110V supply it would draw 16.4 amps! EG, 1800 divided by 240 = 7.5, whereas 1800 divided by 110 = 16.4 Sticking with the 240v supply and the 1800 watt tool the following figures match up: 1800(w) = 240(v) x 7.5(a), 7.5(a) = 1800(w) divided by 240(v) and finally 240(v) = 1800(w) divided by 7.5(a) All of the above concerns AC mains. Still researching this bit Ro, but when you relate all this to a battery powered unit rather than mains, you have to remember that, in this scenario, you’re now talking about a DC supply rather than AC. In other words, as you quite correctly point out, an increase in ‘electrical pressure’ (in a DC device) would indeed cause a proportional increase in current flow, whereas with AC, as explained it’s a completely different ball game. I’m pretty sure that you need to consider that many battery tools have a range of power ratings, so, if you ran a 500w only tool with a 14v battery instead of a 9v battery it wouldn’t necessarily make the drill go faster, but it would definitely make it spin for longer. Furthermore this helps explain why a cordless drill runs progressively slower as the charge from the battery weakens over use. Bear in mind also, that the resistance (how much the tool doesn’t want to play) here is constant. :wink: More on transformer design in the next issue, but for now, Phil - once again 110v is safer ,of course, as the user (on the secondary side) is totally isolated from the 240 supply on the primary side, plus the secondary side has a ‘tapped earth’ which I need to find out more about, and also, any damage to the equipment would only cause the user to be exposed to 55v. I’m not sure why since this is where my maths and physics let me down! Anyway – I hope some of this helps as, like you guys, it’s only intended as ‘knowledge shared is knowledge earned’.
