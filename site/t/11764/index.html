Soundman202011 March 2009#16
I think you might be confusing "isolation", "decoupling" and "flanking". A 10 inch concrete slab is a pretty good isolator for airborne sound between rooms: It has lots of mass, stiffness and density, so it will stop airborne vibrations very well. But concrete also transmits impact vibrations well. In other words, if you stand in front of a concrete wall and hammer a nail into a piece of wood in mid-air, without actually touching the wall, then that sound will not make it through the wall, since it is airborne. But if you put the piece of wood up against the wall and hammer the nail in, that sound WILL get through very well, since it is coupled directly to the wall. It is impact noise, and the wall transmits its easily. If you build another wall just beyond the first one, the sound of the hammered-nail-against-the-wall will NOT get through the second wall, for the same reason as in the first case: the sound is airborne, so the wall stops it. And the huge impedance mismatch between the air and the leaves on both sides and in the gap, plus the mass, rigidity, density, damping,etc. means that the sound in the air does not "get through" the wall easily. HOWEVER: neither of those discussions deals with the issue of flanking. Flanking is sound that gets around your isolation. It takes another path, detouring around the air gap, not through it. So if there is a steel bar connecting the two walls in the above example, that is a flanking path, and the vibration in the first wall is transmitted directly to the second wall, regardless of the air gap. Ditto if you have both walls directly attached to the same floor. Something else is transmitting the sound directly, mechanically, from wall to wall, "bypassing" your air gap. In order to stop flanking noise getting around the walls, you have to decouple in some way. Either you do something to disconnect one or both of the walls from the floor (such as mounting them on a decoupled, resilient platform of some kind), or you move the hammer-and-wood away from the first wall, so that they never actually touch it, and you only have airborne sound again, no direct impact. In the case of a drum kit, a good drum riser will help decouple the kit from the floor. Does your current studio have a well designed drum riser? That might be the problem. Or maybe the walls just are not up to par, and the drum sound is getting through anyway. In the case of your second-floor studio build, just relying on a single concrete floor is not going to solve the issue, no matter how well it isolates, since it is only ONE leaf, and it is still coupled to the building structure. Like several people here already said, since floating a floor is probably not an option, the only other option you have is to treat the ceilings in the rooms below, to decouple those from the slab. Remove the existing ceilings, and either use RSIC clips and hat channel direct on the underside of the slab, or resilient channel on existing joists, or a completely new set of joists that are NOT coupled to the building structure, to hang your new ceilings. Then add at least 2 layers of 5/8 drywall with Green Glue in between, and acoustic caulk in the small gap around the edges. That will take care of the flanking, and will also improve isolation greatly, since it adds a second leaf. And if flanking is still an issue after that, then you will have to do the same on the walls in the rooms below: add a second set of decoupled walls to the rooms below. Perhaps with RSIC clips on the walls and hat channel + drywall, or RC on the studs (if there are any), or new separate studs, plus a couple of layers of drywall with GG. Just like the ceiling. As far as I can see, this is the easiest and cheapest way of dealing with the issue. Floating your entire second floor (or at least the studio part) would be the other option, but it will be prohibitively expensive, as it involves many tons of concrete that is cast within a new sub-floor framework that is attached to decoupled screw-jacks on isolation mounts, that are raised once the concrete is set. And the existing structure most likely cannot take the huge additional load anyway, so you'll have to build additional pillars to support that, etc, etc. It can be done, but not easily and cheaply, like just hanging new ceilings downstairs can be done! The simplest, least expensive approach is to isolate the rooms on the first floor, as far as I can see. There just isn't an easier way to do what you want, since the laws of physics apply to your building in the exact same way that they apply to every other building. You can't wish them away. IMHO. YMMV. - Stuart -
