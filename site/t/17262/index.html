Hola Blazer, y bienvenido! :) También tenemos un foro en español, pero tu inglés es excelente, y por lo tanto probablmente será mejor continuar aquí: hay mucho más pérsonas en el foro en inglés. You have a great space, and a fantastic opportunity to start from the ground up! You are making me real jealous with that! :) Lucky guy! Regarding your basic questions: Area: The ITU and EBU recommendations for critical listening rooms (eg. studio control rooms) is for a minimum of 45 m3 total room volume. They don't specify shape, but that is based roughly on a rectangle with 10 foot ceilings (+/- 3m). That implies roughly 15 m2 floor area, or roughly 5 x 3. But that's not carved in stone! There are many, many variations here. There are different "schools" of studio design, with different shapes, ratios, sizes, angles etc. Most can be made to sound good. What you'll find most recommended here on the forum is a roughly rectangular shape, possibly with spayed walls (angled) and possibly with an angled ceiling, using the RFZ concept as the basis (RFZ = "Reflection Free Zone") That's the most common way of doing things, and will give you a great sounding studio.
Determining if I should build walls in awkward angles or build a box and do the angled treatment inside.
Yes. No. Maybe. :) In other words, either will work, but there are pros and cons. The reason why you might NOT want to angle the walls and ceiling, is to make it dead easy to build. Many studios are plain rectangles. The reason why you might WANT to angle them in the control room, is make a proper Reflection Free Zone, where the shape of the room is specifically designed to deflect reflections past your head and around you, so that you can ONLY hear the perfect, clear, pristine sound direct from the speakers, without it being muddied by reflections. With that design (recommended) you design the room to hear nothing but the speakers. The first reflections should arrive at your head at least 10 db below the direct sound, and at least 10ms later, (preferably 15 down, 15 later). You might also want to angle walls to eliminate flutter echo, and for other acoustic reasons, especially in the live room (tracking room). There are pros and cons both ways. RFZ will get you the best possible sounding room, especially if you flush-mount your speakers ("soffit mount"). But it makes things harder to calculate and predict in advance, and a bit harder to build (but not much harder!). Like most things in acoustics, it is all a trade off: You decide what fits you best, and live with the consequences.
I just need a live room where a typical 4-5 member rock band with double-bass drum drum set can fit comfortably.
OK, that implies that you need good isolation ("soundproofing"). A full acoustic drum kit can easily put out 115 dB. Your studio is right on the property line, so you need to isolate that down to whatever the legal limit is for your area. Call your local municipality and get a copy of the noise regulations. The difference between that number and 115 dB is your design goal for isolation. That number will dictate possible construction methods. The construction method that you choose will dictate the amount of space that your walls and ceiling take up. That in turn will dictate the size of the slab on which you will build your studio in order to get the amount of floor area and volume that you need. Etc. As you can see, there is a basic procedure that you should follow, that will sort of lead you to the design. First you need to set your goals and basic design concept, get your numbers in order, figure your techniques, figure your budget, and the rest will fall into place.
Not sure if I really need an isolation room. Read somewhere that vocals sound better in live room anyway.
Iso booths have their uses, especially if you will ever need to record two things together, such as vocals with a band, or an electric guitar cab with drums, etc. If you will never need to do that, then you don't need a booth.
-Would 12 feet ceilings force me to have a large area in order to have the correct ratios?
Not necessarily: Some ratios allow for long or wide rooms with lower or higher ceilings, and in any case ratios are not extremely important. Sometimes people get all hooked up on trying to perfect a ratio down to the last cm, but that really is not necessary at all! The idea with ratios is to stay away from the bad ones and get close to a good one, and you are done. Period. Don't get hooked on something that isn't too important. Besides, if your room is not rectangular (splayed walls, angled ceiling), then ratios are meaningless anyway: Ratios only apply to rectangles, not any other shape. As soon as you angle something in the room, then you can no longer use the normal equations for predicting modal responses that involve that particular wall. So for example, if you angle just your ceiling then you can no longer predict anything involving the vertical axis, but you can still predict x and y axials and tangentials: just not the z axials or tangentials, and no obliques that involve the ceiling. But that isn't a bad thing! Once again, people get too hung up on trying to predict every last aspect of the room, when in reality that is somewhat a waste of time, since the actual room will most likely not behave exactly as predicted anyway! You will still need to measure the response of the room after it is built, in order to decide what treatment you need. That goes without saying. So don't rely on prediction too much, since even minor variations in the actual construction can change the response. So just do basic prediction to get things roughly right, then after the shell is built run a full acoustic analysis of the room BEFORE you put any treatment in it, then put the initial treatment in and run the test again, to see what more needs to be done.
Is the high ceiling necessary only in the live room? Or in the mixing room too?
Height is good! Make the rooms as high as you can, within reason. But if you have to choose, it is more important to have height in the live room. Especially if you want to record drums.
-How do you calculate the shape of the ceilings?
Depends on what you want to do! For flutter echo, at least 12°. For RFZ, that depends on the rest of the design, but most likely 15° or more. You also do not need to angle the entire ceiling: It is possible to do just the front part, and leave the rest flat. Therea are many options.
If I put my studio in the red area, what dimensions would you choose? In this example is 12m x 6m. But it could also be 11.5m x 6.5m, 9m x 7m, 12 x 7.2m, ..., etc. How do you come up with a final number?
Yes! No! Maybe! :) Once again, it depends on what you want to do! If you need to record large groups, go with a bigger live room. If you need great acoustic sound on instruments, then go with a larger live room. If your priority is getting top quality mixes, then go with a bigger control room. If you will also be mastering, then go with a bigger control room. The good news is that you have plenty of space! With 12m x6m, I could easily come up with a design for a nice large control room with good ratio and acoustics, suitable for mastering, plus a good size live room suitable for a 5 member band plus at least one iso booth, good for either vocals or for a guitar cab, and possibly also a second vocal booth. That's a nice size.
What if I built the studio above the cars? like in an attic?
Not a good idea. Studios need a LOT of mass! Putting it on the second floor implies major support structure below. Expensive. But you could build it on the ground floor, and build part of your house above it! :)

Designing a studio is a lot more than just a handful of rules. It will never be exactly as predicted. Reality seldom matches theory.

Simple, tiny mistake, major consequences. If you compare two live rooms, do you consider better the one with more natural reverb?
That depends on what you are trying to do, what your design goals are, and what type (genre) of music you will be recording. If you plan to record Baroque orchestras, pipe organs, and 200 person choirs singing Handl or Bach, then yes, you'd need a place with 2, 3, or 4 second longer reverb times, or even longer. On the other hand, if you are recording soft, breathy, sexy, romantic Jazz vocals, then you might want reverb times under 400 ms. Plus, it isn't just the total reverb time that matters: it is the reverb time in each octave or frequency band that matters. There are guidelines and "rules" for the RT in each band, for each of the various types of music and sizes of room. It would be a bit complicated to go into all the details here, but the guy who designs your studio should know all about that, and will ask you detailed questions about the type of music you want to record, the types of instruments, types of vocals, how many at a time, etc. He will also probably suggest more than one live room if you have diverse needs, such as maybe a separate drum booth, vocal booth, guitar booth, piano room, etc., or he might even suggest variable acoustic treatment in the live room(s), so you can modify the acoustic response as needed, changing it for different types of recording. There are many options, and a good designer will take them all into account, and interrogate you about your design goals. The control room is a different matter: that is fairly well defined, and needs to be flat, neutral and with well controlled reverb times, but the live rooms can be whatever you want it to be, depending on your needs.
I know there are lots of "in betweens", but is there a limit on this? For example, is a cathedral too big?
Nope! A cathedral is the perfect size... for a pipe organ, symphony orchestra, and/or large choir playing music that needs long reverb times. But a cathedral would be a terrible place to record jazz, rock, pop, rap, gospel, techno, or a bunch of other genres. And the same applies in reverse: a room with reverb of 600 ms would be good for some of those genres, but totally inappropriate for the orchestra and choir doing a recital oc Bach's Brandenburg Concertos. In other words, it all depends on what you plan to do! That's the job of the designer; to take into account your needs, and design a room that meets those.

The general tendency these days is to have a room that is NOT dead, and has some character to it. Hi Scott. Welcome to the forum, but please check the forum rules for posting: You seem to be missing something important! :)
We were thinking about contacting one of the companies that makes beds and buying some foam from then.
That type of foam is probably useless, acoustically. The type of foam used in mattresses and upholstery is usually "closed cell" foam, which means that, at the microscopic level, it is something like air bubbles surrounded by foam walls all around, so air cannot move through it. That is no use, for acoustic purposes. For acoustics, you need "open cell" insulation, such as fiberglass, mineral wool, and things like that. At the microscopic level, open-cell insulation is more like tiny fibers, or sometimes like "bubbles that have burst open", and there is free passage for air to move through it. Also, it is a common misconception that foam, insulation, polystyrene, and things like that can be used to "soundproof" a room: they cannot. They do not stop sound, and that is not their purpose. Those materials are used as acoustic treatment on the walls and ceiling of the room, to make the room sound good, but they do nothing to stop sound getting in and out. "Soundproofing" (more correctly called "isolation") and treatment are two totally different things. You cannot stop sound with light, soft, fluffy materials. You can only stop sound with heavy, rigid, massive, solid materials, such as brick, concrete, glass, drywall (gypsum board), plywood, MDF, and things like that. The normal way of building a studio is with two walls made of those materials, with a an air gap between them. That is the best way to soundproof (isolate) a room at reasonable cost. There are other ways, but they are way too expensive and complicated for the average home studio builder. So a simple two-leaf wall with a single air gap is the best and cheapest.
