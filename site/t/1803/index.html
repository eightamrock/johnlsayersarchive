knightfly16 June 2004#5
HR, that was a drawing I did for someone with VERY minimal headroom, so they didn't want any sheet rock hanging down from the ceiling joists - there are much simpler construction methods - if you have a normal amount of headroom, and your ceiling joists will support two more layers of drywall (sheet rock) then you will only lose about 1-3/4" of headroom below the bottom edge of your existing joists for a reasonably quiet ceiling. You would likely still need to put the layer of sheet rock up against your subfloor, but then all you'd do is put rockwool batts (or similar) in the joist cavities, put Resilient Channel across the joists, and hang your two layers of sheet rock. The stickies give most of the details on this approach... Steve

dymaxian16 June 2004#7
Nope, if you have the headroom, go ahead and build a false ceiling that sits on top of the interior stud walls. This is probably the best solution for sound isolation, just because it'll give you almost complete structural isolation. But it also takes up the most room. It'll help the headroom a little bit if you stick the false ceiling joists up in between the existing floor joists above, tho- set their bottom faces an inch or 2 down, and just drywall over that. There are ways to hang the ceiling off the floor structure above, but isolating that kind of thing is always tricky. If you can build it up off the interior stud walls, you're better off. Good luck!

knightfly16 June 2004#10
First - any conduit can carry sound where it's not wanted - best way I've thought of (haven't tried this, but don't see any problem) is to buy some of the rubber pipe couplers that are typically used for DWV service (no pressure) available at Home Depots, etc - they are just a big rubber tube with a metal wrapper and two attached hose clamps, normally used to attach sections of large (3-4") drain pipes together. I'd use these where the conduit passes between rooms, to isolate any flanking noise - if you also stuff the conduit full of insulation (after wires are in place)between rooms, keeping the 2-leaf principle in mind, this should work as well as anything (along with at least 2 bends in each room's conduit) For metal conduit, they make special isolator joints with a removable plug that you fill with expanding foam - I've not seen those for plastic, though they might be available. second - depth required for a floating ceiling depends on the span - you need enough room for adequate frame depth above the wall caps. The actual wallboard can go inside the room's walls, hung on Resilient Channel. Couple examples: both consider two layers of 5/8 sheet rock, which weighs around 2.3 pounds per square foot per layer - for this reason, we need to use a minimum of 10 PSF dead load when calculating spans - with that in mind - 24" centers, 20 live, 10 dead load, using Douglas fir #2 and better (also called standard and better) spans - 2x4 - 7 ft. 3 in. 2x6 - 10 ft. 8 in. 2x8 - 13 ft. 6 in. 2x10 - 16 ft. 5 in. 2x12 - 19 ft. 1 in. above, with 16" centers instead of 24" - 2x4 - 8 ft. 11 in. 2x6 - 13 ft. 0 in. 2x8 - 16 ft. 6 in. 2x10 - 20 ft. 2 in. 2x12 - 23 ft. 4 in. It's possible to use 12" centers for longer spans, not common so I'll just give one example - 2x12, std + better, 20/10 load, ceiling joists, 12" on center - span max is 27 ft. 0 in. - (for minimal headroom, using 12" centers with 2x4's would span 9'10", 2x6's would span 15 ft. 0 in. Hope that helped... Steve

There are times when a single ceiling makes more sense, such as when it's more important to minimise sound getting upstairs than to have max isolation between rooms within the studio - although, here's a way to get most of the best of both - check the drawing about 1/3 the way down this page - http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... c&start=75 This is by no means the ONLY way to build, Rod prefers alternating ceiling and wall layers while leaving the same gaps and filling with acoustic caulk - my drawing might be a bit more forgiving of ceiling joist sag, while Rod's is undoubtedly more structurally sound. Either will work, the main thing is not to violate the mass-air-mass rule of isolation - more on that in the sticky section titled "complete section" - Floor coverings - nothing's cheap any more. Normally you want a more live floor and dead ceilings (mic interference, ceiling reflections to mix position, etc) - Since you're floating a floor, probably the least expensive would be vinyl floor covering (with a throw rug or two if necessary) BTW, don't forget that a floated floor makes a really good drum head if not damped - it needs to be stuffed with insulation or sand (ask Rod a lot more questions BEFORE construction if you decide on sand filling) Hope that helps... knightfly28 June 2004#18
A floated floor can vibrate at its own resonant frequency, which is likely to be in the audible range - just like a drum head does. To stop this from happening, there needs to be either sand or insulation pressing against the underside of the floated floor panels, so they don't vibrate. Can you do a basic drawing of your wall where the electrical panel is? Show type of wall construction (concrete, hollow block, stud frame, etc) and where in the room the panel is located... Steve
