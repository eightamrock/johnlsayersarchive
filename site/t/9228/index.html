Scott, What's the definition of industrial steel? How thick do you think the galva, pre-painted or skin-coated sheet is used on the out and inside of these steel buildings? 0.63 mm to 0.75 mm is a standard for outside. That material goes from roughly 0.6 mm to 1.2 mm, the latter which are already heavy executions for larger span-widths or higher loads. The inside skin of the building is often thinner than the outside. The walls and roof of such types of buildings get their strength trough the designs (profiling) of this corrugated steel and the shape and profiling of the inner boxes. The whole idea is based on the fact that one can easily roll-form steel sheet (even pre-painted or pre-coated) to give it strength by it's shape in function of span-width and load, with very thin sheet. The design makes them mainly strong, not the steel thickness (plays part of it of course). American standards may deviate somewhat with imperial measures. Corrugated steel has also very strange looking TL curves, since it has an extreme broad coincidence range, defined by the stiffness, and which is asymmetric in function of direction. If I made a comment about this, is because I know about it. Wall Panels Inside structural liner trays can have a bit thicker steel sheets, but still thin stuff. Roof panels If interested you can have a look to the above links and browse through this site, to have an idea about corrugated steel, finishes and thicknesses for industrial steel building. There are more of these producers of course, since you find these kind of factories world-wide.

rod gervais15 August 2007#15
I am not talking the cheap - buy it at home despot (or wall mart) - sheds - but rather commercial steel structures - constructed using columns/beams @ 20 to 50' centers, structural purlins - that are typically shelled using blanket type insulation installed outside of the purlins - with a steel skin then installed over that, If you begin with that type of a structure (understanding that everything you do beyond that point HAS TO BE a part of the structural design as well)- you can construct the basic frame - infill the outside of the building (between the purlins( with rigid structural steel framing - sheath the building with multiple layers of 5/8"exterior grade gypsum sheathing (I am speaking about densglas here) - cover the metal deck roof with 4 to 6" of lightweight concrete - on the inside of the building add additioanl purlins (on 10' centers) - well inboard of the exterior members - utilizing rubber isolators to break flanking connections to the steeel columns - install hat channels running vertically - then skining this with multiple layers of drywall - liberal use of insulation within the cavity is recommended - in the case of the movie studio i constructed - the cavity was 18"to 24" (depending onlocation) with 6" of therma fiber insulation installed in the outer steel stud frame - and an additional 6" of insulation installed inboard of that, leaving a 6" open air cavity. The concept for this particular building was a very rigid exterior wall assembly - with a very floppy interior face (hat channels supported on 10' centers makes for a very floppy wall. the buiding exterior is then finished with standard steel siding - take special care at roof to wall junctures - making this air tight is doable - but a challenge none the less - Inside - below the roof - steel netting supported 24" of fiberglass insulation - plus another 2" of rigid insulation - and the walls were covered n their entirety with 2" of rigid insulation. Remember - this was a 15,000 s.f. movie studio - 40' clear to the underside of trusses - 50' clear to underside of roof/ the room has a fantastic sound - but is dead enough to make moie shoots work....... Bands love to use this space to prepare for world tours due to the clear height - which is perfect to practice hanging their show - as well as the room sound for the band's practice sessions...... For recording studios this would have to be well thought out to imcorporate the room within a room concept - but could worl perfectly well....... We have had shootsin side the studio with clean power provided by huge portable generators sitting just outside the building - and no noise problems within........ and yes - those units are noisy when they're fire up...... Steven Speilburg told me that this was the only movie stduio he had ever shot in that he did not have to re-dub any of the audio - and it sits 100 yeards from a very busy highway........... one that has a ow of lf rumble from big rigs taking off due to the fact that there are weighing stations sitting immediately to the left and right of the site........ So it can be done - very effectively - but it costs a whole lot of money to do it right. Rod

TukarStudio wrote:
Has anyone else had problems with contractors calling back about estimates? Are there any secrets to finding a good contractor?
It's hard to get good, reliable, experienced, reasonably affordable contractors to take interest in relatively small jobs like the ones that are common on this forum, especially in a strong economy. Case in point: I had a heck of a time finding a concrete contractor who was willing to work with me on my terms (which were really reasonable: "Be licensed by the State Contractors' License Board, be insured, have no employees or carry workers' compensation insurance, return my calls, let me do what I can myself to save costs, and in return I'll be very accommodating with your schedule"). I finally found a great one via someone I knew who has years of experience working as a manager for a large residential builder in the area. My advice: talk to as many people as you can who have had work done on the same scale as what you want to have done, find out who did their work, and whether or not they were completely satisfied. "Word of mouth" referrals are the best advertising. When I'm happy with the service of a company, I tell gobs of people. (The same is true when the opposite occurs! :evil:) Be aware that there seems to be an inverse relationship between contractors who appear to be hungry for your business and contractors who actually deserve your business! :evil: If that weren't the case, I suspect more of us would be hiring people to do our projects for us! ;-) --Keith :mrgreen:
