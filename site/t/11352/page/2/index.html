Soundman202027 November 2008#32
garlo wrote:
I have nt the solution to decouple walls & roof structure except my cork.
Well, some folks here did explain that to you: Just don't attach the walls to the ceiling! Leave a gap between the top of the walls and the roof. Only attach the inner walls to each other (so they don't fall over!), and install a proper ceiling on top of the walls, but don't attach them to the roof at all. But it looks like you decided to not follow that advice, which is why lilith_envy said that your roof is going to sing like a choir. I'm not an expert here, like he is, but from what I've learned here if your walls are coupled to your roof then all of the sound vibration picked up by your walls will be transmitted directly to the roof, which will vibrate in unison. So sound will leak out of your studio through the wall-roof connection. And the reverse is also true: Any sound vibration from the outside that is picked up by your roof, such as the rain that you mentioned as already being a problem, will be transmitted into your walls, which will vibrate in unison. In other words, the rain sound on your roof (and all other sounds) will still be heard inside your room, due to the direct connection ("flanking path"). It does not matter how good your ceiling is: the sound is not going to go through your ceiling, and will go around it, through the structure, so your ceiling cannot block it. The sound is going to move through the direct mechanical connection between the roof and the walls, and your ceiling cannot do anything about that. Your wall will vibrate along with the roof, and you will hear the rain sound (and other sounds) coming out of your walls, not through your ceiling. Now, the way I see it, this may or may not be a big problem, depending on many factors, but mostly on how much noise you are prepared to put up with. However, you did specify 60 dB, but I don't see how you can get 60 dB of isolation when you have a direct flanking path with a solid coupling between the outer leaf (metal roof) and inner leaf (walls). From what I've read, I doubt that you'll get much better than maybe 40 or perhaps 45 dB (CR to outside), and even less in the low frequency range. Are you using RC to decouple your gypsum board on the walls, or are you screwing it directly to the studs?
Sorry thats 60dBbetween CR & studio & 50dB between CR & voice studio ( voice studio is mainly used for voice over ...)
OK, that's what I figured. Do you mean that you want an STC of 60, or that you want 60 dB of transmission loss? Or something else?
I hope my new ceiling with 20cm of insulation & 2 x13mm GB will help.
It will help a bit, but it cannot stop the sound that is going around it. It can only stop sound that is going through it, but your problem is going to be that sound will pass through the structure itself, not through the ceiling. So even if you had an infinitely perfect ceiling, with 120 dB of isolation from 20 Hz to 20 kHz (impossible, of course), even if you had that, it still would not help, since the sound will be in the walls, not the ceiling. In your current situation, the wall-roof coupling is your limiting factor. Unless you can fix that, it doesn't make much sense to build excellent ceilings or walls or anything else. I don't know how to calculate how much flanking you will get through your direct steel-to-steel-to-gypsumboard connection, but that is the isolation limit on your construction, so you don't need to waste money on building the walls and ceiling to be much better than that limit. Maybe the experts here can estimate some realistic numbers for you, but that's beyond my current knowledge level. Once again, I'm not an expert on this, so I'm just telling you what I've learned about it from the real experts over the last several months. - Stuart -

Soundman20206 December 2008#44
the risk of sound vibration from wall to the roof structure just by few screws is limited,
Actually, that simply isn't true. That's like saying "It doesn't matter if I leave the door open just a little bit: The risk of sound coming out is limited." Or "It doesn't matter if I leave a hole in the wall: the risk of sound coming through that hole is limited.". Or "It doesn't matter if there is a hole in the bucket: the risk of water coming out is limited". The truth is, even one single screw is enough to damage the isolation. If you don't understand what those "few screws" will do to your isolation, then I strongly suggest that you try this so that you get the idea: Get a tuning fork from someone (the old fashioned kind, used by doctors and musicians years ago), and tap it on the table so that it vibrates. Hold it out in front of you, a few inches away from your face. That is what your isolation will be like if you decouple properly. Now tap the tuning fork again, and this time put the base of the tuning fork directly on the table. That is what your isolation will be like if you have screws coupling the two structures. It is a HUGE difference. Anyone who says it doesn't matter, either doesn't understand even basic acoustics, or didn't understand your question. I STRONGLY suggest that you try this yourself, so that you understand the difference. I think you will be shocked.
I put 10mm rubber now ( normally used to go under big printing machine to decouple from the floor).
Did you do a compression test on the rubber, and did you do the calculations, taking into account the surface area and the final mass of whatever you are building on top of it? If not, then the rubber is probably no use. If you don't put enough weight on it to get the correct compression, then it will act like a solid, and will not decouple. If you put too much weight on it, so that it compresses completely, then it will act like a solid, and will not decouple. You can't just throw in any old piece of rubber and assume that it will work in YOUR application, just because it worked somewhere else. You need to do the math, and make sure. You need to know how much it must compresses under what loading, and how to get that loading with your construction. If you don't do that, then it's a waste of time and money. You might get lucky, but chances are that you won't. Either your construction will be too heavy and over-compress the rubber, or it will be too light and not compress it enough. Either way, it won't work. And of course, if you put screws or bolts through it, so that they connect directly from one side to the other, then the rubber will not decouple at all, and will be totally useless.
my method is a good & only one due to the fact that I am alone.
I don't understand why being alone is a problem for building two wall frames at a right angle! Nor for adding two more, and putting beams across the top. I've done that alone, several times. It's not hard, if you plan it correctly. Framing walls in wood or in steel is pretty much the same, if you know what you are doing. And if not, then you probably should not be doing it alone!
due to the fact that the ceiling will not be flat & is still not designed

How did you know how strong to build the walls, if you don't yet know what the weight of the ceiling will be?

I don t understand why you think that the ceiling can t be hung on the structure.
Because the structure that you have there is not strong enough to support the weight. 5/8 Gypsum board weighs about 12.5 kg per square meter. You said you are going to use two layers. Your plan shows about 75 square meters. That means that you are planning to hang nearly two thousand kilograms of gypsum board, plus the steel, screws, caulk, mud, paint etc. Plus insulation, lighting, cabling, etc. A 100 mm layer of insulation alone is another 400 kg. At a rough guess, you are planning to hang around 2,500 to 3,000 kg of mass from that roof, in addition to what it is already supporting. What xSpace is saying (and he knows about construction) is that your roof is not strong enough to support that amount of weight. He is warning you that your roof will probably collapse.
