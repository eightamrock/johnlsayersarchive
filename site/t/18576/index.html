Welcome to the forum Stan! Okay, first a disclaimer: I am just an avid reader, rather than a practising acoustician. I am sure some of the others will be able to chime in, if I put anything wrong. I would choose your second layout for these reasons: - Speakers firing along the long wall (preferable) - More symmetry with speakers and boundaries. In acoustic terms your room is small, which has some implications on how you would treat it: - Absorption is your friend. - The lowest room modes are around 45Hz and 67 Hz, and above 80Hz where they are more dense. - You may need specialised traps to handle 45Hz & 67Hz. - The back wall is too close to the listening position for diffusion. I would make the back wall as absorptive as possible I would suggest you have a look at the following topics: SBIR - http://www.genelec.com/learning-center/ ... cellation/ Speaker placement - http://www.genelec.com/learning-center/ ... estanding/ First reflections and RFZ design And this if you are willing to experiment with a more complicated design to tame the low modes: http://www.gearslutz.com/board/bass-tra ... rbers.html In you diagram you have added yellow 'treatment'. It is important that you specify what this is exactly. Colm

Like you see i have decided to mount speakers in the wall because i hope that those cavities in corners mass filled with rockwool will little help to reduce radiation low frequencies in to room as my room has bad size ratio.
The basic concept is correct: "flush mounting" your speakers like that is a very, very good idea, since it eliminates many of the problems caused by having speakers close to walls. This is called "flush mounting" and also "soffit mounting", and is excellent. So you have the right idea. However, there are several problems with the way you show it in that diagram: First, the front panel of the soffit must be totally flat: It cannot have those "webbing" shaped things sticking out around the speaker. The reason why this is called "flush mounting" is because the front face of the speaker must be totally "flush" with the front face of the soffit. Acoustically, then become one single panel that acts like an "infinite baffle". So even though your webbed design looks really nice, it would be a disaster acoustically. That needs to be fixed: the front panel of the soffit must be perfectly in line with the front panel of the speaker itself. Basically, the soffit panel is an extension of the speaker box. Next is the tilt angle and height: you show your speakers raised up very high and titled down at a steep angle: once again, that's a bad idea acoustically, even though it looks nice. The acoustic center of the speaker should be located 1.2m above the floor, and the speakers should not be tilted at all. There is no reason to tilt them. Tilting your speakers creates many, many problems acoustically, and should be avoided if possible. If you really have no choice and HAVE to tilt them, then the tilt angle should NEVER be more than 10°, and preferably no more than 7°. I have never tilted speakers more than 5°, since it just complicates things so much. So that needs to eb fixed too.
Here 2D plan of new walls size plus angles and their main First reflection.

You have seven different angled pieces there, from your front wall to your side walls: I never use more than five, and I try to keep it down to three whenever possible, to simplify construction, and also to make the soffit surfaces as large as possible.

Is there a reason you have your soffits at 35° angle instead of the usual 30° needed for an equilateral triangle?
The equilateral triangle is just an ideal, but is not written in stone. You can adjust the angle if you have to, to make the rest of the room geometry work out. You can go up to as much as 45° in extreme cases, but it's probably not a good idea to go beyond 40°, as you start to get "head shadowing" effects beyond that in small rooms. But 35° is fine. - Stuart -

Soundman20203 December 2013#16
If i would not move those up they will be covered by NS10's because there is not enough space to leave both on side of each other at the same level.
There are physical limitations to what can be done acoustically in any given room. The smaller the room is, the fewer options you have, and sometimes you have no choice but to sacrifice something. Do you REALLY need the NS-10s in there? Sure, you do need to check your mixes on those, since they are a great reflection of typical poor home speakers, but you could do that just as well in another room...
Do you have any suggestion how to sort positioning of both pairs of monitors?
The only other possibility I can think of is to separate the NS10s as far apart as they will go, and increase the toe-in angle accordingly, so they no longer interfere with your mains. However, in a room that small, they ARE going to interfere with your mains, no matter where you put them, simply because of their size and location. Even they way you had them, down below the mains, is going to be a big problem, since they are in the direct path of the sound from your mains: there would be major edge diffraction, reflections, phasing, and comb filtering going on all around them, and no chance of clear sound from the mains. If your room were larger, then there would be places that you could put them, but in a small room like that, there really just isn't any place you can put them that does not cause distortion for the sound coming form your main speakers. - Stuart -
