Luftweg20 October 2006#79
The way I see it is that both Keith AND TomVan have made very good points... It MAY be very likely that Audio Alloy paid for their own testing... I'm a pharmacist, and I can tell you that a great deal of drug studies are paid for -- at least partially -- by the manufacturer(s) of the drug(s) that get tested. In professional peer-reviewed journals, the funding for the study HAS to be disclosed (actually, EVERYTHING about the way any study is written and submitted must follow specific guidelines before being accepted and published -- this is why MD's, PharmD's, PhD's, etc., only take seriously peer-reviewed journals). When I was interning and presenting new articles to healthcare professionals, not very many of them were ever impressed much with 'who funded the study'. Supposedly, the journal has researched whether any article had any 'improprieties' with the way it was funded (and remember, these are the 'peers', i.e., fellow medical/science professionals); if anything was found improper, the study would have been rejected for publishing (the study could then make changes and try to resubmit, subject to the same scrutiny again, and so on). SO, one big thing to look at with any study is WHERE it was published.... If it was in the NEJM (New England Journal of Medicine) or in the 'World News' tabloid makes a big difference; the NEJM won't accept a questionable study, whereas the World News could care less (or may actually WANT it to be sketchy). Here, a peer-reviewed engineering journal would be where to look for the most reputable info. A company that only publishes its own study (whether self-conducted, or through a third party) in its own advertising is NOT using a peer-reviewed process -- but that's still not to say that claims might not be totally legit. As far as what Green Glue actually is -- or has to be chemically -- is still up for debate as far as I feel. Clearly it has to have certain static physical properties, and fluid dynamic properties, but that likely leaves a whole world of chemical possibilities in order to achieve those properties... i.e., there's most probably no unique 'magic' set of chemicals that can accomplish the same thing... I've briefly thought of other materials that may substitute for GG... Dielectric (silicone) grease came to mind (this is NOT like the silicone caulkings, but rather remains a viscous liquid); alas, this stuff -- even in bulk -- seems to be more expensive than Green Glue... And even though it may have the functional physical properties of GG, it may or may not have some others that are required to have it be 'acceptable' to be put between sheetrock layers... tests would have to be done. But, in a sense, GG is a 'blind' item; it's almost impossible for anyone who is constructing a studio to 'test' whether it performed as it should, or as claimed (i.e, no one's gonna put the walls up first without it, run tests, and then tear it all down and put the walls up again WITH GG... and run more tests!). So, with that understanding, most testimonials can't seem to hold much validity, and one has to rely on lab test data. K

Considering that probably 90% of the world's population seems to be made up of thieves and murderers these days (OK, maybe a small exaggeration) I wouldn't give up proprietary information EITHER - of all the acoustic companies out there today, Brian at GG has arguably been the most helpful, insightful, and prolific THIRD PARTY tester I'm aware of. If you check their site, you'll see that ALL the published wall tests were done by an independent acoustic lab with all the i's dotted and t's crossed. Seeing as how Walmart doesn't offer such a service and there doesn't seem to be a "Wall-test-mart" chain sprouting up yet (as in, there are very few accredited labs throughout the world) I'm not sure what else GG could do to show what their product is capable of. I asked Brian in another thread whether they'd done any double frame wall tests, all I see so far are single frames with/without RC, sound board, MLV, etc - I agree that Auralex is playing it awfully close to the vest with the U-boat Durometer secret - I couldn't get anything out of Jeff while he was employed there either. If, however, one were to do as Chris is doing, buy a couple and TEST them, then I see no reason why they wouldn't work as well as any OTHER source of EPDM for a LIMITED WEIGHT, non-concrete floor such as Chris requires - When you have a weight limitation such as this, the only way I know of that can help compensate is to increase the air gap and make sure the floated floor is as heavy as you can make it under the safety constraints, as DAMPED as you can make it (GG would be an EXCELLENT inter-layer for this) including a slightly compressed insulation fill for even MORE damping - and understand that this won't make it PERFECT, only BETTER than WITHOUT these techniques. Obviously (see my description of the mega-press at my work) if there's money enough and support enough, ANYTHING can be isolated to an acceptable degree; that's not what we're talking about here. With enough searching/calling, a cheaper source of EPDM can probably be found; but for convenience, the U-boats are possibly the most sensible FOR THIS APPLICATION, provided you do your own tests per my suggestions in Aaron's floating floor thread and others. Considering the number of "foam-by-mail" ripoffs in the acoustic jungle, I don't really blame Auralex for being tight-lipped - remember, it was discovered that at least one "sound-proofing foam" seller merely copied Auralex' specs and posted them for their own inferior, NON-flame-retardant product - this is one way to keep prices down and buyers up. Behringer uses these techniques all the time, which is one reason I think they're crap. Gotta go, my own "windmill" is calling, and like Don Quixote I'm committed... Steve
