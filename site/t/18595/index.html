Hi Anthony, and sorry about the delay! And also Welcome to the forum!
and doesn't care if i go until midnight drumming or mixing .. Of course in 3 years if he's married and moved out I'm going to have make sure the place is as soundproof as possible.
That would imply that in 3 years you'd have to rip out everything you do now, in order to be able to soundproof the place... :) You cannot soundproof after the acoustic treatment is done: the sound proofing has to go in first, then the treatment afterwards. In other words, if you think you might have to isolate ("soundproof") at some point in the future, then you should do it now instead, and save yourself a of of money and time!
Obviously the bulk of that responsibility will fall on the ceiling, but before I get there I wanted to know if anyone had any insight on pouring a layer of light concrete as the foundation on this floor.
What is the floor made of right now? If it is already concrete then you probably do not need to add more mass to it. But if it is wood, then you have the issue of structural integrity: is the existing structure able to safely support the huge added mass of a layer of concrete? Only a structural engineer can answer that question, so now would be a good time to hire one!
Sp the real question is ... would adding the light concrete help with 1. isolation from room to room within the studio and/or 2. isolation to the floor above ?
If you are going to go to all that trouble and expense, and you really want exceptional isolation, then you might as well float the to floors independently. That said, you should read this thread first, to understand all the implications: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8173
Before any serious construction begins I do plan on having a structural engineer out to give me a run down
Yes! Very smart move!
When the studio space is not on the 1st floor, does adding a light concrete layer first, then building a floating floor on top help at all ?
Yes, it absolutely can make a big difference. The issue normally is cost, and also deciding if you even need to do it. I'd suggest that you buy yourself a sound level meter and do some tests to see how much isolation you are getting at present, and how much more you need. Based on that, you can decide if there is a concrete truck in your near future, or not.....
I can actually acquire the concrete for free so it's not so much the expense with this step.
Free concrete is good! But even tough the concrete itself might be free, the laying of the form work, the planning, design, structural calculations, acoustic calculations, floor jacks, labor, inspection fees, permits, and many other factors won't be. The cost of the actual "wet gray stuff" is only part of the equation.
