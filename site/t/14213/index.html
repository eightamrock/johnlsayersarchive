Soundman202023 March 2010#22
But which Shape is better? i've seen so many designs of studios but the ceiling seems to vary in all of them
I'd go with #2! The angled ceiling helps with both flutter echo and your reflection free zone. The ideal angle is 12 degrees, and it looks like you have plenty of height to do that, from your comments to john. Also, try to make your ceiling as high as you can get it! More height is better, acoustically. (Of course, make sure that you don't end up with lousy dimensions or a bad ratio). As long as you have at least 4 inches of air gap at the closest point between your ceiling an the floor above, you should be OK. If you can spare the space, go for 8 inches at the closest point: it will improve your isolation.
6 inch gap insulation framed by 2x4 double drywall then more insulation framed by a 2x1 and covered with cloth?
Partly correct: What it should really be is: - 6 inch gap filled entirely with insulation. (This 6" can include the stud space) - Two layers of 5/8 (15mm) drywall, preferably with Green Glue in between - Acoustic treatment, as necessary (which might or might not include cloth-covered 703 framed with 2x1).
But maybe the inside one would benefit to be angled down to avoid standing waves within the room?
Nope! You do not need to angle your glass for acoustic reasons. That's a myth. You might want to angle it for aesthetic reasons, such as to avoid glare and reflections from you lights, but acoustically there is no advantage at all to angling glass: It just reduces your isolation.
Should the double ceiling then be starting at 7' (5 inch wall, 6 inch airspace, 5 inch wall) making the roof to the studio end at approx 8.5 feet, leaving another 2 feet to the beams and 3.5 feet to the bottom of the floor above?
You don't need 2 feet: 8 inches is probably good enough, unless you have REALLY loud neighbors (or you plan to be a really loud neighbor yourself!).
Should the ceiling inside the studio be angled or "stepped" up from front wall to back wall?
Sloped. See above. Helps deal with flutter and necessary for RFZ.
will using the brick wall as the outside studio wall not potentially give me problems?
Nope! It will REMOVE your problems! See below...
previously i had the brick neighbour dividing wall, then two double leaf walls (brick - 3 inch gap - 5 inch double leaf - six inch gap - 5 inch double leaf) essentially creating an outside room that only touches the floor, then an independent room inside that.
That would have been a three-leaf system: Bad idea! You also seem to be confusing your air gaps and stud spaces: it's the same thing! the space between the studs IS your air gap. so if you have 2x4 studs set two inches away from the brick wall, then you have nearly SIX inches of air gap: the studs space, plus the 2 inches. All 6 iof of those inches are your air gap, and all 6 of those inches should be filled with insulation (mineral wool or fiberglass). But getting back to the point: between you and your neighbors you do NOT want more than two leaves: You want two leaves, and ONLY two leaves. Not one, not three, not more, not less. Two and only two. One leaf is already there (the brick wall) so you only need one additional leaf: A stud frame with a couple of layers of drywall on only ONE side of it. A 3-leaf wall will do much better job of isolating high frequencies than a 2-leaf, but it does a much WORSE job at isolating low frequencies, and a 2-leaf is already really good at isolating highs anyway. So contrary to intuition and Internet myth, a wall with three leaves in it is a bad idea (unless you really know what you are doing).
Here's my guess for the angle of the roof leaves,
You don't need to guess! 12 degrees, at least, is what you need. Also, your walls need to be splayed at least 12 degrees ( 6 degrees per side ), but you already seem to have that right. The exact splay angle depends on your speaker geometry, but you need at least 6 degrees per side.
