This whole time i thought there was a six inch completely free air space between two walls (each wall comprising of studs, insulation and two slabs of drywall on only ONE side :) ) - so essentially you would have two sets of insulation staring at each other over a six inch "air only" gap. I thought the whole point of room within a room was to have that air only gap to completely disconnect the walls, but i'm gathering that insulation between them, touching both walls does not count as a connection, is this correct?
Yeah, that gets many people confused! That's why I prefer the term "MSM" (Mass-Spring-Mass) to "MAM" (Mass-Air-Mass): less confusion The basic components are your "Mass" of the one leaf (usually a couple of layers of drywall, the "Mass of the the other leaf (ditto), then the "Spring" (the air space between the two masses, which is filled partially or totally with insulation). The spring is actually a damped spring: the spring is the air itself, and the damping is the insulation. They occupy the same physical space. It is kind of like the way the suspension works on some cars, where the spring is a metal spiral and the shock absorber (=damper)runs down the middle of the spring: they are both in the same place, when you think about it. The frame that holds up each "mass" is only there to hold it up, and it can go on ether side of the mass: it doesn't matter if you put the frames inside the air gap, or outside the air gaps, or one inside and one outside. There only purpose is to provide support. You measure the air gap (spring) between the masses, regardless of where the frames are. So the air gap is just the distance between inner surfaces of the two leaves. That gap needs to be at least 4" to give you decent isolation, and more if you can afford it. And yes, you should try to fill the air gap completely with insulation, if you can. No it does not create a mechanical link, provided that it is the correct density (48 kg/m3) and that you don't jam it in tight. You just want it to fit in, with being compressed. If you shove it in too tight, compressing it, then it DOES create a flanking path, but if you just place it in, using exactly 4" of insulation for a 4" air gap, then you are fine. Of course, leaving a small gap without insulation won't do any harm, so if you have a 7" gap and can only find 6" insulation, then that inch isn't going to be a big deal. But in general, try to fill it as much as you can. You want as much damping as you can get in the wall. Those diagrams show a common way of almost filling the cavity using standard insulation and studs, since that is what is easily available, I guess, but studies show that you get better performance by filling the cavity fully. I don't recall the exact figures, but if you do not put ANY insulation at all in the cavity you lose up to about 16 dB of isolation, depending on the wall. Half-filling the cavity gains you about 10 dB (IIRC), 3/4 full takes it to 14 db, and the last bit takes it to 16. I think that was for an 8" gap with 2 layers of 5/8 drywall on each leaf, but I'm not 100% certain, so don't quote me on that! It's been a while since I read that paper...

Yup! Sounds about right. That will give you nearly 6 inches of "S" in your MSM equation, which is pretty good. But don't forget that with 2x4 studs you don't actually get 4 inches of depth, so you can't put a 4 inch layer of insulation between the studs and also a 2" layer in the remaining gap, as you'd have to compress the insulation a bit to do that. 4" of insulation will stick out a bit from between "2x4" studs. So if you make your 2" 'air pocket' a bit deeper, say 2 3/4, then you will be fine.
It's funny because i've read everywhere to go with two leaf, and silly me never even thought that the brick would be considered one of the leaves!
Yup! And brick is a pretty good leaf too! It is very dense (much denser than drywall), so it will be giving you good isolation already. That, plus a 6" gap, plus 2 sheets of 5/8 drywall should give you great isolation. One word of warning though: Seal that brick! Paint it, or plaster it, or stucco it, but do something to it to seal the porous surface. Your leaves MUST be sealed airtight, and brick isn't very airtight on its own. The surface needs sealing. Important!

Well, partial is better than none! So you have two choices: You can either just do as much f the ceiling as you can at 12°, from the front wall to at least as far back as the mix position, then reduce or flatten it out completely the rest of the way back. Or you can do the entire ceiling at a lesser angle. (Not sure which is better, acoustically, but I'm inclined to prefer the "front part at 12°, rear part at lesser angle" approach.) - Stuart -
