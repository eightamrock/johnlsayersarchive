Soundman202030 March 2009#7
Well, I've read many good, solid reasons for keeping a dead ceiling and a live floor, and you can find an interesting discussion about that on Ethan's web site, but the one that makes the most sense to me is that wherever you go in the world, your ears are always the exact same height above the floor, so your brain is very, very accustomed to hearing reflections off the floor, and uses that as an acoustic clue. The distance to the ceiling varies all the time, as you walk around, so your brain pretty much ignores that. So we, as humans, always expect to hear a certain kind of acoustic clue in our music that indicates the floor is "there", where it should be, and when that sound is absent, the music sounds vaguely strange. That's the argument that makes the most acoustic sense to me. So, I guess the alternative for you, if you have zero choice about carpet, is to build your ceiling at the same distance above your head as the floor normally is, and always keep your mics at the same distance from the ceiling as they normally would be from the floor, but that sure sounds like a complicated way of doing things! I really can't say how important this is, but practically all the good studios I've seen and been in always have live floors and dead ceilings. If that is just ordinary carpet, then the acoustic characteristics are probably unknown and unpublished, so you would basically be on your own trying to figure out what it will do to your room acoustics, and how bad it will make it. Normal house / office carpet is selective absorptive and selectively reflective, at different frequencies, but unless the manufacturer publishes acoustic data (unlikely!), then you really don't have much clue as to what it will do. My guess would be that it will make your room very "cavey" and "dull" and "hollow" sounding, since it will absorb some highs and not others, at varying levels, while not doing anything at all to most bass frequencies. But check out Ethan's site for that discussion on room treatment. - Stuart -

Soundman202031 March 2009#9
hat about some thin material or panels that could be dropped on the floor just prior recording to add back some amount of reflectivity,
Sure. that would work. I would suggest something like plywood or MDF, though, as it is more massive, and would "hide" the carpet a bit better, in the acoustical sense. It is also more durable than plastic, and gives you a better surface to walk on. You could cut one large sheet of plywood into several smaller pieces that fit together, to make it easier to handle, and shape them to fit around furniture, etc. Once the carpet is in, you might want to experiment a bit with different amounts of plywood coverage, until the room sounds good. For example, start with 25% of the floor covered, maybe in the middle of the floor, and see how that sounds. Then add another piece to get maybe 40%, and see if that improved things. You don't want to go too far the other way, either! You say you already have a strange sounding room, due to those ducts, so you probably don't make it too live. Just play around with coverage until the room sounds as natural as possible, with as little reverberation and resonance as you can get while still getting good sound. Trial and error os what you need, I think! - Stuart -

Soundman202031 March 2009#11
Though just for clarity's sake, what exactly is MDF? I'm intrigued...
MDF="Medium Density Fiberboard". It's a type of manufactured wood, made from wood particles in a resin base, and pressed into shape to form large sheets. A lot of cheap furniture is made from that kind of manufactured wood these days. It might be slightly cheaper than plywood in your area, or more expensive. It varies from place to place, I think. It probably won't hold up as well as plywood in relation to wear over the years, but it will still work. One thing I forgot to mention: don't forget to treat your ceiling! If you have a live floor, you should have a "dead" ceiling. It will give your room a more "spacious" sound, acoustically (making it sound as though the ceiling were much higher than it actually is), and also helps to control flutter echo and comb filtering. A few inches of fiberglass or mineral wool should do the trick. - Stuart -

Soundman20202 April 2009#13
Pretty much the same, I'd say. But you do not want to paint or glue insulation! That will kill the acoustic properties if the painted / glued part. It works because it is porous, and the fibers can vibrate along with the sound to absorb the sound and turn it into low-grade heat. If you paint it / glue it, then the fibers cannot move, air cannot penetrate, and it loses acoustical characteristics. What you can do in the ceiling is to put it between the joists, if you build the ceiling inside-out, and use something like string, package strapping, or wire mesh stapled across the joists, with acoustic cloth below that to hide the "ugly factor". - Stuart -

Soundman202011 April 2009#15
Is it going to be worthwhile to make bass traps in that corner with only 2 ft wide (60.96 cm) to work with?
Well, 60 cm is better than nothing! It should still get you a decent amount of bass trapping.
I could maybe add more space by making the triangle lopsided giving it a longer base, but would that in anyway effect it's ability to absorb bass for the worse?
I don't think it would have a negative effect. If it will increase the surface area and depth of the trap, then it can only help, as far as I can see.
Do square bass traps work the same as ones straddled across a corner?
Do you mean "square" as in "up against the wall" instead of straddling the corner? That will still work, but it won't be as effective. Room modes are concentrated in the corners, so you get a free boost in effectiveness by straddling corners. A wall gives you a 6 db Boost, the corner between two walls gives a 12 dB boost, and the "tri-corners" (where two walls and the ceiling meet, or two walls and the floor) give you an 18 dB boost, theoretically. So straddling tri-corners should be your first priority, followed by normal corners, and last of all just up against a wall. - Stuart -
