Soundman20202 February 2012#4
and also to rent the space out (eventually in the future).
That would make it a commercial facility, legally. So there's a bunch of requirements that you'd need to consider in advance. You should probably check with your local municipality to find out what those are. One aspect will be wheel-chair access, ramps no stairs, wide doors, etc. You should consider that now, to avoid having to tear things out and re-do them later.
It's a really tight budget, I know, but US$ 1,500 is what Ben estimates he has to spend at this point
Unfortunately, I don't think your goals are consistent with your budget. What you'd like to do is going to cost an awful lot more than what you plan to spend. So either you should increase your budget (add a zero), or lower your sites on what you want to achieve.
I envision that this space will have to be constructed in phases as more funds become available.
Fair enough, so then let's talk about the TOTAL budget, over, for example, a period of one year. You still need to design for the final outcome, even if the money isn't there yet. With the budget you have now you can probably seal the room somewhat as one single room for now (not divided in two yet), get a start on isolating that, and put some very basic treatment in. That's about it.
I don't have a sound level meter yet. Do you have any suggestions on a reasonably priced, professional grade one?
E-bay, Amazon.com, etc. A decent basic meter will cost you around US$ 50-100. Professional ones go for about ten times that price, and more. But hte basic one is all you need. As long as it has both "A" and "C" weighting, and both fast/slow settings, that's about all you need.
Ideally NC 15-20, however, I doubt this is possible.
NC is a rating that's more used for figuring how quite the building systems are, such as HVAC. It's a curve, not a level, so isn't easy to calculate. It would be easier to just give a single decibel number for both how loud you are and how quiet you need to be.
Scenario #1 fits our program.
OK. So seal the existing walls airtight, beef up the ones that are just stud walls with more mass on top of the existing surface (NOT on the other side of the studs! Leave the studs open, as they are now). By "mass" I mean ordinary 5/8" drywall. Cheap and effective. The goal is to get all four walls sealed airtight, all the gaps, even the little tine ones, and to also have at least two layers of mass on them, on just ONE side of the studs. That includes the windows: Seal those so that they cannot be opened. That's your outer-leaf, ad is probably all you can afford to do right now. The inner leaf will have to come later.
There is not a plan for HVAC at this point.
:shock: There should be! Even if you don't plan to implement it yet, it's a very, very important part of the studio! You can't survive for long in an air-tight room... the "V" in HVAC is "ventilation": you need a plan for that which includes the acoustics of how to ventilate the room without letting sound out/in. So that's what you can do to start: Get the meter, get some readings, beef up and seal the existing outer-leaf wall and the windows, and see hoe much money you have left for basic treatment. - Stuart -

Soundman20206 February 2012#6
Thanks for the reply. Just purchased this sound level meter. It seems like a good choice ($70, "A and C" weighting, and has a fast/slow setting, conforms to the IEC651, ANSI S1.4 type 2 for sound level meters).
That looks fine: It will do the job.
1. Any recommendations on acoustic caulk? I know Auralex makes one but I was wondering if there is a more reasonably priced, generic brand that is sufficient.
Green Glue makes one as well, which is supposed to be good. USG makes one that is supposed to be really good, but is also messy, yuck stuff to handle. There are probably others, too. But if you are on a very tight budget and can't afford proper acoustic sealant, then ordinary household caulk is a possible substitute, provided that it is good quality and never hardens, remaining flexible, soft and rubbery even after it cures.
2. How do you propose I should seal the windows?
Two possibilities: If you want the daylight, then just bolt them closed and seal around the edges, twice: once with thin "weatherstrip" type rubber actually between the fixed and moving parts of the frame, then caulk all around the edges. Or if you don't need the daylight, then build a "plug", which is basically a large chunk of something massive (like plywood and drywall) that fits into the window frame, and seale tightly around the edges.
We had this idea of making essentially shutters for the windows that can open and close.
O doubt that shutters will work, since there is no way to seal them reliably. Acoustic isolation depends 100% on total hermetic air-tight seals. Even tine air gaps have a severe effect on isolation. It would not be easy to build shutters that could seal well enough to be of any use.
I guess they would be constructed similarly to an acoustic door (magnetic seal, bulb seals, etc.)
If you could figure out how to do that, then I suppose it is an option. But a removable plug would probably be easier. The main issue is that whatever you do has to be massive (very heavy), and has to be totally sealed. - Stuart -
