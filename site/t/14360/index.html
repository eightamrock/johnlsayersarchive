Soundman20209 May 2010#3
I agree with John: EQ has no place on monitor speakers at all. Apart from the possibility of causing phasing issues (if you don't have phase-linear equalizers), EQ usually only works for one specific location in a room anyway, since you are trying to compensate for room mode issues, and those vary according to your position in the room. Far better to build and treat the room properly to start with, so that no EQ is required. The only time I EQ rooms is when I run live sound in medium size venues, and that's usually more to deal with issues in the signal chain, rather than the room itself. In that case I aim for as flat a response as I can get, and use the console EQ for rolling htings off as required. Even for those venues, I prefer to try to minimize room issues by careful speaker placement, and only EQ as a last resort. But for a studio control room, EQ should never be needed. If it is needed, then there is something wrong with the room, or with the signal chain. - Stuart -

Soundman20209 May 2010#5
I'd be really interested to see the response curves of that room, as well as accurate waterfalls plot of the low end, since I just can't see how it can be that good of a room, based on the photos. Do you have a lot of hidden treatment in there? For example, I don't see any visible bass trapping, first-reflection-point absorption, or front wall treatment, and there seem to be several other issues, such as parallel surfaces, no soffits, etc. I'm really curious about the speakers, too: You say you have them "sitting in a cavity in the wall on a shelf and not mounted"? I guess that's what is behind the grey cloth covers on the front wall? Could you show some photos of how that was accomplished, since it is very likely the source of your equalization issues, given they way you describe it. All of your speakers have fairly flat responses, so should not need any EQ at all. The fact that you perceive the need to EQ them indicates one of three things: 1) The speakers are incorrectly mounted, and you have resonance, comb filtering, phasing, or reflection issues due to the mounting. 2) The room is not correctly designed / built / treated / set up to smooth out the modal response, give you a reflection free zone around the mix position, and treat the remaining issues that any room will have. What ratio is it based on? 3) There is an issue somewhere in your signal chain, from DAW to speaker, causing non-flat response. In all of the three cases, the solution is NOT to put an equalizer in the chain, but rather to fix the underlying issue. Trying to EQ away a problem is like sticking band-aids on a gun shot wound: it hides the "ugly" superficially, but doesn't solve the real problem, and the patient will die anyway. In any event, I've never heard of aiming for anything but flat response in a control room. It's practically impossible to achieve that, of course, but it is the holy grail that everyone shoots for. I can't see ANY reason to roll off either highs or lows on the monitoring system, as all that does is give the engineer a distorted picture of the mix, and his mixes won't translate well. If you roll off highs, your mixes will most likely end up overly bright. I HAVE heard of people rolling off highs in LISTENING rooms, such as home theaters and presentation rooms, and yes I've heard that they do that for ear fatigue, but once again that has no place in a studio control room. You shouldn't ever get ear fatigue in a CR anyway, if you are mixing at 85 dB in a properly set up room with good equipment. My suggestion would be to pull everything out of your final signal chain, going directly from your DAW to the speakers, play calibrated pink noise through your system, one speaker at a time, and measure the room response with a calibrated measurement mic, set exactly at your mix position to start with. Get accurate response curves, and accurate waterfall plots, and post them here, so the experts can take a look at your present room response, see what is wrong with it that is distorting the perception of how the speakers sound, and suggest the correct solutions for fixing your problem. Also draw up an accurate model of your room in SketchUp and post it here, showing the exact shape, dimensions, speaker positions angles and mountings, mix position, doors, windows, existing treatment, etc. Clearly, you have a major problem in your room, and it has been partially disguised with EQ up to now. This is your big chance to fix it properly, and turn it into a truly great room. - Stuart -

I am reading a different question than what John and Stuart are answering. A big part of the confusion may be beacuse wht is being asked about is not clearly specified. Their are 4 different responses that are connly confused when discussing room response. One is the output of the monitor system on axis, the second is the total power output of the monitors, the third is the repsonse of the room itself and fourth is the combined effect. Current practice is to have a flat response from the monitors, and a roll off on the high end in the room response. The end result is the monitor/room response that rolls off ont eh high end, while having flat response from the initial sounds. I hope this helps, and emphasizes monitor/room system imprtance. Andre

Soundman202012 May 2010#14
What my experience in studios has been (20-something years, mostly in post-production studios for radio and TV, all around South America) monitor response is kept flat, and rooms are not treated with enough absorption, which of course isn't a good combination anyway. The result, as you can imagine, is that mix engineers compensate subconsciously by rolling off their mixes slightly in the high end. For some reason, it works quite well. Mixes that come out of those rooms do seem to translate fine to TV screens in homes: I often hear mixes in progress when I visit my customers' studios, then hear them again a few days later at home, on the TV or radio, and they sound pretty well balanced to my ears. Why that works, I really can't tell you, but it does. I'm only aware of about half a dozen really good rooms in Santiago, which were properly designed and set up by qualified, experienced acousticians (there aren't too many of those around here!), and even in those rooms I haven't seen any roll-off on the monitors. 've seen BASS roll-off a number of times, but never highs. Not that I specifically checked for highs, but I don't recall seeing it. However, I will be sure to do so next time I visit those studios. Maybe it's just a Latin American thing, or maybe it's just a TV / radio thing, assuming that most consumer TV's and radios don't have good high end resposne anyway. I really don't know the reason behind it. Or maybe there ISN'T a reason, except for "we've always done it like that"(!), but that's the way it is here, and curiously it seems to work. Like I said before, I'm aware that roll-off is common for home theaters here (a friend of mine specializes in home theater design and installation), and I usually also end up doing some roll off when I run live sound in small and medium venues, but high roll-off isn't common in Latin American post-production studios in my experience, where "flat" is the way it is done. Maybe Javier can comment on his experience here. From what I recall of his setup, he does not roll of highs on his speakers either (he has both Adam A7s and NS-10s), and his mixes sound pretty good to my ears. But now that there is a doubt here, I reckon it's time for me to do some more research, to satisfy my own curiosity! - Stuart -
