Hi Mira Music. Please check the rules for posting: there is one you are missing! :) But here are some brief comments:
have to soundproof the garage
I'm just "splitting hairs" here, but "soundproofing" isn't the correct term. Soundproofing is a myth: impossible to achieve. All you can do is to isolate something to one degree or another, but you cannot "proof" a room against sound. Any sufficiently loud sound will get through any conceivable structure built by man. so the issue is to decide how much isolation you need. To do that, you need a sound level meter, and to take some real measurements, and put real numbers on them.
I have no experience with building techniques and will hire a contractor to help with this build.
Look for one who already has experience building studios! If not, then you'll waste a huge amount of time trying to educate him on how to do it right, and checking on him to make sure that he's using the techniques for studios, and not the normal techniques for houses, offices, shops.... Big difference. If he does it wrong, you will have wasted your money. When choosing the builder, ask simple questions that only someone who really knows can answer, such as "how will you decouple the two leaves of my MSM wall". If he looks at you with a blank stare, then turn him around and send him on his way, and call up the next guy on your list....
The door is being replaced with a fiberglass door (is this a good option or should I buy a wood door for them to use?).
Mass. That's the answer. The only way to isolate sound is to put very large amounts of mass in its way, with hermetic seals around the mass. So get the heaviest solid doors you can, and attach them in such a way that there are several layers of air-tight seals between the door and frame. The amount of mass and details of the seals will depend on the numbers you came up with, in Step One above: determining how much isolation you need.
The walls are exposed wood studs right now with about 14.5inches between them.
With construction like that, you don't refer to the stud spacing by the gap between the studs, but rather by the distance between the center of one stud and the center of the next stud. In your case, you have 16" OC spacing. "OC" means "On Center".
My options as I see it are… 1. Mineral wool, Isotrax, double layer of drywall with GreenGlue in between the layers. 2. Mineral wool, Isotrax, single layer of drywall. 3. Mineral wool, Isotrax, double layer of drywall, NO GreenGlue 4. Mineral wool, NO Isotrax, double layer of drywall with GreenGlue.
In order of usefulness for isolation: 1, 3, (2, 4). In other words, 1 is the best, 3 comes next, followed by 2 and 4 together at the tail end. 2 and 4 are basically worthless, hardly better than ordinary wall construction. 4 is not decoupled, 2 has insufficient mass. But even better would be to forget the Isotrax, and build a separate stud frame for your new inner leaf. Much better decoupling.
Perhaps the walls that have no-one near them need a little less soundproofing?
Nope. They all need the same, and so doe the ceiling, floor, doors, windows, HVAC system and electrical installation. Can you build an aquarium by only using glass on two sides, with cardboard, newspaper and carpet on the other sides? Obviously not. It won't hold water! The same with your room: sound is like water, and will take the easiest path out of the room. So the entire structure has to be isolated to the same level. The room is only as good as the weakest part. And once low-frequency sound is OUT of the room, it spreads uniformly in all directions. Low frequency sound is not directional.
I am also using very heavy duty theatre curtains on three walls.
No use at all for isolation. Contrary to popular belief, you cannot stop sound with porous fabric, carpet or egg crates. Your curtains might be useful to a certain extent for treating the room acoustically after it is isolated, but no use at all for isolating. Think of it this way: like I said before, sound is like water. Your curtains are like a sponge. Can you "treat" spilled water by mopping it up with a sponge? Yup, you sure can! But can you stop water coming out the tap by putting a sponge across it? Nope, you sure can't! Same with sound. Isolation and treatment are two different things.
I plan to seal the garage door top and bottom and sides with acoustic sealant and use mineral wool and drywall on the spaces around the door.
What is the mineral wool for only in the spaces around the door? ? If the door is already sealed, then the entire air cavity between it and your inner leaf must be filled completely around with mineral wool, not just parts of it.
I will buy a door soundproofing kit (MLV and some seals etc) for the access door.
Forget it. Marketing hype. MLV is just mass. Very EXPENSIVE mass! Sound waves don't care how much you paid for the mass: all they do is react to the amount of mass that they encounter in their path. They cannot read, so price tags are meaningless to them. The only thing they care about is how much mass they bump into, and how well sealed it is, period. MLV has very few uses in acoustics, although it does have some, and "sealing up the garage door" is not one of those uses. You need mass, period, so get the cheapest mass you can. Ordinary drywall fills the bill nicely. So do thick plywood, OSB, MDF, bricks, concrete and suchlike.
What is the next step in soundproofing the garage door? I don’t think it can support a lot of weight if I were to drywall it.
Back to basics: to isolate sound on a tight budget, you have to build a "room-in-a-room", more technically called MSM isolation. That basically means that you have to separate "leaves" of mass around you, separated by an air gap that is filled with mineral wool. That's what MSM means: Mass-Spring-Mass. The "spring" is the air trapped in the cavity between the two "leaves" of mass. Your existing garage structure is your outer leaf. It has to be made massive all around (walls, floor, ceiling, doors, windows), and it has to be airtight. The goal is to have roughly the same surface density all around. For example, when you do the math for figuring our your isolation, if you determine that you need a surface density of 24 kg/m2, then you need to make sure that every square meter of construction around the room weighs roughly 24 kilograms. The parts that don't have that much need to be beefed up. The parts that already have that much or more, are just fine, and need no further work. When you have the entire outer leaf up to that level, then you seal it airtight, and follow the same procedure for the inner leaf.
Would I have to frame up another wall in front of it?
Yes, that is the cheapest way to do your inner leaf: build a separate stud frame about an inch away from your outer leaf, put a couple of layers of 5/8 drywall on it, and mineral wool in the cavity between the two leaves.
Lastly, I can't do any major construction in the roof. I plan to simply get the heaviest boards/drywall up there as a fake ceiling and drape material between the beams. Sound crazy but it's what I have now and it works great aesthetically. Not sure how much it does for soundproofing.
How much dos it do? Not much. Nothing worthwhile, at least.
Am I wasting my time doing these walls and then not much with the roof?
In a single word? Yes. That would be a wast of time (not to mention money). IT would work exactly as well as building an aquarium where only the walls have glass in them, but the floor is made of carpet....
There will probably be many more questions to come ...
No problem! Questions are good, :) and you are in the right place to get the answers. The only problem is that I suspect you might not like the answers... the problem with the answers is that acoustics is a science, based on the real-world laws of physics. Those are cold, harsh, unfeeling laws, that don't give a damn about your budget or your need for speed. You cannot get around those laws. You cannot wish them away, try to make a deal with them, or hope that somehow your garage might be located in a space-time warp where the laws of physics don't apply. Unfortunately, the laws of physics apply everywhere in the known universe, including your garage, so the only way to get the results you want is to "follow the law" so to speak, meaning that you have to do it the way the laws of physics require, or it simply will not work. There are no magical materials (despite the hype you see in the advertising of some manufacturers of materials). There are no magical techniques (despite the myths that you see in so many places on the internet). There are only laws of physics and tried-and-tested methods for complying with them. You can either follow the laws and get results, or ignore the laws and waste your money.

Soundman202024 August 2011#9
I had the idea to first drywall the actual garage building itself and then build the room within a room.
bad idea! That would make a three-leaf system, which means the isolation in the lower end of the spectrum would be worse. It doesn't sound intuitive, since it seems logical that putting an extra layer of mass in the middle of the wall just HAS to improve the isolation, but in reality you are playing with a tuned filter (the MSM wall), and putting more drywall inside it happens to re-tune it to a HIGHER frequency, which is a bad idea. However, adding mass to the existing outer leaf (your current garage wall) is a good idea, and well worth doing. Search the forum for something like "beefing up the outer leaf", and you should find examples of how to do that. Basically, just cut drywall strips to fit in between the studs, up tight against the existing outer leaf. Hold in place with cleats, and seal with caulk. Rinse. Repeat. (in other words, add more layers the same way if you need more isolation.) - Stuart -

Most likely not. :) Ain't it great that you finally found something that you might NOT need to do ??? :) Seriously modes are important, all rooms have them, and there is nothing you can do to get rid of them. The idea is to choose a room ratio where the modes are spread across the spectrum evenly, instead of being bunched up in groups. If you keep your dimensions from being multiples of each other, and don't build a sphere, then you are probably good to go. In other words, if your ceiling is 8 feet high, then don't build a room 8 feet wide by 16 feet long! That would place all your modes in the exact same place of the spectrum, making for a disaster. In that case, make it something like 9 feet wide and 15 feet long. Yes, there are some very good ratios that you can aim for, and some folks try to tweak the positions of there walls down to the last micron, hoping to get a "perfect" ratio, but that's just a wast of time. Stay away from bad ratios, get close to a good one, and that's it. The reason being: reality will screw you every time! :) Once the room is actually built, you will have to measure the response anyway, in order to decide on the treatment, and most likely the actual modes will not line up with the predicted modes, simply because reality is a bitch, and Murphy's law reigns supreme. So if you try to design treatment for specific "projected" modes up front, most likely that treatment will be wrong, because your real modes will be elsewhere. So just design in some basic broadband bass trapping, then measure the actual room response as soon as the inner leaf is in place, and only THEN decide on what additional measures you need to take for dealing with modes.

Floating walls... big stuff you are talking about there: Lots of math coming your way! Basic idea: You have to build the walls to the correct weight so that whatever material you decide to put under it compresses to the correct degree in order to float. Put too much mass on your walls? You over-compress the rubber, and it flanks, meaning you wasted a lot of time and money. Put too little weight on your walls? You under-compress the rubber, and it flanks, meaning you wasted a lot of time and money. If I recall correctly, you need to load neoprene to something like 10% to 15% compression to get a good float (might be wrong on the numbers). So you need to figure the correct weight for each part of your wall so that all of the rubber under it compresses in the correct range. If one part of our wall happens to be heavier for some reason, and a small section of the rubber there flanks, then the entire wall flanks. Meaning you wasted a lot of time and money. Or if one part of the wall happens to be too light, then ... OK, you are getting the picture, I think. Not trying to scare you off! It can be done, but you need to do the math and build carefully, to ensure correct loading. Those two links I gave you tell you how to do it right.
Does this not negate a lot of isolation? Flanking paths? Transference of sound to the concrete and beyond?
Yes and no. You are building on a concrete slab, correct? That's a lot of mass, right there. Underneath that slab is good old Mother Earth, right? That's a lot of damping, right there. And a bit more mass, too: An entire planet's worth of mass in fact, plus an entire planet's worth of damping. Hard to beat that! :) It will take quite a lot of airborne sound energy to get your slab vibrating, so that's unlikely to be an issue. The issue is more likely to be structure born and impact noise, which is why you want to keep things decoupled from your walls and floor. That's one reason why your speakers sit on top of massive stands and are decoupled from them, or are mounted in soffits from which they are decoupled. That's also the reason why people build drum risers and put their bass cabs on decoupling risers too. In other words, eliminate all the direct contact between vibration-making "things" and the structure of the room. If you only have airborne sound hitting the walls and floor, then there shouldn't be any real issue. The question is: what else is your slab connected to?
