Soundman20201 September 2011#32
the landlords have decided to remove all the built-ins and give me the entire space to use and finish.
Cool! That's GOOD news!!! :)
1. For isolation ( assuming 46 - 50 db ) is it better to use the existing structure and double drywall the inside with Green Glue between sheets or build a stud frame for the inner leaf?
For max. isolation? Separate stud frame for the inner leaf. ESPECIALLY if you want to isolate drums, bass or other things with major low-frequency energy.
2. Should I still try and slope the ceiling 12 degrees over the listening position or can I use a cloud to take care of that?
What are you trying to achieve? If you are after an RFZ design, then yes, slope the ceiling. If not, then you can skip that. Either way you probably still need a cloud. (A cloud alone will not give you an RFZ, and is there for other purposes.)
3. I have no immediate plans to build a vocal booth. Is that easy enough to do down the line should the need arise?
Yes, if you plan for it now and set aside the space. Otherwise, not so easy.... :)
4. How do I build soffit mounts for the speakers in such a big space and still make the triangle work without me taking up the whole room?
Change the soffit angles! 60 degree intersect is not written in stone: You can vary it up to 90 degrees if you need to. It flattens the sweet spot a little, but widens the sound stage. - Stuart -

Soundman20207 September 2011#36
I looked at all of them between 14 and 16. 14'11 is optimum. Not fantastic, but it beats all the others for modal spread, especially around 73 Hz. But don't get confused here: those dimensions are the measurements for the INSIDE SURFACES of the INNER LEAF, not for the locations of the frames! BIG difference! Two layers of 5/8 with green glue is going to take up a bit more than 1" 1/4, so set all of your frames about 1" 3/8 BEYOND the lines you draw on the floor. That way, once you have the drywall up, the interior surfaces will be right on the lines. same applies in all directions, ceiling too: to end up with an 8" ceiling height, the studs need to be cut 1" 3/8 LONGER than you think, to allow for the extra thickness of the two layers of drywall. Speaking of ceilings.... what dimension of joists are you planning for that? You are going to be spanning 15 feet, with a heavy load of drywall and rockwool, so those are going to be pretty large joists. Did you allow for that in your calculations? Or if you are using a contractor, did he allow for that? This isn't like building a normal house or office: The loads are much higher, and the construction details are far more important. For example, does your contractor know that he MUST lay THREE beads of acoustic caulk on concrete floor, right under each sole plate (bottom plate) BEFORE he bolts it down? One bead goes down the center line, and one on each side of that, closer to the edge. Does he also know that he has to seal everything? Does he know to use backer rod and caulk in between all drywall joints? Does he know that he MUST stagger the joints? Does he know that he must also run a bead of caulk around the edge of each layer of drywall, and that the drywall must not touch the floor, walls or ceiling? Does he know that there absolutely CANNOT be any hard connections between this new frame and the existing structure? Not even a single nail to steady a new wall against the existing ceiling? This is all critical. Etc. There are just so many details that he MUST do right, if you want this to be a success. Just to scare you a bit: If he gets it just slightly wrong, and leaves a tiny, insignificant crack under one wall somewhere, just 1/16" wide and merely a foot and a half long, that crack will transmits as much sound energy as an entire section of wall 8 feet high and 12 feet wide, thus negating the isolation entirely. That's how important precision is. He MUST do it right, or he is wasting your money. I hope you explained all this to him when you hired him? So all you need is one careless workman who didn't bother to seal something correctly, and all you money is wasted: That's why you see people on this forum going to crazy extremes to ensure that their builds are sealed. They seal everything in sight, then seal it again, just in case. Then one more time, for luck. It turns out they aren't so crazy: there is good reason to this madness. Failure to seal wrecks isolation. Failure to get dimensions correct can damage isolation or acoustic response (best case), totally wreck it (average case), or even make things worse than they would have been if nothing at all had been done (worst case). I guess that's just my way of saying: watch your contractor and his builders like a hawk! Inspect everything they do, and make CERTAIN that they do it right. Tell you contractor he doesn't get paid if they don't seal everything correctly, or if anything at all even LOOKS like it might be touching the existing structure, since that IS what you are paying for anyway! If it isn't sealed, it is wasted. If it isn't decoupled, it is wasted. OK, time to stop my ranting and go hit the sack! - Stuart -

Soundman202010 September 2011#42
Pics! So it DID happen! :) But... what are those horizontal battens across the studs for? :shock: Not meant for mounting the RC on, I hope???? If so, is that method approved by the manufacturer? And even more important: How will your contractor be able to GUARANTEE that not a single drywall screw will ever touch the battens? Remember, that even ONE SINGLE SCREW short-circuiting the RC destroys the isolation of the entire wall. They CANNOT allow the screws to touch the studs or battens, and yet they also MUST achieve the correct depth of penetration of the screws into the RC. How are they going to do that? This is a big question.... 2. What's up with the electrical work? There isn't much visible in the photos, so its hard to see what the complain is, but once again, there is a potential big issue here: Penetrations! You cannot have any! There can be no switches, plugs, cables or light fittings poking THROUGH the drywall: Once again, any hole made by the electrician automatically destroys the isolation you would have achieved otherwise. You should have one singe penetration for the incoming cable, and that should be heavily sealed all around with a putty pack and acoustic caulk, then all of the wiring, switches, plugs and light fittings must be surface mounted on top of the drywall, with nothing going through it (except maybe screws going into RC, but once again there's the issue of how to stop that screw touching the RC...). In other words, you do the drywall FIRST, both layers, backing rod, caulk, tape, mud the hole works, and only THEN can you add the electrical installation, since ti goes on TOP of the drywall, not THROUGH it (except for that one single incoming power feed). Not trying to make you nervous, but these are all major points that don't seem to have been addressed, and MUST be taken care of NOW, before there is any additional advance. - Stuart -

Soundman202010 September 2011#44
the RC is mounted on the bottom of the horizontal studs leaving the metal clear of any possible screw contact and able to "bounce" while supporting the drywall.
Are you SURE you can mount it that way? Did you clear that with the manufacturer? It doesn't matter what the CONTRACTOR says: it matters what the manufacturer has actually tested in laboratories. If the RC is specifically designed and tested to be used like that, then fine! Go for it! But if this is something the contractor came up with on his own, but has never been tested in a a proper acoustical lab, under controlled conditions and with published results, then you actually have no idea what it might be doing. The contractor may well have used the technique in dozens of studios, but unless there is solid evidence that it works, who knows? Does it change the isolation characteristics of the RC? Does it change the resonant characteristics? Does it change the ability to support weight safely? And most important of all: is it allowed by your local building code? Will it pass inspection? A call to your building inspector will clear up that major point... I guess the basic question here is: If there is no published research on this technique, do you really want to be an acoustic guinea pig? Your call...
So I'll have conduit running around my walls then to the outlets? It will be painted over though so shouldn't be too bad. What about my single main light? Conduit across the ceiling?
It doesn't have to be conduit (although that will work fine) A lot neater approach is to use proper surface-mount ducts. When I do post-production facilities for my customers, I recommend LEGRAND structured cable management / trunking systems. Basically, it is just long strips of plastic "C" sections that you cut to length, attach to the walls / floor / ceiling, put your cables in, then snap on a plastic cover. They make straight runs, curves, corners, switch boxes, plug boxes, distribution panels, etc. : easy and quick to install, neat, easy to add / remove / change wiring, etc. : http://www.legrand.co.za/en/scripts/en/ ... P=440&L=EN http://www.legrand.co.za/en/scripts/en/ ... P=448&L=EN They aren't the only folks who make this stuff, and your electrical contractor can probably recommend alternatives. I only mention Legrand because it is what I'm familiar with, and use often.
