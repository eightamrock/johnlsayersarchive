rod gervais24 May 2008#11
Corbin wrote:
Would 1 unit on the exhaust that removes air be just as effective as 2 units that push and pull? In other words would a single exhaust unit effectivley create a cyphon for the air in the room and eliminate the noise from a second unit?
Actually - your best installation would be to push air into the space - pressurize the space - and then balance using a barometric damper to control the required flow - leaving the room slightly pressurized. Negative pressure rooms invite outside sounds in - positive pressure helps keep them out. Remembering (of course) that where air can go - so goes sound. You don't need a lot of static pressure - but can set it how you wish - the key is picking up the fan that provides the flow you want at the static pressure you desire. So if you want 125 cfm of fresh air - and you pick one capable of producing that with 1" of static pressure - then you can pressureize the room to an inch of static pressure and get all of your flow. You do not (at these volumns) require 2 fans - and I can't see the benefit in being able to reverse them - what do you gain? To change from heat to no heat is simple - you install a heating element - hook it up to a thermostat that installs directly inside the duct (downline from the heating element) and then wire it into a switch in the control room to turn the fan on and off. If your comfort level for air blowing on you is 70 degrees - then simply set the t-stat to roughly 72 degrees - (they swing about a 6 degree variance - 3 below setting to 3 above setting) That will average slightly higher than the 70 you want (72 to be exact) nut will only be about a degree below where you like it in the worse case. When it's on and the temps are above 72 degrees outside it just never calls to warm the air - all you do is hit the switch when you walk in the room (or if you want - tie the power into your light switch - when the lights are3 on the fan is on). that is about as simple as it gets. As far as quiet goes - that is a function of the noise produced by the fan itself (either traveling through the duct or through the structure) - plus the noise produced by the movement of the air through the duct. It is a simple matter to take the duct size and increse it to produce exactly the same volume of air at a lower velocity - so air movement should never be a problem. The motor noise is also easy to handle - through the use of vibration isolating hangers to stop the possibility of physical transfer through the structure (reccomended even if you are hanging it from the outside room) and then the use lined duct and finally introducing the air into the room via an isolation box. Reality is that for what you are talking aabout here now - this is no big deal - we do this sucessfully with huge systems in commercial facilities all the time - move a LOT of air - and never have a problem with any sound at all. We are moving 1350 cfm of air directly above the desk in a studio I designed in the Phillapeans - the owner of the studio can't hear it (or anything else)- can't feel the flow (the cool air just sort of drops slowly into the room) - and a microphone directly below a supply register dumping 450 cfm can't pick up any disturbance. How difficult can it be with 125 cfm of air? Sincerely, Rod

Clunk - Just what I thought - this was a case of a severly reduced duct size (a 44% reduction of the minimum reccomended duct size by the fan maufacturer - and that size is smaller than you would want for a studio. So no - no contradiction. AS long as you are oversizing the duct so you move large volumes at low velocities - AND - the fan is remote from the room and not connected directly will all hard duct - then a puch into the room (with barometric relief) is the BEST way to go - If you have an air handler - then you want to tie this into the return air side of the system directly before the air handler - and then go downstream at least 10' to install your pressure relief to the outside world. Not all that tough......... If this is stand alone fresh air - then you are going to have to temper it somehow - to make it warm in the winter and probably less humid in the summer......... I recommend spending your time and energy looking into a way to cool the room rather than heat it. In my experience practicing drums in smallish enclosed spaces, they heat up very quickly and do not require heating of any kind. Trust me- cooling and recirculating the air in the room is the most important part of your project! So please do yourself a favor and do not rule the cooling part out of it...fresh air will certainly help to a point...is there a way for you to cool the room outside of the room so that you're recirculating cooler air?
