knightfly12 February 2006#9
Most mastering rooms are NOT shaped; they are chosen for correct ratios, made large enough so that a lot of the modal problems are too low frequency to matter, and treated at early reflection points and bass trapped to sound as neutral as possible. The comments I've heard from mastering engineers (I'm NOT one) are that they want a "perfect living room" - in other words, something that's theoretically possible in a NORMAL house. (yeah, right...) I have been following this thread with interest. There are a couple of items that seem to misunderstood. These include: All rooms have modes, regardless of the shape. The goal of room ratios is to equalize the effect of modes. Not eliminate their effect or make a flat room. Most room ratios are based on several assumptions, including: All surfaces are completely reflective The room is perfectly symmetrical. There are no obstructions (like furniture, or humans) Sweet spot calcualtions are theoretical considerations of effects which result in data is practically useless. when calculation the room ratios, should i think of the empty room, or "the inner room"?
Think of the walls (remebering that ceilings and floors are just walls on their side :D ) that are reflective at the frequencies involved. If you have a thin panel in front of a concrete wall, then use the concrete wall. If your interior walls are stiff and heavy then use them. In all situations I have been involved with, the inner walls have been built strong enough to be the walls used in mode calculations.
If I will build a room-in-a-room, i should probably calculate, so that the inner room will have the right ratios, shouldn't I?
Correct.

knightfly4 March 2006#22
1 - you're correct, although I've not seen actual AMOUNTS of isolation that's lost due to the multi-leaf effect; it would only weaken the wall BEHIND the trap so it's not like your entire wall would be worse. 2 - You may be over-thinking this; keep in mind that the term "bass trap" is really abused on these types of sites, it can mean anything from a bale of insulation tossed in a corner, to several inches of rigid insulation, to a tuned panel resonator, to a sheet of homosote across a corner with insulation behind it, etc..... Corner traps made from absorbent material are arguably the simplest and best way to treat MOST rooms; these tend to be called "bass" traps, but are really BROADBAND traps that also happen to work on bass - just not as low a frequency as a tuned panel trap can. But these do so much for smoothing out ALL room modes, that they are the defacto standard "first thing" most rooms should have done. Specific, TUNED, panel traps should only be used in extreme circumstances IMO; and only after broadband treatments haven't finished the job. Placement of these is more critical and if used WRONG, they can DAMAGE the room sound instead of fixing it. 2(B) - doesn't apply to broadband corner traps, but would to TUNED panel traps placed FLAT against the wall. See the previous paragraph. Early reflections - if ANY sound path (other than the DIRECT one) can reach your ears less than about 20 milliseconds later than the direct sound (about 23 feet DIFFERENCE in length from direct sound) and is NOT at least 10 dB LOWER in level, then it needs to be absorbed or re-directed (to lengthen its path and weaken its level) - the human brain, when presented with two identical sound events LESS than about 20 milliseconds, INTEGRATES the two into one sound - this causes reinforcements/cancellations at different frequencies, providing a "comb filter" effect. It's like having a 100-band graphic equalizer on your sound, and alternately peaking and dipping every other slider. Worse, if you move your head less than an inch this will CHANGE. So yes, you need to consider early reflections. Something like 2" (minimum) rockwool/rigid fiberglass can be used at these points, and nearly ANY decorative cloth covering (chosen by someone who CARES what they look like :wink: ) will help... Steve
