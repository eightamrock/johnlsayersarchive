Soundman202017 August 2013#10
Complexity, unknown acoustics, wasted space. Complexity, because trying to build a studio with ten walls is going to be a major undertaking: Lots of strange angles to figure out for the framing, especially for the front of the room (assuming that you also slant your ceiling). Acoustics: I've never even seen a ten-sided control room before, and I'm not aware of any studies on how that would perform acoustically. But with that many sides, it's getting to be very close to a circle, so I would expect to see some focusing effects, strange modal behavior, and complicated reflection patterns. Ray-tracing that is going to e a nightmare. I have no idea ho you would go about treating such a room, acoustically. Wasted space, because all of the space behind those walls is lost. That's a lot of space.... So those would be my three main reasons for avoiding ten-sided shapes and going with a more traditional splayed rectangle. I'm not saying you can't do a 10-sided room: just that it would be complex, and I don't see the benefit. - Stuart -

lets say diffuser that has lowest diffused Frequency around 300-500Hz should be OK
Schroeder diffusers (and most other numeric sequence based diffusers) still have an effect down to at least one octave below the low cut-off frequency. For 300 Hz, a full wave is 3.7 feet, three times that is 11.1 feet, but there will still be scattering and lobing down to 150 Hz, which is a distance of 22.2 feet. You would have to tune that diffuser no lower than about 650 Hz low cut-off to be usable... But that's just for the engineer's mix position. The couch is way closer than that to the diffuser. Less than a foot, for the people sitting there. That's why numeric based diffusers aren't normally recommended for small studios. The room has to be pretty large before you can use them successfully. Or tune them so high that they aren't really much use anyway...

You are not wrong, per se, but the problem is that you didn't do the math! :) Tuning a slot wall to low bass frequencies requires some very strange sizes of slots, slats and air cavities... In my understanding Bass trap should be 1/4 wavelength of lowest room mode. (Do I've got it right? )
Not really. If you check the specs for OC-703, for example, even a panel just 4" thick has pretty good absorption at 125 Hz (coefficient 0.84). The quarter wave for 125 Hz is 69 cm, yet a thin panel just a small fraction of that thickness (only 14%) is doing a decent job of absorbing. Andre Vare says that 5% of the longest wavelength is about as much as you need. 5% of a 20 Hz wave is 86 cm...
a slat dimension would be slot width 1mm. Slat width 180mm. Dept from the wall 672mm. slat Depth 19mm. just one slat for now.
:) Yep! My point exactly. You need a huge slat with a very tiny gap (1mm) and a very, very deep cavity: 67 cm :!: And with only one single slat. the Q would be very high: in effect, that would be tuned to exactly 33 Hz, with very little absorption at nearby frequencies. Even assuming that you could build a 1mm gap accurately enough, if the mode happened to be at 36 Hz instead of 33, you would not hit it. And if the gap ended up at 0.8mm or 1.5 mm instead of 1mm, you would also not hit it. Helmholtz resonators are notoriously hard to tune, and since they have a high Q, they need to be built and tested and adjusted very carefully. What looks good on paper doesn't necessarily work out in practice. Finally, with one single slot, assuming it is 90cm long and 1mm wide, with a cavity depth of 67.2 you have an effective resonant volume of about 6 cm3. With a Helmholtz resonator you need the effective volume to be about 1% of the room to get a useful effect. That room has a total volume of about 50,000,000 cm3, so you are a little short on effective volume! You'd need may more volume to have a useful effect on that room mode. And that doesn't even consider the limited efficiency of Helmholtz resonators at very low frequencies, or the practical limitations of accurately measuring room response and resonator response at such frequencies. That's the reason why broadband absorption is recommended for bass trapping in small rooms: it does the job very well, even at low frequencies, without requiring excessive space or complicated tuning.
I think using ratio would be easier to treat the room and deal with resonances that might boost/cut sound produced in the room. That's why we design control room in ratio and I believe we should do the same in live/studio room so mics can pick up how instruments really sound. That's is my purpose of using ratio in live room.
The problem is that ratios are only valid for rectangular rooms with 6 sides (4 walls, floor, ceiling) where all the opposite surfaces are parallel. If the walls are not parallel, or if there are more walls, then ratios are meaningless. They no longer tell you anything about the room. All ratios (and all ratio calculators) are based on the modal response of rectangular rooms. Since mode are directly related to walls, ratios are also related to the walls. If the room is not a perfect rectangle, then ratios and calculators are invalid, since they are giving you the wrong answer. For non-rectangular rooms, the only way to calculate modal response is with FEM/FEA software and a good operator who knows how to set the boundary conditions correctly, and knows how to interpret the results. For rooms that are slightly off rectangular (for example, if the walls or ceiling are splayed by a few degrees) then the error isn' that great, and you can still get a rough idea of room response, but it won't be accurate. However, your LR has 7 sides instead of 6, and one of those is greatly angled anyway, there's no point at all in basing the room on a ratio. Besides, live rooms are supposed to have character, coloration, and a unique "sound" that musicians find attractive. Control rooms have to be neutral yes, but neutral often doesn't sound "good" to musicians. Live rooms are supposed to sound good, so that musicians feel comfortable making music in them: So a live room should be designed to proved a nice "vibe" or "mood", but without being terrible acoustically either. It's a balancing act...
