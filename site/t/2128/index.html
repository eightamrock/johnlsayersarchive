knightfly14 August 2004#3
I would go more with John's design - for one thing, a triangular iso booth will be really hard to treat for decent sound - those sharp acute angles focus sound too much, and make it really difficult and "peaky" trying to find a good mic position - plus, a triangle makes things feel too claustrophobic, which can screw up a good vocal take just by the stress on the singer. Windows - if you're planning on outside windows, you need to leave enough depth in the frames so you can use double strength SINGLE pane glass in the outer, and a heavier SINGLE pane inner glass, sealed and decoupled from the outer frame - On the cable troughs - if you have enough headroom to be able to float your floors, that would be a more isolated way to go - if so, you can include cable troughs as part of the framing for the floated floors. If you won't have the headroom to spare, or don't need the extra isolation of floated floors, then careful planning for console and J-box positioning will be necessary as well as separate troughs for power. You may want to check with Aaron in the Installation and Wiring forum for more insight on this. Not sure if it's my eye or the drawing, but it doesn't look to me like your CR is symmetrical; looks like you have enough room for a nice setup there though... Steve

Anybody else get the feeling that the permit plans show an unfinished basement, and somebody is planning ahead enough to not have to talk to the "blessed bureaucrats" again? :wink: If so, and you still have the option, can you raise the ceiling height at this point in time? Windows - just like walls, you need two leaves as heavy as you can afford, as far apart as you can get, and at least slightly different thickness from each other. Even using 3/8 and 5/8 glass 6" apart, your STC would be around 47 and only about 26 dB at 50 hZ - so, depending on how loud you intend to get and how close/irritable your neighbors are, you could be in trouble even with that. If you stick with the wimpy 1/8" glass for the outer pane, then use 1/2" glass 6" inside that, you would have about STC 47 still, but only around 19 dB TL at 50 hZ. From this, I wouldn't recommend any less than 3/8 outer glass and at least 1/2" inner, if you can space them further apart so much the better. 3/8 and 1/2 glass spaced 8" instead of 6" would get you a couple db more STC, with the performance @ 50 hZ improving to around 27 dB. This was partially addressed earlier by me, but in more detail as hot points: 1) Windows are partitions, just like walls, floors, ceilings and doors. 2) The best isolation construction/cost wise is to have two layers ONLY in the partition. Putting in additional layers will REDUCE the sound isolation. 2b) Depending on the construction, the layers can have different names. Eg. gypsum board walls, leafs; concrete walls , wythes; windows, panes and doors, well, doors. 3) The layers should have as little connection between them as possible. So in your case you have two layers final: concrete outer wall, and (I am assuming) stud/gypsum board inner wall. You want ONE layer of glass in each, unconnected to each other. Looking at the cost point, it may not cost much more at all to have windows in the concrete made from 1/2" glass. Only one pane to handle, simpler frame, etc.
Are these windows safe for fire escape?
? I have never heard of basement windows in a residence be suitable for fire escape. The design concepts I detailed above still apply. The difference would be that the windows would open. Your contractor should be aware of local by-law requirements and the cost to make the windows opening if you want it and it is not required. The one saving grace on this is that usually the greatest problem with opening partitions is the seals. Because of the importance placed these days on weatherproofing fairly good seals are not expensive specialty work.

cadesignr16 August 2004#25
Oops, forgot to tell you something. Considering you will have 10ft ceilings, and very little time to plan at this point, I think pouring one complete slab is wise. If nothing else, for a complete vapor barrier. You can then either float seperarate concrete, wood, or even roll out floors with embedded pucks. Although you will loose a little height, your time frames leave little for planning, although, if it were ME, I would spend one day digging a little deeper in those areas for the first slab. Remember, you have thresholds and leveling of the floors between floating room floors, and also non isolated entry point floors. Change in levels are something many people forget about till they have to install doors. Nothing is worse than trying to fix floor level differences after the fact. Most professional designs even account for 1/4" differences between areas of carpeting, tile, wood, terrazzo, etc. Especially if you have thresholds that require unusual door bottom hardware, cam lift hinges, and avoiding having to step up or down room to room. In my view, these things are CRITICAL to professional details and normal movement between rooms, and should TOTALLY be planned down to the last screw, BEFORE concrete is poured. fitZ :D
