rod gervais21 May 2007#2
I suppose it's possible to build something - but it won't include any isolation from the otside world to speak of - and it won't include very much in the way of acoustic treatments. You have an available area of 1050 s.f with a budget of 20,000 - that comes to 19.04 per square foot. If I build the cheapest house I could possibly build - my hard costs will be around 70 per square foot. And I am not building a recording studio for that. (although I do acknowledge that I am not starting with a finished shell either). I design studios and can tell you that I have a client right now building out a clean neat small control room with a small iso-booth (no live room) The 2 rooms come to a total of 311 s.f. His budget is 30,000 and he will have a hard time keeping his numbers within that budget. That's a little closer to reality......... cost per square foot wise. My recommendation to you would be to 1st sit down and come up with an actual plan of what you want to build - it has to be very exact as to what you want to do - is shoud include lights, power, HVAC (include fresh air), each and every sheetof drywall, rolls of tape - gallons of compound, sand paper to sand it with - nails, screws, doors, windows, finishes - room treatments, trim, trim nails, saw blades, etc., etc., etc. That plan should be detailed to the point of identifying each and every piece of wood framing it will take you to build this - you should have nothing left to the imagination. You then break out the cost for each room seperately........ and be very realistic about this - what ever you estimate the cost to be - add another 15 to 20% on top of that - don't think for a moment that you have everything thought out or covered. Even in the real world of big construction - we carry in our budgets monies in a budget field called "contingency" to cover the things we didn't think of or couldn't see when we bid the project. I promise yo there will be times where you are ready to work and slap yourself on the forhead and say "damn, I need_______________" and have to run to home despot to pick something up before you can work. If this happens to people who bid jobs every day of their life - that's their actual job - believe me when I say it will happen to you. OK - so now you have a room by room budget - covering everything you can think of - with a 15% contingency for "just in case" ........ Now pick which room means the most to you first - let's say for the purpose of conversation that would be the live room so you have somewhere to practice with your band......... then build out that room complete - the best that it can be - and ignore the rest. Then start saving your money again......... It makes no sense what so ever for you to try to build everything at once if in the end you have 3 rooms that are a piece of crap that are 1/2 finished - sound like hell and you hate the fact that this is the way life is. It does make sense for you to make the best you have with your money - which - when it's finished = you are going to love - and its going to feel soooooo good that you will be missing the rooms you don't have - and will have all sorts of incentives to save money to get the rest of it done. I hope this helped although I know it isn't what you wanted to hear. Sincerely, Rod

Ok....now I will answer on the other end of the spectrum. I am a relative newbie to this forum for sure, and admittedly not any where near as seasoned in terms of high-end studio design as others on this board. So with all due respect to all the deep thinkers on this board, I will offer up a counterpoint. Being that I am just completing a ground-up studio build myself, I can speak only from my own experiences with this studio, and my previous three home studios. I work a full-time day gig as an engineer, and I have a side business as a licensed builder. My home studio these days is primarily for personal use, although I do offer time to a select few friends as well. When I started this studio build, I went into to it with a CLEAR picture of what the general use of the studio would be, and the budget I would need. In my mind, studio design is as much about who will be using the space, and HOW as it is anything else. Will it be a 60-hour per week commercial studio with bands parading in and out, or will it be primarily a project studio for personal use only? Then I would consider the need for isolation based on the type of music, work habits, and proximity to other contributing factors...ie...neighbors, street noise, on and on. Finally, BUDGET always plays a huge role. Unless you have very deep pockets, it is simply impossible to build a studio by the book, with the whole list of "must haves" as told by the experts. In fact, if you look through these forums, except for a chosen few, most on here have built some really great spaces by mastering the art of compromise. In an ideal world, the sky is the limit. But at least for me, (and likely others here as well), there are other demands on my money such as mortgage, food, etc.. So the money I was able to use on my studio was not a lot by many people standards, but certainly a ton for me. That said....my project scope was as follows: Being that my children are now out of college and out of the house, my wife and I tired of maintaining a 3500 sq. foot house, 3 acres of land, and paying $8000 a year in R.E taxes. So we decided to downsize to a 1500 sq.ft ranch with a 1/4 acre lot, and LOW taxes. When we did, the first thing that was obvious to me was that I was going to have to address two issue: First being, the place had no garage. That's like having pants with no pockets. Secondly...where in the HELL am I going to put my studio? The answer to me was obvious......build a garage with a studio above. While I certainly had some inherent design issues to overcome, I am thrilled with the results. Due to space limitations and setback requirements, as well as trying to keep the building in keeping with the neighborhood and the main house, I was limited to a 22 x 24 garage. My TOTAL budget for this project from dirt to doorknobs was $50,000. Building the basic structure which included excavation, foundation, floor, frame, roof, siding, windows, garage doors, etc.. cost me roughly $25K. That is doing things with good quality materials, and building a structure that no only will function well as a studio, but one that I can be proud of. Keep in mind too that I did most of the work myself which saved a good deal of cash. So...that left approx. $25,000 to build a comfortable, functional, attractive studio. My wish list was: 1. A good size control room. Note: as I mentioned before, I knew going in what my work style and needs were, and for me, a big control room and small ISO booth work best. 2. A small ISO booth to track vox, guitars, drums. 3. A reasonable amount of isolation from outside, to outside, and room to room. 4. A reasonably good sounding environment to achieve good results. 5. Better than adequate heating/cooling. 6. Pleasing aesthetics. 7. Good lighting, including natural light.....yep......actual WINDOWS in a studio! 8. Acceptable storage...see #10. 9. Hardwood floors 10. Cherry cabinetry, granite cabinets, under cabinet lighting, etc.. A lot to ask of a tight budget. However, with careful choices, knowing what my budget and end goal was, I can say beyond any doubt that I am thrilled with the result. My studio has reasonably good isolation, sounds fantastic, and has a killer vibe. I am midway through my first session and am thrilled with the sound. Budget-wise, I came in just under...due to being able to do most of the work myself. So what is the point of this post? Well....it is easy to make a blanket statement.....ah, you can't do it for this much money....etc....but the bottom line is with COMPROMISE and some creative thinking, It CAN be done. Much of it has to do with knowing exactly what your end goal is. If I went by the book I am sure this studio would have cost me $20k more than it did. If I posted here a step by step on the compromises I had to make to get it done under my budget, I am POSITIVE I would have been torn from limb to limb for cutting corners. I had three choices going in.... 1: Ah, you can't build it for that much money and still do it "right". So...don't build it. 2: Ah, you can't build it for that much money and still do it "right". So...wait, save a bunch of money, and do it "right". 3: Take my budget and make it work for me, and end up with a project I am extremely happy with. In the end, I have a VERY functional, VERY comfortable recording environment that works well for ME, that all who see it love....AND.......I still can pay the mortgage and the gorcery bills!

Exhibit "B" Reporting... ;-) :lol: I think it is fair to say this: Success is defined by project owners on a project-to-project basis. If one has certain must-have goals and is able to achieve them, then by definition that is a success. Where it gets fuzzy is that people often aren't very explicit about their goals. When that occurs, we all start making assumptions, based largely on what our own goals would be. When we measure the imaginary goals against the stated limitations (usually budget), we make conclusions about the achievability of the unarticulated goals, using our fictitious goals as a substitute. This serves as a reminder to everyone to clearly state their goals when asking their questions, as well as a reminder to everyone answering the questions not to make assumptions about the goals. 8) The one thing that I (and I suspect Rod, and others) will always presume to be goals in a project is safety, structural integrity, and code compliance. Oh, alright, that's three things... But they're like peas in a pod when you think about it. So, if someone has a limited budget and certain stated goals, we'll assume it's going to be done safely, sturdily, and legitimately, which will increase costs over doing it unsafely, flimsily, and/or illegally, which may condemn it to the "it can't be done" category. Another thing... Sometimes we have goals in mind when we start a project (any project, not just studios), and then in while executing the project, we make compromises that compromise the outcome. Often the compromises are made with full knowledge, or at least high suspicion, that achieving the goals will or may be impacted, and then we usually adjust the goals accordingly. I also sometimes think there's a pride factor that comes into play when someone finishes a project and it doesn't achieve the goals as expected. Adjusting the expectation after the project is finished in order to call it a success isn't legitimate in my opinion. Simply put, such a project failed to meet its goals. We need to be more comfortable admitting when that happens so that we (and others) can learn from the experience. I recognize that the "Way of Rod" (and the inferred "Way of Keith" ;-)) reference is just an illustration and an abbreviation of the things we tend to stand for... But it bears pointing out, for the record, that we didn't make these things up. (Well, sometimes I do make things up by accident, but then, thank goodness, Rod is quick to correct me! :lol:) As such, they really shouldn't be named after us... Unless of course it is unique to Rod's fine book, in which case, please be my guest with such Rodification references. 8) --Keith :mrgreen: --Keith :mrgreen:
