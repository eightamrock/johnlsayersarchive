Stan, welcome; before we get much further, we need you to edit your profile to include your location - I know you already told us, but when your thread gets to be 96 pages like SOME of our people I won't mention :twisted: it'll be much easier to just look at your name and be reminded. The same "sticky" that asks for that will give you a good head start on getting somewhat up to speed on terms, techniques, etc - it's here http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=3231 Your space is just in the lurch between being big enough for 2 rooms and NOT - if you intend to have more than 1 or 2 people in at a time, it might be better to consider a single larger room with some "convertible" acoustic treatments - less reverb for mixing, more for tracking/jamming - just a thought. With a single room, it's more difficult to set levels without "cut and try" approach, but a good set of high isolation headphones can help some with that. One room is also cheaper to soundproof, since you don't need ANOTHER sound proof wall between the rooms, which will also rob you of valuable floor space - just a few things to think about. I personally do a lot of "one man band" stuff, including shooting/editing corporate training/safety videos, writing the sound track, doing voice-over, etc, and for me a single room works well. Others have different needs, YMMV as they say. Be sure to keep HVAC in mind during this planning - IIRC, Austin's pretty humid and warm so you'll likely want a properly sized AC unit that's quiet and will help keep your humidity down. Most wooden instruments prefer a steady diet of 42-45% humidity year round, and I know in my space (Oregon) I have to have a dehumidifier running about half the time to keep my piano and guitars happy. If you've not already found 'em, read through the "stickies" at the tops of the forums and look for any reference to "mass-air-mass" in them - this is absolutely the best "bang for buck" there is for sound isolation, the better you understand this one concept the happier you'll be with your end result. sharward8 June 2007#6
Hey Stan, The pleasure's mine. :-) Acoustic drums and bass are awfully nasty instruments when it comes to isolation... Bass is (obviously) all low and drums are all over the map. Low end is especially difficult to isolate. Obviously it will be much louder inside the room than it will be inside the house or outside the house... But if extreme isolation is a critical goal for you (which at this point hasn't really been articulated by you yet, non-Iron Maiden playing notwithstanding ;-)), you'll most likely need to have an inner leaf ceiling and the existing ceiling you have would be the outer leaf ceiling, and the existing ceiling would probably need another layer of drywall on it (assuming it can be added safely without overloading your trusses!!!). Three layers may even be required, although you haven't given us enough information for me (or anyone) to credibly say for sure. The good thing about Green Glue is that you can add one layer of drywall with Green Glue in between and end up with performance that comes close to four layers. While this is not a money-saver (because Green Glue is quite expensive relative to extra layers of drywall), it's a godsend when you have to be careful with keeping your dead load relatively light. In other words, one layer of 5/8" drywall is about 2.3 pounds per squre foot. Four layers is about 10 pounds per square foot, but two layers plus Green Glue is about 5 pounds per square foot, and if your trusses can only handle an extra 3 or 4 pounds of dead load, but you need superior isolation, then you have a solution without having to strengthen your ceiling framing assembly. Dig? Anyway, we still don't know anything about how close you are to your neighbors' property lines, whether isolation to/from the outside world and/or the rest of the house is considered a luxury or a must-have, whether you are more concerned about noise getting in or getting out (i.e., if you're concerned about noise getting in and you're a quarter mile from a freight train rail, you've got issues!!), etc. Bottom line, you're asking us what you need to do (and/or what you can get away with not doing), but you haven't really fully expressed what you need to accomplish (isolation-wise). Input..... Need Input.... --Keith :mrgreen: P.S.: I just reread your post, and I see you have a garage door to contend with... Yikes... :roll: ...Those are tough. You need to make some decisions about whether or not you want to retain the use of that door, and understand that high levels of isolation usually mean complete removal of the door or small likelihood of it ever being fully operable again... :roll:

Hey Stan, Good info so far. You may want to seriously consider doing a single room setup. Two rooms isn't impossible, but it is ambitious, given your limited square footage. I'm guessing (if I'm reading your information correctly) that your house was built within the past five years or so. I base this on the thickness of the walls and the raised section of garage floor at the rear. This "higher floor at rear of garage" technique seems to be is common ever since building codes were made stricter in the area of maintaining very shallow thresholds at doors and having a 36" clear area on which to step. I'll bet the area on either side of the door between th garage and the utility room is flat but for the threshold. My house was built in 1999/2000, right before this became a requirement. I'm no code expert -- but I am painfully aware of the code constraints that got in the way of my initial plans. This one in particular complicated my original floated floor plan so much that I ultimately ditched it altogether, thanks in part to Rod's advice... But I digress. ;-) I guess I'm saying all of this to point out the fact that since it appears you're in a newer home, you might also have homeowners' association, municipal planning, and/or other restrictions that may limit your options. For example, my city bans full garage conversions now, requiring at least a 10' x 20' clear area for (in theory) one car ("clear" being defined as free of walls or other permanent obstructions, although the "step up" at the rear is OK) and a fully operational garage door. I suggest you bring a sketch of your existing footprint to your municipality's permit counter, just to see if there are restrictions that would get in the way of what you'd like to do, and if so, what you might need to do to adjust your loose plans to accommodate them. Hopefully you won't have any such restrictions beyond the building codes adopted there... But it would be good to know before you inest too much of your energy. :-) I've been very fortunate to have had nothing but positive experiences at my city's building and planning department, and I wish you the same. I'm a big fan of prog rock (and prog metal specifically :twisted:) myself... You very well could have a pretty steep hill to climb in terms of isolation. Fortunately, thanks to your acreage, your family is at risk of being the biggest victim of the excessive noise. ;-) The neighbors are farther than, say, mine (I have 5 foot setbacks to the side :-x), but they're still not exempt from your noise. I remember early in my planning phase a couple of years ago visiting a pro studio in Stockton with fellow forum member mikeklooster, standing on the back lawn some hundred feet from the live room where someone was tracking some drums, and it was loud as hell. A housing development had built up around the studio (in fact, the studio is actually a converted house. The studio is "grandfathered in" because it would never be approved today. Frankly, I'm surprised it's still operating... But, again, I digress... ;-) ...The point being, drum noise carries far and wide, so be careful in your assessments and your assumptions. Solid adherence to the mass-air-mass principle, with perhaps a helping of "better safe than sorry" in terms of the amount of mass and the depth of the air, will ensure success. Finally, since you apparently have a decent sized lot (unlike me, with a mere 1/8-acre :roll:), have you considered a standalone accessory building in the back yard, or perhaps an addition like our esteemed forum member, studio911? Definitely checkout studio911's project if you haven't already: they're framing now and he grappled with many of the same questions and challenges you are facing now. Getting around that garage door of yours is going to be a royal pain in your ass... :roll: ...Especially with your requirement that it remain operable.
