That implies upwards of 110 dB, probably 115, perhaps approaching 120. Id would be good if you take measurements with a sound level meter, to put real numbers on that.
The neighbours are set back a good way from the boundary that the noise shouldn't be a problem, and they seem understanding of what I'm doing so far.
Your CURRENT neighbours might be fine with the noise, but the NEXT ones might not... :) There are certainly legal limits that you need to meet, so call your local municipality and get a copy of the nose regulations. Then you'll have two objective decibel numbers: the "how loud you are" measured with the meter, and the "how quiet you need to be", on the noise regs. The difference between those two numbers is the answer to the question "How much isolation do you need?". With that number in hand, you can then look over the documentation about what types of construction materials and techniques will give you that amount of isolation. That's the best procedure for figuring this out, as it is based on real numbers and proven methods, rather than "guesstimation".
The proposed external walls, (150mm from boundary fence)
so your outside wall is only 15cm from the property line? That makes things a bit harder, since some municipal regulations specify that the noise level must be measured at your property line, not at your neighbour's house.

As you can see from the drawing, the master bedroom is next door! (no other option in design). Therefore, my main concern is keeping the sound from the rest of the house, as opposed to outside noise coming in.
OK, so you'll also need to pull out your sound level meter again, and measure what levels are acceptable in the rest of the house. We can walk you through how to do that, if you want. But the idea is that you should arrive at a number for how much isolation you need, and based on that design the isolation walls.
1/ Foundation: Will a concrete slab, (as opposed to stumps/bearers/joists) be a better option?
Concrete slab on grade, definitely. Having the floor in direct contact with Planet Earth is a good thing: it acts as a really, really effective damper on vibrations that get into the slab.
2/ Floor: What would be the best floor construction? I've heard differing opinions on whether or not to float the floor.
Here's probably the best explanation of floating floors that I'm aware of: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8173 and a couple of related threads: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8134 viewtopic.php?t=8135 And if you really want to get into the technicalities, to fully understand what is involved and how to do it right, then this is arguably the most useful research paper on the matter: http://www.nrc-cnrc.gc.ca/obj/irc/doc/p ... /ir802.pdf
My idea was to build the room on concrete slab, but have a wooden floor (smooth surface for recording acoustic guitar & vocals). Then for drums, have a standard rug or proper drum rug if they exist?
Concrete slab is an excellent surface for a studio, just as it is, provided that the room is treated correctly acoustically. If you look around some of the build threads on the forum, you'll see some pretty amazing things some people have done with their concrete floors, in terms of staining and polishing. However, of you don't like the look of concrete, then other good options are ceramic tiles, linoleum, hardwood flooring, and laminate floor. Personally, I think laminate is a great choice for a studio, but any of the others will also work fine. But the big point here is that there cannot be any air gap between your final finish floor and your concrete slab. Whatever it is you decide on, it must be laid directly on the concrete (perhaps with some type of underlay), but not on a raised platform of any type, for the reasons outlined above in the threads on floating floors. So your basic plan is just fine: Nice wooden floor, with a rug of some type under the drums. Or maybe a drum riser.
3/ Walls: What would be the best wall construction for my scenario?
That will depend on your "number": the one derived from your sound level measurements, about how much isolation you need. But regardless of that actual number, you will need to do the full "room-in-a-room" type of construction to get the type of isolation you are talking about. What will change with the number is the type of materials you'll need, and the total thickness of the wall. The basic concept is simple: You will have four walls around your room anyway, from the house itself, You then need to build a simple stud frame just a few cm away from those existing walls, and put drywall (plasterboard) on only one side of those studs, not both sides, and fill the cavity in between them with fluffy insulation. You do the same for the ceiling, running new joists across the top of your new walls, and hang drywall from that, also with insulation in the gap. That's the overall concept. The new "inner-leaf" room does not touch the existing "outer-leaf" at any point. It cannot, must not, will not. Isolation depends in having the inner-leaf absolutely decoupled from the outer leaf, with not even a single nail bridging the gap. There's more to it than that, of course, but we can get into the details as your planning advances.
4/ Ceiling: Best ceiling construction for my scenario?
See above. The basic concept here is that all sides of the room need to be done to the same level of isolation. If you do the walls to 60 dB and the ceiling to only 35, then 35 is all you get! Think of it like an aquarium: you have to have glass on all sides to keep the water in. You can't replace the glass on one side with cardboard, paper, or a blanket!
5/ Insulation Material: Best stuff to put in the wall cavity?
Either mineral wool or fiberglass. If you go with mineral wool, then the density should be around 50 kg/m3. If you go with fiberglass, then you need about 30 kg/m3. And make it as thick as you can: fill the entire cavity, if your building code allows that. The difference between an empty cavity (no insulation) and a full cavity is pretty impressive. Once again, there's more to it than that, but that's the basic rule of thumb.
6/ Window: Should the external window be acoustically designed, or would it not make that much difference?
I don't see any window marked on your drawing. But in any event, if there is a window, then yes, it should be designed with maximum isolation in mind, and it must be designed as part of the entire isolation system. Basically, this method of isolation relies on the principle of a tuned resonant system, and the way you tune it is mainly by changing the mass on each leaf and by the size of the air gap. So each part of each leaf has to be designed with that in mind, including windows and doors.
7/ Door: Will the double door lock out more sound?
Yes, if done correctly. One door in each leaf is the way to do it. The doors must be solid-core (not hollow or foam filled), thick, heavy, and sealed all around the edges with proper rubber acoustic seals. Once again, there are many details to be considered for doors and windows, but you can get to those as the design progresses. 8 / ... Well, you didn't have a point #8, but you should have! It should be HVAC. Since isolating your room acoustically implies that you'll be putting two very complete air-tight, totally hermit leaves around it, then smothering it with thick layers of thermal insulation, you absolutely do need a proper HVAC system. First, since people have this annoying habit called "breathing", you need to get a steady, constant supply of fresh air coming into the room, and you also need to exhaust the same amount of stale air, at the same rate. Without that, you would use up all the oxygen in the room rather fast, and end up unconscious. But even before you got to that stage, the air would be so stuffy, muggy and lousy that you'd be gagging and gasping. Then there's the problem of temperature and humidity. Since the room is basically wrapped in very thick, efficient blankets, the heat put out by your body, equipment, and lights will build up very rapidly, and since you exhale water vapor with every breath, the humidity will also shoot up pretty fast. So you need air conditioning to keep both of those under control. In this case A/C is not a luxury: it is a basic necessity. Yo won't be able to use that room if you don't have a good HVAC plan. This doesn't have to be complex: Simple ducts, fans, silencers and a small mini-split system is all you need. So you need to take that into account too, as that all takes up space in your walls and ceiling. 9 / Electrical. You'll need lights and power outlets inside, and the electrical system also affects the isolation of the room, so it needs to be considered at the same time. Overall, it looks like you have a nice space there, with realistic goals. With careful design and construction, it can work it rather well, I think. - Stuart -

What is "maximum isolation"? :) 90 dB? 100 dB? More? Less? There is no such things as absolute, total isolation on planet Earth. There are only degrees of isolation, differences in sound levels, that can be measured in decibels.
