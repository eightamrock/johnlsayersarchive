Soundman20206 July 2012#30
Wood floor has been laid down.
It is generally better to leave the floor right until the end, after everything else has been done, to avoid damage during the rest of the work. Yours is already in, so I'd suggest that you should cover it very carefully, and very well, to protect it. It looks pretty good, though! Very nice.
Question: What to do with the flat, horizontal ceiling in both rooms? Suggestion for liveroom: to build a sloped wooden ceiling, adding diffusers and clouds.
I wouldn't use diffusers in there. The room is too small to be able to use most types of diffuser, and especially not on the ceiling. Diffusers need lots of space around them, and there just isn't anywhere near enough space between your head and the ceiling. So I would just use absorption up there, mostly. Clouds are a good option.
and for the controlroom maybe build a false ceiling: wooden frame, filled with acoustic hangers; sloped 12° from front to back, covered in cloth....?
The 12° angle only applies to hard, solid reflective surfaces, and mainly for the purpose of dealing with flutter echo. It makes no sense, acoustically, to angle cloth at 12°, since cloth is totally transparent to sound. You might want to angle it for aesthetic reasons, but there is no need for acoustic reasons. I would just put a cloud over the mix position, maybe hard-backed and angled if you suspect vertical modal issues, or if you want to eliminate flutter echo, or if you are aiming for an RFZ design.
I'd start by building basstraps for the entire width of the backwall of the controlroom
Correct! I'd do superchunks in the vertical corners, then thick absorption (maybe 6" or so) spaced away from the surface across most of the rest of that wall. It could also be angled, if you want: bigger gap at the top than at the bottom.
Then take care of the first reflections in the controlroom with resonators and absorbers.
Absorbers, yes. Resonators, no. Resonators won't help at your first reflection points, since they are tuned. At best, they will only help slightly, and only at the frequencies they are tuned to. You are much better of with just absorption at your first reflection points. - Stuart -

Soundman20209 July 2012#32
I already started with some of the aspects of my previous post. Such as the angled ceiling
The angled ceiling won't do any harm: It just won't do any good either! I'm not an expert on hangers (John himself would be the guy to comment on this for sure), but to me those hangers seem to be the wrong dimensions to work as hangers. They'll probably still work as ordinary absorption, but their height is just too short to be useful as hangers. They seem to be only a few centimeters high, and there isn't even any absorption in the ceiling itself, which is part of the design of a hanger system. They are also hung between the joists, so there are only a couple of them in each bay: To me, that would prevent them from acting together, as the pair in each bay would be unable to affect the pair in the next bay, due to the barrier between them. But you don't need to take them out, as they'll still be good for some sort of absorption, but I just don't see them working as hangers are supposed to work. What type of wood did you use to make them?
...and the diffusors.
What frequency range are those diffusers tuned to? They seem to be large, so I'm guessing that the cutoff is in the low mids, which means that they affect frequencies with long wavelengths. The general rule for numeric-based diffusers is that they must be AT LEAST three wavelengths away from your ears, and that "3 wavelengths" means "3 wavelengths of the low cutoff frequency". Assuming that the lowest frequency that your diffusers affect is 500 Hz (just an example), the wavelength for that is roughly 70 cm, so you would have to have them at least 2.1 meters above your head. Since normal ear height for someone sitting in a chair is 1.2 meters, you would need to have a ceiling that is at least 3.2 meters above your head in order for those diffusers to be useful on your ceiling. And for people who are standing, the height would have to be even greater. Some researchers do not agree with the "three wavelength" rule: some say it should really be a minimum of seven wavelengths! Still others say that the absolute minimum distance, regardless of tuning, should be ten feet, which is about 3 meters. So no matter which way you look at it, those diffusers are way too low. If you really wanted to, you could use diffusers that are tuned to much higher frequencies, so that the lowest frequency would be for wavelengths that place them high enough above your head. Assuming that your ceilings are at 2.4 m, and that everyone will be sitting down in that room, then you should tune then no lower than 1kHz. However, there is plenty of evidence that diffusers still do affect frequencies as much as one octave lower than their lowest tuned frequency, so you should tune yours no lower than 2 kHz. Of course, if people will be standing up in that room, then their heads will be much closer to the ceiling, so the tuned frequency would have to be even higher.
but for the vertical corners I was thinking of making hangers. Isn't that the better option? Or do you think superchunks would be better?
Either one will work, if you build them right. There is acoustic test data to show how superchunks work, but I have never found any data from independent labs that have tested hangers to see how effective they are, so it is impossible to say which one will work better. Both will work.
In that corner I could hang about 5 to 6 hangers that would be 45 to 60 cm deep and 220 cm in height.
That sounds fine, as long as you also line the walls around the hangers with thick absorption.
Do these need to be closed constructions hung to the wall or can I build these frames directly on the existing construction and then fill 'r up with rockwool?
For first reflection points, the absorption must be exposed to the air, so you can't seal it off from the room behind a panel. The front face (at least) must be exposed to the room. Also, absorbers work down to lower frequencies and work more effectively if they are spaced away from the walls, so if you can leave an air gap between the absorber and the wall, then that helps. They should also be as thick as possible, at least 10 cm, and even more if yo have the space. - Stuart -
