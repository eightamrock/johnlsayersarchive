then treat the room broadband absorbers and ideally some bass traps if the space allows.
Bass traps are not just a "maybe, if I can fit them in": rather, they are an absolute necessity, not even the slightest question about it. It's a small room so it WILL need bass trapping, and lots of it. All three rooms are small, and all three will need bass trapping, but the control room is the most critical so it will need the most bass trapping. The live room will also need some, but not to the same extent as the CR, basically because it is a Live Room, it can have a bit of character of it's own... as long as the "character" is harmonious, pleasant, and comfortable for musicians. With no bass trapping it all, it won't... And since it is a smaller room than the CR, it will need relatively more bass trapping, to get the modes under control. Also, since it is not a control room (where the position of the speakers is fixed), it is probable that all possible modes could be triggered, so the bass trapping needs to be designed with that in mind.
If I am trying to sound proof the room as much as possible so that the noise from within is not disturbing the outside world then surely reducing the transmission loss (especially in the low frequencies) is a good thing?
No, if you want MORE isolation you want to INCREASE the transmission loss, not reduce it! If you reduces the transmission loss, then you would have LESS isolation. And as Brien pointed out, a 3-leaf system will have REDUCED transmission loss in the low frequencies, which is exactly where things like drums, bass, keyboards, and electric guitars put out lots of energy. That's where you need the most transmission loss you can get. And 3-leaf will give you less of it than 2-leaf, in low frequencies. Also, "soundproofing" isn't a term that is used much in acoustics, since it means different things to different people, and can't be achieved anyway, in the real world! As implied by Brien's signature: it is impossible to stop sound. Any sufficiently loud sound will penetrate any conceivable barrier that Man could possibly build. "Isolation" is the term you hear most often, which sort of means what most people think of when they say "I want to soundproof this room". You mentioned your "Room #3" a lot, saying that you need to isolate it for drums, but you didn't say how much isolation you need, so its impossible to say if your plan will achieve what you are hoping, or not. You should fist put a number to that, in terms of: "I need xx decibels of isolation to/from the outside world, and yy decibels of isolation to the other rooms". Based on real numbers, it is then much simpler to come up with construction techniques, materials, and dimensions that will get you that amount of isolation. Without having a number, it's all just guesswork. But the one issue that is for certain, is that your currently planned 3-leaf roof won't be as good at isolating low frequencies as the equivalent 2-leaf. That doesn't mean that you cannot build a 3-leaf: it just means that if you do decide to build a 3-leaf, you will have to compensate for the reduced isolation (and higher MSM frequency) by increasing the total mass and also increasing the size of the two air gaps. That implies extra cost for the heavier materials, and lost space (headroom) due to the need for larger air gaps, so if this can be avoided, then it's a good idea to do so. That said, it is not always possible to make a 2-leaf work, and sometimes you do have to.
2. If so, how much distance would you suggest I leave between the first/main ceiling and the 2nd?

3. Which type of insulation would be best to install between the joists of the independent ceiling in order to reduce sound transmission as much as possible? Dense Rockwool (RW6/Prorox SL980)?
The insulation does NOT stop the sound from getting through: that's a common misconception. What the insulation actually does is to damp the MSM resonance in the wall cavity. It serves the same purpose as the shock absorber on your car: it damps oscillations. In fact, your entire wall works exactly like the suspension on your car. There too you have an MSM system (Mass-Spring-Mass), and just like your wall, it is tuned to a specific frequency. The wheel is one "mass", and the spring separates it resiliently form the other mass, which is the car. Without the shock absorber in place, it is really good at stopping road vibrations from getting through from the wheel to the car. In other words, it is a vibration isolator, just like your wall. But if you happen to drive over a road that causes it to vibrate at its resonant frequency, it becomes a really lousy isolator, and not only transmits that vibration to the car, it also amplifies it. That's where the shock absorber comes in. In fact "shock absorber" is a really bad name for it, since it does not absorb shocks at all! What it does do, is to damp the vibrations in the suspension system. And that's what the insulation in your wall does: it damps resonance inside the cavity. Without that, your wall would be a really lousy isolator around the MSM resonant frequency of the wall. But it does not, of itself, stop sound getting through, any more than the shock absorbers in your car stop the bumps from getting through. So, the insulation has to have the correct characteristics to act as a damper in an acoustic MSM resonant system, and it turns out that the optimum density for that is 50 kg/m3 in the case of mineral wool, and 30 kg/m3 in the case of fiberglass. Either of those will work just fine, provided that you get the correct density. If you get stuff that is too dense, then it won't damp the low frequency resonances in the wall cavity (it will only damp the higher harmonics). And if you get stuff that is too light, then it won't damp the higher frequency resonances well (it will only damp the fundamentals, assuming that you don't get it way too light, in which case it won't even damp those!). It's a common myth that "more dense is better". In reality "optimal density is better, and either too dense or not dense enough is bad".
4. What would you recommend regarding the plasterboard - 2 layers of 19mm?
Probably, but once again that can only be calculated once you have your number: how many decibels of isolation you need. That said, there are also structural issues: You'll need to get a structural engineer to take a look at what you have there, and tell you how much more stress (weight) you can put on it safely. Hopefully there is still enough dead-load margin in there that will allow you to add the mass you need to get the isolation you need.
