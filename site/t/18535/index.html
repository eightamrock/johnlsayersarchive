OK, but have you considered other sound sources? For example, wind, rain, hail, thunder, nearby roads, railway lines, or maybe farm machinery. Also aircraft flying overhead (if you are on the approach or departure paths for Ezeiza or Aeroparque, for example, or close to a hospital or military facility with lots of helicopter activity)... etc., etc. There are many external sounds that still might be a problem. It would be good to set up a sound level meter at the location where you plan to build, leave it there for a few hours on a "Leq" setting, or similar, and see what the results are.

Looks reasonable, but you could save space by only splaying the front section of your side walls, just far enough back to create your RFZ. From there to the rear wall they can be parallel: That allows you to splay the front sections at a greater angle (good for RFZ) and at the same time keep the rear end of the room nice and wide. The sound lock is probably not needed, but there is an issue with that layout in general: Right now, to get to any of the rooms you have to go through Iso-2. So all the load-in, load-out between sessions of equipment and instruments has to go through several sets of doors, and anyone in the LR or CR who need to go to the bathroom during a session, or go out to answer the phone, or get something from his car, has to interrupt the guy in Iso-2... It would be better to have separate access paths for each room, if possible.

Soundman20207 October 2013#5
so ill take the SPL meter and do some measurings. A curve always isnt't it?
I would use "C" curve, as it is much more sensitive to low frequencies. "A" is what is normally specified for legal purposes, in noise regulations, but "C" is what makes more sense for studios, since it is closer to the way humans perceive loud sounds.

Much better! You have the concept correct, but the actual angles depend on many things, such as the speakers themselves, the size of the room, the size of the soffits, etc. The bet way to determine the angles you need, is by "ray-tracing". But you have the right idea now. - Stuart -

Soundman202010 October 2013#7
There probably is software to do it for you automatically, but I just shoot out lines ever ten degrees or so and see what they hit, then bounce a second line off that. And since your room is symmetrical, you only need to do that on one speaker to confirm that both are correct, so it's just a dozen lines or so, unless you have a very complex shaped room. - Stuart -
