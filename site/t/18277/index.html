Very true, but there are also physical limitations imposed by the laws of physics. One of those is modal resonance in rooms with identical dimensions. Unfortunately, you have a very similar problem with the control room: Those dimensions give you a room ratio of 1 : 1.12 : 1.75. On a very well respected prediction tool, that ratio comes in at 24th place on the ranking of ratios, and is listed as "A worst case scenario". Fortunately, the room is big enough that you can do something about it: Build a simple stud-and-drywall wall across the rear end to reduce the length to 11 feet, and you then hit 2nd place on the ranking of good rooms, with a ratio of 1 : 1.12 : 1.37. That's a very different story, and very usable. So the first suggestion would be to build an inside-out wall at the rear, with 2x4 studs and two layers of 5/8" drywall on just ONE side of those studs, facing away from the room, so you end up looking at the studs when standing inside the room. You could even put a door in the middle of that wall, so you can use the narrow space behind it for storage. That makes the room pretty decent, actually. On the small side, to be sure, but still usable as a basic control room. - Stuart -

ITU, EBU amd AES specifications all recommend a minimum floor area of 20 m2 for a control room, which is around 215 square feet. However, that's optimal, for a pro "critical listening room". It doesn't mean that you can't have a darn good room in a smaller space: it just means that a small room won't be quite as good as "optimal". Most of us home studio builders can live with that! :) In fact, John has built a very successful studio in a shipping container. So the idea is to make the control room as large as you can afford to make it. Yours is a bit more than half the "optimal" size, and that's reasonable. Bigger is usually better, but you also have to live with reality. For a small room, more important than the actual floor area, is a good "ratio", or relationship between the dimensions. You don't want any two dimensions to be the same, for example, not to be multiples of each other, nor within 5% of each other, nor a number of other bad combinations. There are tools for calculating all of this for you, so you don't spend hours with pen and paper, trying to figure it out. One of the best tolls I know of, is Bob Golds' calculator: http://www.bobgolds.com/Mode/RoomModes.htm Plug in likely numbers to that, and see how things stack up. If you can get close to a ratio in the top ten, pass all three BBC tests, and get a smooth Bonello chart, then you are fine. There are some other guidelines for control rooms, such as symmetry: it is critical that the room must be symmetrical such that the left half is a mirror image of the right half. If you can't manage that for the entire room, then at least do it for the front half of the room. The rear part is not so critical. So if you want a window in the front wall, put it in the middle of that wall, not off to one side. Also, don't put doorways or windows in corners, as you will need the corners for bass trapping Then there are other guidelines for positioning your speakers and the location of the "mix position" (your chair), as well as for the placement of the basic acoustic treatment that the room will need. And there are also guidelines for the HVAC system: since a studio is basically an air-tight box, you do need to have a good HVAC system. Else the studio won't be usable for more than a few minutes at a time! It will get stuff, stale, hot and humid in there surprisingly fast, so HVAC is not a luxury here. It isn't even "optional". You must have it. There are many other guidelines, "rules of thumb", and suggestions for designing studios, and you are in the right place to find them all! The forum is actually huge, with hundreds of successful build threads, and mountains of useful, solid, helpful acoustic data that. There are also some books that might help you get a better handle on things: "Master Handbook of Acoustics" by F. Alton Everest (that's sort of the Bible for acoustics), and "Home Recording Studio: Build it Like the Pros", by Rod Gervais. And also read John Sayers own work @ http://johnlsayers.com/Recmanual/index.htm. Those three should get you on the right track. And of course, the forum itself: if you get stuck, ask! That's what this place is for... :) - Stuart -
