First of all I must state that the location is a bit difficult, it's really close to a major highway so there's constant noise and the sudden air-break from big trucks
:shock: Ouch! Not fun! Do you also get a lot of low-frequency rumble from heavy vehicles passing? Your analysis curve doesn't show it, but that might be a limitation of the equipment he was using: it might not have been sensitive down at very low frequencies. If you do have that problem, and you are planning on recording drums and other stuff that has a lot of lows in it, then you might be one of the few candidates for a real floating floor. Hopefully not, but being that close to a major highway makes me suspect that vibration and rumble from trucks might be an issue. This might be a bit harder to build than the first one!
He recommended that I should use a wall construction that gave me at least STC60
STC is not a good system for measuring isolation in studios. STC does not take into account low frequencies at all, and you have a LOT of low frequencies that you need to take care of. In order to get rated at STC-60 a wall does not even need to isolate AT ALL at 63Hz, for example... So don't look too much at STC. What you need is something that will give you the Transmission Loss (TL) that you need at each of the frequency ranges that are problematic. First, assuming that you set one of your design goals as NC-20, for example, then you will have to get your level at 63 Hz down to about 51 dB. It currently seems at be at 65 dB, so you need about 14 dB of transmission loss at 63 Hz. For 125 Hz you are peaking at 68 dB, but you need to be at 42 dB for NC-20, so you need 26 dB of transmission loss there. At 250 Hz you are peaking at 75 dB, but you need to be at 34, so you need 41 dB of transmission loss at 250 Hz. Etc. With that info in mind, you can then look for construction techniques that will give you the required amount of isolation for each band.
The room within a room is the solution since the place is in such a critical situation.
Yup! No doubt about that.
The first leaf being a 20cm filled concrete block, then insulation, air, insulation and 2x 16mm gybsum layers. Is that the best way to go?
Perhaps, but you'll never know until you do the math! :) In any event, I still have a hunch that your floor is going to be more of an issue than your walls. If you are just a few meters away from heavy traffic at high speed, then my bet is that you are going to get a lot of vibration coming in through the ground itself.

Sounds like a good plan to me. But I would forget about that door that faces the highway: That's asking for trouble! It will be really hard to get the level of isolation you need with that door. The overpressure from heavy vehicles whizzing by is not going to be kind to that door.

Yup! I think so too. Your wall angles still seem to be a bit too much.
The problem is that the engineer that's helping me with that told me that if I'm gonna build a filled concrete wall that long it will have to have that wall in the middle to support it since it'll be too heavy
Why can't you use pillars at several points along the wall? That's the normal way of doing it. Just a square section, plenty of rebar, and pour concrete. You can have walls as long as you want, provided that you reinforce them correctly. Plenty of very long walls held up perfectly in the 8.8 earthquake that we had here in Chile last year, and I can assure you, that was a pretty good shaking. A common method of construction here is to have a reinforced concrete "cage" for the building, which is basically just pillars on the corners and every 5 meters or so along the full length of the walls, then fill in the gaps with brick. Works great! That's how my house is built, and it has survive two earthquakes above 8.0 so far, without even so much as a single crack. Some of the walls are over ten meters long, so that method should work for you too. - Stuart -

gullfo20 March 2011#9
Measuring the existing structure is simple - get your self a decent sound level meter and use you largest speakers to play sine sweeps and drums/bass music and measure all around the building and areas where the sound can be problematic. To Stuarts point, placement of the proper supports is crucial for building integrity especially during seismic events high winds etc. Placing them to align with the corners of the rooms will make them much less intrusive as well as either sides of entry doors etc.

Soundman202022 March 2011#11
Do you have a sound level meter with a direct mic output, or a spectrum analyzer, or even a good mic with flat response? There are many ways of measuring the ambient noise level around in specific frequency bands. It would be good to know exactly where on the spectrum your issues are, in order to design the isolation suitably. If you have a good mic with flat response then you can download REW and use that to get an idea of what you are dealing with. - Stuart -
