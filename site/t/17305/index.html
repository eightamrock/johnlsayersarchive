Hi Arthur, and Welcome! :) A couple of comments on your post:
The floor and the ceiling as well as the window and left wall are 15 mm thick, whereas the right and bottom walls are 6 mm thick.
I think you mean cm, not mm! :shock: 6mm is about 1/4". or about half the thickness of a CD case! :)
Although I still do consider (or did?) building an isolated room within the room, ... The main question is unsurprising: is there a point to plan sound isolation in this space?
From what you say, it sounds like you don't really need much isolation. You are not doing any tracking, only mixing / editing, and you are doing it at low levels, so if that is working out for you, and you don't mind the occasional impact noise or street noise interrupting your sessions, then I don't see the need for all that expense. However, you might want to do some tests with a sound level meter to make sure that you are not violating your city noise regulations. Your current neighbors might be fine with your noise, but future neighbors might not! So it would be good to measure the real levels, to check compliance. That said, there are a few things you can do to improve isolation without going for a full "room-in-a-room": The first thing you can do is to seal everything, the second thing you can do is to seal everything, and the third thing you can do is to seal everything... :) For example, go around the room carefully, checking for tiny cracks around the edges of your walls, ceiling and floor, and seal anything that you even suspect might be a gap, no matter how small. Use caulk for that. The same applies to your windows and doors: Check for gaps, and seal the gaps with weatherstripping, caulk, or something similar. Sealing the door includes the gap underneath! The entire room needs to be sealed airtight. If you do that well, you can increase the isolation considerably. Another option is to replace the glass in your windows with thicker laminate glass, and to replace your door with a heavy solid-core door.
I was also thinking of doing that purely to improve the modal response and create a better ratio of, say, 1:1.2:1.9 (W:H:L !), instead of current 1:1.04:1.86.
Well, your current ratio is not good, so trying to improve that would be a good idea. But you might not need to build an entire room-in-a-room to accomplish that. For example, a hard-backed cloud can help to break up modal issues to a certain extent, and even building a single side wall a few cm in from where it is now would help. You can also use some types of angled treatment on the walls, instead of splaying them.
I assume the best place for my desk would be against the window rather than anywhere else.
Yes, that looks like your best option. Are you planning to soffit mount (flush mount) your speakers, or just put them on stands?
I do apologise if I missed any of your guidelines and did forget to mention any details. Hope this is not the case.
Looks like you covered all your bases pretty well! :) In short, to me it looks like you have a usable room that needs some work and lots of treatment, like any small room, but you don't seem to need additional isolation, so construction should be fairly simple. - Stuart -

Hi Arthur, Oops! Didn't notice your update! Lots going on...
I assumed that placing the closet against the back wall (by the door) would create substantial asymmetry. Thus, I came up with something like this:
Looks good, yes. Symmetry at the back of the room isn't as critical as at the front, but it's still nice to have if you can. Plan on treating the forward-facing surfaces of that closet with absorption, so it doesn't need to be neatly varnished/painted.
What matters is the even reverb time across the whole spectrum I guess, but that is to be measured and addressed at a later stage I suppose.
Well, technically, it isn't reverb in a small room. You need a path length of about seven times the frequency in order to talk about reverb, so small rooms don't have a statistically valid reverberant field in the lows, or the mids, and it doesn't matter for the highs. But what small rooms DO have, is modal issues. Room modes are easy to confuse with reverb, because they kind of sound like the same thing, but they aren't. Modes are standing waves, defined by room dimensions. The idea is to choose your room dimensions so that your modes are spread out as evenly as possible, without having several all bunched up together, nor large gaps between them. That's hard to do in small rooms. The smaller, the harder!
I will most likely install two superchunks against the new walls, such as this:
Yup! That will help. All modes terminate in room corners, so putting superchunks in corners is a good way to damp them. But that's only two corners: rectangular rooms have 12 corners, so see if you can try to cover at least a few more. Once again, hard to do in a small room. But once again, the smaller the room is, the more bass trapping it needs!
and later on, taking into account the measurements (waiting for the mike atm), it will probably be more or less this:
Also looks good. It seems like you have the right approach. Once again, symmetry at the back isn't critical, so you can pretty much do what you want with the closet there, but the way you have it looks pretty good to me. - Stuart -
