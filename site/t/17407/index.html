From your description this is what is normally refered to as a "Project Studio". References to a control room are really a misnomer as there is nothing to be controlled. But this is just nomenclature... You mention asbestos tiles - in most places people would want these removed - in fact in some places it is required to remove them and in a few places, the government may subsidize of certain percentage of the cost. Concrete is very popular these days for studios, particularly having it stained. This staining technique is not yet very well known here in europe. Aside from this however is the point that concrete floors in a basement are considered not particularly healthy and people complain of backache and increased colds. I would suggest you look into Novilon or other linoleum type flooring. It is extremely economical and because of the incredibly wide range of styles (finishes, patterns, plus 3D finishings) will enhance any theme you have in mind for the room. This type of flooring retains the desired acoustic reflectivity, while adding a cushioning effect for your feet plus a thermal isolation from the cold concrete floor (many products are sufficiently thick on their own, others may require a underlay), and as a further advantage, offer a certain amount of inherent decoupling or buffering between furniture (incl. speaker/stands) and vibrations propagating through the concrete floor. I was also a little curious about those "hollows" and whether they are removeable or alterable. I understand that one of them acts as a soffiting or rather casement around a pillar - albeit overkill (seen dimensionally). One thing that gets stressed is electrical wire hi and acoustical wire low. Your electrical wire is essentially going to be running parrallel to your gear, and that is a potential 60Hz problem with unwanted noise in your sound chain. What are the chances of, before you cover all this up, of re routing the electrical wire to be vertical in the stud bays? If this is not an option, then you have to be aware that the potential is going to be there, especially the closer you move towards the wall with wire in it running horizontal.

One quick question about sliding glass door. My builder is a fan of triple pane for sound isolation...does this create a 3 leaf problem?
Yes it does! 3-leaf for sure. And because the leaves are relatively low mass (as compared to the walls), and the air gaps are very thin, that puts the resonant frequencies right where you don't want them: the lows and lo mids. Proper acoustic sliding doors are tough, heavy, and have a single pane of thick laminate glass, as well as excellent seals all around. Two doors like that with an air gap between them will give you pretty decent isolation. Soundman202029 May 2012#20
It's safe to assume a decent exterior slider won't work, or were you saying that I don't want a triple pane slider? I think you were saying that two sliding patio doors will work, with an airlock, right?
Exactly: A pair of sliding doors separated by a gap of about 4 inches or more, will give you reasonable isolation. The issue with the triple-pane slider is the high resonant frequency and low isolation in low frequencies (three-leaf). If you just wanted to stop ordinary speech and office sounds, then the triple glazing would work fine, since those sounds are mostly in the middle area of the audios spectrum, with very little energy in the low end. But music is different: lots of energy in lows, particularly in drums, bass guitars, keyboards, etc. Two glass sliding doors with a gap between work in exactly the same way as your two-leaf wall around the room: they make a tuned MSM system. In fact, when done properly, the doors are actually PART of the same MSM system as the walls. So the idea is to choose glass that gives you the same surface density (kg/m2 or lbs/ft2) as the rest of the wall. Since glass is about 3 to 4 times as dense as drywall, you need glass that is about one third the thickness of the leaf. So for example, if you have two layers of 5/8" drywall for your leaf, then your glass should be 3/8" or 1/2". That will maintain the same density across the entire surface of the wall, including the glass. But glass that thick in large panes, is very expensive. It's also very heavy, meaning that the frames, rollers, tracks and everything else about the door must be heavy duty, which increase the cost even more. If you don't mind the cost, then that's fine! But if you do mind the cost, then maybe building your own hinged doors with glass in them would be a much less expensive proposition. If you think about it, the opening offered by a sliding glass door is not really much bigger than the opening of an ordinary hinged door, since one pane slides in front of the other. When fully open, you have about the same amount of space. So sliding glass doors don't offer bigger openings. They do offer a lot more light, though. But if you did ordinary hinged doors but only put glass in the top half, and then put a fixed window next to it, also with glass only in the top half, you'd save a huge amount on glass and still get a good view outside, and good light. Finally, seals are an issue. Sliding door seals are more likely to give you problems in the future than the seals around hinged doors, especially if they just rub along the door as it is being opened and closed, instead of being held out of the way by a mechanism that only seals when the door is fully closed. But then you have that mechanism to think about.... I'm, not trying to convince you to dump the idea of sliders! John uses them all the time in his designs, and they work great. But the type he uses are the best quality, and most expensive. So that gets back to the money issue: if you don't mind splurged several grand for a pair of good sliding glass doors, then go for it! I'm just pointing out some issues that you should keep in mind when selecting the doors.
Ok, I think I am following you, but bear with me, as I am definitely not confident in this arena...axis intersect is where I'd be sitting correct? The point where the axis coming off of each speaker crosses?
Exactly. The acoustic axis of the speaker is the imaginary line that pokes out perpendicular to the front panel, at the spot where the sound appears to come from, after the separate tweeter and woofer sounds have merged. That spot is sometimes shown by the manufacturer in the manuals for the speaker, or they might be able to tell you if you call and ask. If not, then imagine another line joining the center of the woofer with the center of the tweeter: the axis will start on that line, much closer to the tweeter than the woofer. So pick a spot around 75% along that line, and you won't be far off. That's where the acoustical center of the speaker is, and where the acoustic axis starts. So if you imagine a line coming out of the speaker at that point, and projecting back towards you, that line should just graze your ears, then continue on backwards, and meet it's mate from the other speaker a few inches behind your head. It's the angle between those two that I'm talking about as the "intersect angle". If that angle is around 52°, then you are fine. (That implies that the speakers are angled in at 26°, which is a little low, but nothing to worry about). The optimum intersect angle is often quotes as being 60° (meaning the speakers are angled inwards at 30°, or 4° more than yours), and you can even go as high as 90° (the speakers would be angled at 45° in that case) but that's the limit. Beyond 90° intersect (45° toe-in) the image starts to break apart, since the speakers are now basically spread wider than any sound source in nature: Your head itself is creating a "shadow" that prevents the left speaker being heard by the right ear, and vice versa, so each ear is basically only hearing the speaker on that side: there is not much stereo image or sound stage like that. That's why I was concerned when you said you had angled them 52°, and from the photo I couldn't figure out which was the front wall and which was the side wall! What you show in your second photo is fine. That is a 26° toe-in, or 52° intersect, so that's OK. Your sweet spot will be a bit narrower (side to side) but also longer (front to back) than the "standard" setup at 30° toe-in (60° intercept), but that's fine, as long as you are aware of it. Your sound stage will also be slightly "squashed", so you'll have a sub-conscious tendency to pan things more than they need to be, but the effect will be very slight, so don't sweat it!

to aim for a position roughly 1/3 of the distance back in the room...
Once again, you'll often see people saying that the "perfect" location is 38% of room depth (front to back), but that's theoretical "perfection" (whatever that means!), so don't go crazy tearing walls down to try to get your ears at 38%! It's just a guideline. The important thing is to stay away from really bad places in the room, such as 25% and 50%, and get close to 38%, so your plan is fine. But just to clarify, it is your EARS that we are talking about now, not the intersect: your ears should not be at 25% or 50%, and should be in the general rough ball park of 38%. But like I said; don't go crazy here! In a small room, even moving your head forward or back a bit to reach a fader, or get more comfortable in your chair, is going to move your ears several percentage points. If the room is 10 feet long (for example) then leaning back or forwards just 6" each way moves your head by ten percent of the room depth! So trying to finesse things down to the last 1/64" is pointless. Just get in the ball park. And many engineers actually seem to prefer to be a bit closer to the front wall that theory predicts.
