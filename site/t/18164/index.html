Hi Kevin, and welcome to the forum! :) Great first post, by the way! :) :)
I want to build a studio above the garage in my new house.
Well, you are probably already aware of this, but unfortunately, that is the first and biggest problem. Building on a second or higher floor is much harder than building on the ground floor, for several reasons. Primarily, you don't have a large, massive, thick, dense, rigid, concrete slab under you, that is in turn directly damped by the entire Planet Earth, so isolating is a lot harder, and second you don't have the structural support that you need for all the huge amount of mass that you will have to add, in order to even get reasonable isolation. That's why you frequently see recommendations here to ONLY build on the ground. It is so much cheaper, so much easier, and so much better, all around. Isolation requires mass. That's the fundamental truth about sound and "soundproofing". Without substantial mass there simply is no way to get high levels of isolation. There is no such thing as "lightweight soundproofing", despite the marketing hype you sometimes see on questionable web-sites from questionable companies about their questionable products. Adding snake-oil to a product does not make it more soundproof! :) You cannot bypass the laws of physics. So my first and most fundamental suggestion to you would be: build it on the ground, next to the garage. In the long run, it will be cheaper to do that than to do what you are trying to do. Building in the second floor is more expensive for two main reasons: To get to the same level of isolation as a ground floor studio, you will need much more mass since you have to do a major beef-up of the floor just to get something resembling the isolation of a slab-on-grade. And secondly, all that extra mass requires extra structural support, which the garage itself was never designed to handle. So you'll spend a lot of money just on beefing up the garage structure to the point where it can handle the huge load that you'll be placing on it. So your choices are these: - Build within the exiting structural limits, on the second floor, and just live with the poor isolation. - Spend a lot of money to beef up and isolate that second floor to get the level you need. - Spend less money, and get more isolation, by building something separate, on it's own slab.
The music recorded in the space will primarily be loud rock (guitars, bass, drums, vocals).
So you will have the loudest, hardest-to-isolate instruments in there. Bass and drums are the two hardest things to isolate, due not just to the very high levels, but also to the very low frequencies. Both bass and drums put out a large amount of energy in the low frequencies, and the lows are exactly the most difficult to isolate, requiring even more mass and larger air gaps.
I am hoping to receive some guidance/suggestion for how to layout and design the studio between these two rooms to have a great sounding live room as well as a very balanced and accurate control room.
There are specifications that you can follow from the ITU, EBU and AES about how a control room should sound. Each organization has its own set of specs, but they are very similar in general, with very little difference. So base your goals on those, and you will be fine. The problem is, they only tell you "what" you should aim for, not "how" to get there! :)
there is no question that we will be easily hitting the 110db mark.
Probably closer to 120. Drums alone played hard can hit 115 dBC.
I would love to build enough sound isolation to not drive my wife crazy during the day as well as to keep normal life and activity of a home with 2 small children out of the recordings.
It would be good to put actual objective numbers on those subjective levels: Get a sound level meter (they are not expensive), and measure what your wife considers to be "not driven crazy" by setting up your sound system in your present house, and playing the typical music that you will be tracking/mixing, then keep adjusting the level of that until she says she is "not crazy". Also measure the level that you think is comfortable for your kids, when they are doing important things like studying, homework or sleeping. OK maybe they aren0t old enough for having homework or studying right now, but in a couple of years... ? You'll then have two numbers: the big one is "How loud am I?", which is around 115 to 120 dBC, and the other is "How quiet do I need to be?". Subtract the little one from the big one: that's how much isolation you need. Then take a look at IR-761, IR-832, IR-766, IR-169 and IR-811, find a set of construction technique and set of materials that provides the level of isolation you need and fits your budget, then build that. That's the normal procedure that you should use, since it is based on actual, objective numbers, not "guesstimates". Those documents contain lists of hundreds of ways of building walls, floors and ceilings, and show the isolation achieved by each one, as tested in independent acoustic laboratories. They tell the truth about what you can expect foe each method and each type of material.
One other factor to consider is a highway down the road that is moderately trafficked but will occasionally have large semi-trucks driving by.
Then you might also need to look at isolating very low frequency vibration, which makes things a bit harder still. Can you feel vibration in the building right now, when truck go past? If you touch the floor or walls gently with your fingertips, or even better, listen to them with a doctor's stethoscope, is there any vibration or sound in the structure as cars/trucks/busses go by?
This is a standard home construction type build utilizing 2x4 wood studs for framing, OSB outside, insulated using Cellulose and finished with 1/2" drywall. The studio space is located above my garage and has only been framed and closed using OSB outside.
OK, this is the time to get a structural engineer involved. After you do the basic calculations about how much mass you will need to isolate at the levels you want, get him to come in, examine the existing structure, examine your rough plans, and give you his professional qualified opinion on if you can do it or not, and if not, then what do you need to do to beef up the structure sufficiently that it can handle the huge load.
The floor of the studio was built using prefabricated trusses. My builder said that the trusses have been engineered to withstand ungodly amounts of weight because he knew that we would be building more rooms in the space above the garage.
"Ungodly amount" is not really a good, accurate, objective measurement of how much load it can handle! :) You need to know what the design parameters are for live load and dead load of both the floor trusses and the ceiling trusses, as well as the load-bearing capacity of the overall ground floor structure. To give you an idea, your walls and ceiling will probably weigh in excess of 40 kg/m3 (aprox 8 PSF), IN ADDITION to the existing walls, floor, roof and ceiling, and your new floor will weigh much more than that. So figure out your total wall area, multiply by 40, and that will give you a very rough estimate of the ADDITIONAL dead load you are adding. Rough guess: you have about 160 square meters of wall, floor and ceiling area to get two rooms in there, fully isolated, which implies about 6400 kilograms of mass. That's roughly 14,000 pounds of materials, WITHOUT considering the new floor. :shock: So the question is: Did your builder make that strong enough so that you can park four or five cars up there, or a couple of small trucks? That's what we are talking about, in terms of mass. That's roughly the weight of THREE standard 20' shipping containers! Did your guy build your room to that level? can you really shove three shipping container in there, without it collapsing? Besides, regardless of what your builder says, you still need the professional opinion of a qualified structural engineer, in writing. (Note that the above weight estimate does not consider the weight of the new floor, nor of additional mass that might be needed on the existing outer leaf.)
The ceiling and roof have also been constructed using prefabricated roof trusses built out of 2x4 wood studs.
How is that roof ventilated, and what are the code requirements for that? Isolation is a complete "envelope", including the room. The entire envelope must be sealed air-tight in order to get isolation, but a roof needs ventilation. So there is a potential issue there: you may need to use three-leaf construction for your ceiling, which reduces low frequency isolation.
I think it would be great if the studio opened at the top of the stairs into the control room. Then you would have access to the live room from the control room.
That is one possibility, yes, but it does mean that load-in and load-out for each session require dragging all the equipment and instruments back and forth through the CR into the LR. And since you are on the second floor, it also means lugging all of that up and down stairs all the time.... so make the stairs very wide, not to steep, and with easy access paths at the top and bottom. No sudden, tight turns, or low rafters overhead, or low/narrow doorways.
It would be awesome to have a window from the control room into the live room as well as a sliding glass door. However, I recognize that I am tight on space and would be willing to settle for just the sliding glass door if there is just no room to work with. ... As it stands right now my budget is $6000
A pair of sliding glass doors will eat up a heavy chunk of your budget. HVAC will eat up most of the rest. To be very honest, your budget is unrealistically low. You are talking about an area of about 600 square feet, and a budget of US$ 6000. That works out to just US$ 10 per square foot. That's about enough to do just the finish flooring in good quality laminate, including labor and underlay... You are already building a house at present, so you know roughly what it costs per square foot to build. Apply that same "per square foot" rate to the studio, but add about 25% to it: that's roughly what it will cost, in realistic terms, to build a studio. Put it another way: a 4'x8' sheet of 5/8" drywall will cost you something in the region of US$ 10, and it covers 32 square feet. You need to cover about 1650 square feet, with at least two layers, so total of 3300 square feet. That's about 110 sheets (allowing for some waste). There goes US$ 1,100, just on drywall. Green Glue costs roughly US$ 0.75 per square foot. There goes another US$ 1,200. 2x4 studs are something like US$ 4.00 each. You need about 250 of those, so there goes another grand (and that's making the rather unlikely assumption that you can do your ceiling joists with 2x4's! More likely to be 2x8's...) So after spending more than half your budget, all you have done is purchase studs, drywall and green glue. Still to go: labor (that's a biggie!), HVAC system (another biggie), doors, windows, insulation, caulk, nails, screws, bolts, electrical system (another biggie), lights, acoustic treatment, surface finishes, etc. I'm not trying to be a wet blanket here, or to discourage you from doing this: I'm just pointing out the cold, hard reality of what it costs to build a studio, in objective terms. And we still have not considered the new isolation floor in any of the above. I would seriously re-think your budget, and also seriously think about doing this as a separate out building, on its own slab. If you cannot do either or those, then the space and budget you have are "usable", but be aware that you will not be able achieve good levels of isolation. - Stuart -

This takes a bit of juggling, trad-offs, hand-banging, and tears. :) There are recommendations in documents from the ITU, EBU, AES and other organizations, on the minimum foot print and room volume that you need for a "critical listening" room: Basically, 20 square meters of floor area for 2.0 or 2.1 rooms, and 30 square meters for multichannel rooms, such as 5.1, 7.1, etc. That does not mean that you cannot have a small room that is still good: it just means that these guys have figured out the optimum size, and that's what it is. So based on the "1 to 5" recommendation for the relationship between CR and LR, the LR therefore needs at least 40 m2 of floor area, and ideally about 100 square meters, or perhaps somewhat less if the ceiling is considerably higher in the LR than the CR. So that's the basic starting point. According to these guidelines, you need about 120 m2 for CR + LR. That works out to roughly 1300 square feet. You don't have that much space, and neither do the vast majority of home studio builders! It just isn't realistic for the average home to have a studio that big! :shock: However, you do have nearly half the area that they say, which is a darn sight better than most studio builders! So you could conceivably shrink things by 50%, and that would be the solution. But that would give you a control room with only 10m2 of floor space (about 107 ft2), which might not be enough if you need to fit in an entire band to listen to the mix, for example. So this is where the juggling starts: How much extra do you want to give to the CR? You could keep it at the recommended 20 m2 (215 ft2), but then it is about the same size as the LR, which is not good, especially if you also need to fit the band and their gear into the LR! Juggle---juggle--juggle.... This is the thing that you need to define for yourself: how much floor space to you want to devote to each of the rooms? You know what your priorities are, and how an average session goes for you, so you are the only person who can figure out this part. Based on your decision, we can then help you refine that further by seeing what arrangement and shape of each room will fit into your space, combined with aspects like room ratio, ceiling heights, etc. So the ball is in your court for this bit: What split of that floor area makes sense for you? How much floor area do you need in each room? How many people / instruments / hangers-on etc.? - Stuart -
