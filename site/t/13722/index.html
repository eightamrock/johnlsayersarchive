Soundman202029 October 2009#12
can I put p/board straight on the bottom of the joists - not in-between?
Short answer? Nope. that would make a three-leaf system. And since you say that you need to maximize isolation as you basically have none at all, then three leaf is a bad idea. I think that you need to go the slow-but-sure method of beefing up: Carefully cut drywall to shape to fit up there between each pair of existing joists, cleat it, caulk it. And do it again. And again. You need at least two extra layers of 5/8 (15 mm) drywall up there, probably three if you really want max isolation. BUT!!! That raises another question: Can that structure take the weight? You probably need to get a structural engineer in there, to tell you how much extra load that existing ceiling can take. Assuming he gives you the go-ahead, then I'd go for as many layers of drywall up there as he says you can put in. I'd also follow Lilith's lead here: Build your inner-leaf ceiling inside out, to maximize height. You'll have plenty of space up there between the existing joists for your insulation, so your inner-leaf ceiling drywall can be just an inch or two below the existing joists, I reckon. And once again, since you say that isolation is paramount, I'd go with three layers of drywall on your inner leaf too, with doors and windows and HVAC to match. my $ 0.02 - Stuart -
xSpace29 October 2009#13
"an I put p/board straight on the bottom of the joists - not in-between? " I do not know what p board is...but: The issue here is establishing the mass/air/mass that is part of each feature you are going to design in this build, or any build. You have some heavy stuff, like sheet-rock on a framed wall(mass) and then you have distance between the next barrier(which is the air) and then you have some heavy stuff, like sheet rock on a framed wall(mass). This M/A/M (mass/air/mass) is part of the walls and the ceiling, not so much the floor but ONLY because it is often a 4 inch concrete slab damped by the Earth. This is enough to pseudo match the TL of the walls and not cause the builder to get a head ache with the whole "lord, do I need to float a floor" issue. So if you attach this "p board" directly to the bottom of the joists, two things happen that are in direct opposition to what you are trying to gain. One is that you establish the M/A/M with a minimum of mass on the upper barrier(the underside of the upper floor area). Second is that you effectively reduce the rooms headroom by using this method you suggest rather then methods that have already been proven to be more effective in re-claiming or at least maintaining the existing headroom.

Several things Chris, if you have a moment. Installing the P/Board directly to the underside of the existing joists is not going to be as effective as you can get with some planing and time. The inside out walls do not gain you any height, far from it. It is not the goal of the inside out wall to gain vertically but to save/gain floor space, square footage/meters. So using them[inside out walls] in your situation may well be a good choice but to be the most effective you still have to frame a ceiling directly on these new walls that does not connect in any way to your existing building. It will require some planing, keeping the NEW ceiling joists running in the same direction [parallel] with the existing joists is your way to go about doing this. I think you should add mass to the overhead existing floor first.

"the ceiling would then attach to both brown and yellow walls only and not including purple/green walls. The same for the ceiling in the 'purple' room - it will be attached to the outside and inside of the double stud walls (purple and brown only)," A few things. You may want to read up on mass/air/mass, look into some of Johns designs and some of the builds happening here. Reason being is this. Your existing warehouse, brick walls and concrete floors is a real nice start for anyone. You have to consider the existing perimeter wall as your first partition in a two partition [double wall] configuration. That is the easy part. Once you get this one little glitch worked out, it should start becoming more evident of the ceiling placement and how it fits into your framing. Essentially, you will have your existing building wall as the first wall then you will frame up separate rooms that have a ceiling sitting directly on top of this per room framing. This will be the second wall, will create the air gap and keep the framing decoupled as you are wanting to do.
