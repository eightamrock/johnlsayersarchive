xaMdaM9 April 2005#6
flygrade, I'd say only as a general statement that yes, bamboo is going to be OK for some of your finish millwork. Note that I conditionally say "some" of your millwork. I haven't worked with enough of the species to comment on how stable it is. (warping, cupping, twist) As far as I know, it will likely be a real challange to get much 1" thick material. Then too, most of the bamboo that I've seen here locally in the states is in the .25" thickness range. (Laminate flooring thicknesses) If this is indeed the typical thickness of the species, you'll be glueing up an awful lot of it to do any major millwork. But if you've got an abundant supply, a good sharp jointer and planer, plenty of glue/clamps and lots of time, go for it! Bamboo is a bit hard to rough sand because of the fiber content. However, it seems to finish out fairly easily if you can start from a medium/fine grit of paper. It doesn't hold dark stains too well from what I recall. Other than that, what little bit I've worked with held edges well and drilled up fine. Again, I'm certainly no authority on bamboo, so I have no idea as to it's suitability for anything structural. Good luck, Max

John Sayers12 April 2005#8
1) If I would paint the wood in the studio would that affect the positive acoustic properties of wood?
No - painting makes no difference.
2) In general how does wood have a positive influence on the room acoustics? Does it absorb or diffuse? What are the properties of wood when we're talking about acoustics?
wood is reflective as a straight surface, if used in slots it's still reflective but the resonator aborbs low mids. The more rigid the wood the lower the frequency of reflection i.e. Hardwood reflects more than softwood. Wood used in a panel absorber reflects but the panel absorber absorbs low frequencies. cheers john

knightfly19 April 2005#12
Steel will not resonate very well (when it does, buildings tend to fall down...).. this sounds dumb, but a resonator MUST resonate to function... hence using an extremely stiff piece of material would (I think) defeat the purpose... This would be true if the SLATS were what was supposed to resonate in a slat absorber - but they're NOT. In fact, you want the absorbent in a Slat resonator (AKA Helmholtz) to be AGAINST the backs of the slats, with a cloth cover between them (the cloth increases helmholtz action by adding to the resistance of the "air column' between the slats) - the "resonance" is a function of the air column behind the slats, the "acoustic resistance" of the slot between the slats, and the permeability of the material that's "blocking" the slots. About the only thing that should change the result of a(properly designed) slat resonator as far as the SLATS are concerned, is their width, thickness, and spacing. Oh, and somewhat the amount of higher frequencies your slat material of choice reflects. Everything else you see from the front is purely aesthetic, other than possibly some materials being harder to damp; if you like Cocobola or Zebra wood or Walnut,(or concrete or steel, for that matter) then do it...
