Ok, I read the posting requirements, and I'm going to endeavour to give you all the information I can. First of all, this is an attempt to document my build and share my experiences. I've spent the last few weeks devouring several books, this forum, and many other similar sites. I've come up with a plan that fits my budget - but it must be understood that it's not the greatest build plan out there. I could get way more detailed and extensive with my renovations, but I have neither the time nor the money. I'm doing this build in a very particular way: I'm trying to build it in phases, so that we can get to using the space as soon as possible (we're being kicked out of my guitarist's basement by his wife, and need somewhere else ASAP) for basic practicing. Then over time, and as I have additional funds, I will add to the build. To begin with, I have a room within an already finished basement (it was finished when we bought the house). Just having a finished basement did a great deal to dampen the sound coming out of the house - I was surprised (I've never owned a house with a finished basement, and was used to practicing in unfinished basements). Shortly after we moved in, I setup my PA system down there, and my bass rig, and ran my iPod through them, then walked around the house, inside and out. I was amazed at how much the sound was dampened. Inside the house, in the room just above, it's still pretty loud (hardwood floors on the subfloor), though not loud enough that you couldn't watch TV in that room if you turned it up just a bit. I walked outside and found that I could hear the music while standing right next to the house, but it sounded like it was very far away, as if a few rooms away. I walked to my two nearest neighbors houses and could barely hear anything - they didn't even notice it until I pointed it out to them. Well, that was a relief. So only if the neighbors are outside or inside with windows open and they're in the rooms facing our house, should they be able to hear us. But most everyone in this neighborhood runs central air, and the white noise from the external AC units masks the sound completely. Now, I know things could get a bit louder with a real drumset, two guitar amps, me pounding away on my bass rig, etc., plus I wanted to isolate the room from the rest of the house a bit more, so I am proceeding to update the sound qualities of this room in this build. Luckily I have a lovely, tolerant wife who not only puts up with my music, but encourages this and other such creative endeavours. She's very excited that our house will be the new hub for the various bands and music projects I'm involved in. And I'm very grateful to have her support, though I understand that she may not always feel this way, so I'm going to do my best to isolate the sound to that room alone. (since this seems long-winded, I'll continue in another post...)

Because of budget and time constraints (like I said, I have to make the space available for practice ASAP), I'm working with the existing construction as much as possible. The room currently is 12x12, with the fourth wall not a complete wall - it has a large open doorway in the middle of it. I'm knocking out that wall, and rebuilding it about 7 feet away, this time with a door that will close. The final size of the room will be 12x19. The room will be setup with the main control console in the room, mainly because I'll be the one running the Logic rig/mixer, and be playing much of the time as well. But I'll also have a small removable panel in the wall to pass through cables, so that I can set up the control console in the next room if necessary. In the event of passing those cables through, I'll use yet to be determined techniques to make a decent seal around them. The current room has two walls that are studded against the poured concrete foundation. In the attached drawings these are the wall to the left, and the wall at the bottom. There's little I can do to these walls without removing the drywall, which I'm not interested in doing at this point. Frankly, they seem to be doing a pretty good job absorbing sound. The top wall in the drawing is just drywall on studs, and open on the other side. The other side is open to a very small server room (~12x7), a storage room, and the furnace/water heater. My plan is to put fibreglass batts between the studs on that wall, then cover the wall with Audimute Peacemaker rubber sheeting (basically a budget and environmentally friendly version of MLV), and then put on a layer of drywall up on that side. But that's actually the very last step of my build, because it's not absolutely necessary to use the room. But since it will definitely enhance the sound isolation quality of the room, I'll be doing it eventually. (Also, the fact that I have to work around the furnace, well that's put it on the back burner as well.) The wall to the right of the drawing is the all new one I spoke of. Since I am building that from scratch, that will be the most sound dampened wall yet. Basically it will be framed in with 2x4s, with an foam-filled metal exterior door used as the door. From what I've read here, I know it's not the idea door, but it was only $116 at Lowes, frame included. I'll use some of the techniques described in this forum to dampen and seal the door better than it is, but I'm not going to go through the trouble of the Super Door treatment - with all the other weaknesses this room will have, the door needs to just be good, not great. The area on the other side of this wall is the rest of my finished basement, which will mainly be used for entertaining (but not while we're recording, so I'm not that worried about a bit of sound bleed. After framing, this wall will have a layer of Audimute Peacemaker rubber sheeting applied, then a layer of drywall. From the outside I'll put fibreglass batts between the studs, then another layer of Audimute Peacemaker, then drywall. Although I could double drywall it, I'm saving that for a later upgrade for the whole room if I decide it needs it (I'm hoping not). (and now the ceiling... continued...)

Well, before I get to the ceiling, let's talk more about the outside environment. We live in the city, but it's in a suburban area. We're on a cul de sac, and the closest houses to us are about 50-60ft away - to the south (side) of the house and to the west (back). Every other house is about 85ft away or more. The room is in the northwest corner of the house , so that means two of the walls of the room are against the foundation, and both those wall face the house to the west. These two closest houses are the only two I'm really worried about disturbing, but mainly just the house to the west, due to the proximity of the room, and the fact that the house to the south has their garage between us, and they always run their AC. The other big concern, as I mention earlier is not letting all this sound bleed upstairs and fill the entire house, so that one cannot watch TV or relax in other parts of the house. Although the wife is tolerant and even encouraging, I don't want to press my luck - this is going to be my musical domain for the rest of our lives. I know that her tolerance might wear thin one day if I'm not careful. The ceiling of the room was previously a drop ceiling. I've removed and thrown away the cheap fibreglass-based panels, but left the grid up. The subfloor above the joists is right under the hardwood floor of our living room and part of the kitchen (stone tile) upstairs. I've basically come to the realization that without major changes to the flooring upstairs (which we just put in a year ago before I ever had plans on building this basement studio), I just have to live with the fact that we can't record while people are walking around upstairs. I've talked to my wife about this extensively, and she agreed that she won't mind spending time on the second floor or out of the house when we're in recording mode (which won't be as often as practice mode). So, I've applied Audimute Peacemaker sheeting to the underside of the subfloor between the joists. It's reduced walking sounds from the floor above a little, but not much. Its more there to absorb sounds going upwards from the room. My plan is to then fill the space between the joists with several layers of fibreglass batting. Then I'm going to replace the drop ceiling tiles with a thicker, stronger acoustic tile that Armstrong makes. It's a fiberboard type, with a good acoustic rating. Each of these tiles is 2'x4'. I have 2'x4' Auralex-type wedge foam sheets, and I'm going to spray mount them to the ceiling tiles. Then when I drop them back in the grid, the foam will be against the grid. This will keep the tiles from touching the grid directly, and, I hope, prevent any rattling from the grid. I've already played my bass rig extensively down there before I pulled out the old tile and didn't notice any major rattling or resonance in the grid. So I hope between the Peacemaker sheeting on the subfloor, the batting between the joists, and the acoustic fiberboard tile in the drop ceiling, I'll absorb enough sound to make living upstairs from the room tolerable, and maybe even make it so my wife can watch TV directly above us while we practice. And the wedge foam is intended just to break up any slap-back off the flat surface of the ceiling tile. Before anyone says it, I do know it won't do much if anything to dampen the sound leaving the room. (next... current room status and wall plans... continued...)

Control Room Window. Well, since I don't actually have a control room, and will rarely be using the Logic console outside of the room, I'm not too worried about having a window into the space. After reading all the gory details on just how difficult a job it is to install one properly, I've opted not to do so. So I had an idea that to me seems much more logical, cost effective, and just plain simpler than an actual window to the control room: A webcam. Or several. Then anyone can you want to give access to the cam can see in the studio, not just the control room. I'm actually amazed this hasn't become a more standard practice for budget studio setups. It just seems logical to me. And we get the added benefit of being able to dump a live cam (or at least periodically updated images) out to our band's website for promotional purposes. You can pick up WiFi cam that has its own built-in webserver (doesn't require a PC for broadcast) - so you can hit it from any browser - for about $100, but I've found refurbed units for half that price. We plan on installing several in the room.

As far as my tests with the Peacemaker stuff, I've found it's a comparable, lower-cost, and more environmentally friendly version of MLV. I've tested it directly against the MLV, and I've found very similar results. MLV is way more expensive and heavier (thus cost more to ship), and in my experience building a room divider with it (on plywood) I found that it worked almost as well as MLV twice it's thickness. And it's much easier to work with. The fact that it's mostly made of recycled materials is what turned me on to it in the first place, but the price is what brought me back. I didn't get to talking about the bass traps last night, I was tired and needed to go to bed. I have wedge bass traps for all four corners of the room that extend from floor to ceiling. I had considered using the Audimute blankets to build my own absorbers (stretch the blanket over a 2x2 frame, fill cavity with rockwool or cotton batting, and cover with fabric for a finished look). It's been done with much success in the past, but I'm most concerned with sound leakage (neighbors) - so I'm more apt to cover the walls completely with the blankets. And the Auralex-type stuff is just to soften the ceiling, not for major absorption. I was hoping to avoid all the drywall work, but I could always opt to add a second layer of drywall with green glue or Peacemaker or MLV in between the current layer, and then just make absorbers (as described above) with the blankets instead of covering the walls with them. I just want to keep the peace with my neighbors and in my house.
