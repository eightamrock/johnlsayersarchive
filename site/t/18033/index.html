Hello forum members, My wife and I recently bought a house in Central/Western Massachusetts. The house has a 3-car garage that we are planning to convert into a recording studio and art studio (for my wife). The garage is detached from the house, with a sunroom/breezeway connecting the house to the garage. The inner dimensions of the garage are 36' x 24'. Height from concrete slab is approximately 8.5' to the bottom of the roof peaks and 14' to the top of the roof peaks. Right now the garage is completely uninsulated. It already has electricity and lights. Separate electrical panel from the house. Five windows and one doorway to the sunroom/breezeway. The garage has a concrete floor. Slab on grade. So a vapor barrier and insulation will need to be part of the design plans for flooring. The house has a radon mitigation system, so we might need a radon mitigation system for the garage as well.

I would say that the projects that come into my studio range from singer-songwriters to 7-piece bands. Rock, Reggae, Latin, Blues, Celtic, Dixieland, Klezmer, African, etc. - Although I do receive some income from my recording studio, the bulk of my income comes from performing (trombone and keyboards) in about seven bands that perform regionally (as well as occasionally touring nationally and internationally) and teaching instrumental music at three elementary schools. So the recording studio income is a "piece of the pie" for me, and not my full-time job. In any case, it will take a LONG time for the studio to pay for itself!! - My budget for this garage conversion is $25k maximum (including all finishing and room treatments). Of course, if it can be done for $15k, then my wife and I can spend the extra money on other home improvements such as a greenhouse, standby generator, tankless water heater, etc. - In the previous incarnations of my studio (in other houses), I did not have maximum isolation/soundproofing from room to room. Standard construction. For my purposes, I was still able to get good results. If there was a faint sound of drums bleeding into a close-miked guitar amp placed in a different room, it never presented a serious issue when mixing. And of course drum bleed into a scratch lead vocal track doesn't matter, since subsequent recording sessions (without the whole band) would be dedicated to replacing the scratch vocal track with a new lead vocal track. Of course the vocalist would be in a different room than the drummer while recording scratch vocals. Usually I like to have the leader of a session (often the leader is the vocalist, but not always) in the control room with me while he/she is recording scratch vocal tracks, mainly to make our communication easier. I like to place an electric bassist in the same room as the drummer for good eye contact and groove, but the bass would be recorded direct. So no bleed issue. A keyboardist could be in the same room also, and recorded direct. Even an electric guitarist could be in the same room as the drums, provided that his/her guitar amp would be miked in a different room. This approach worked out just fine in my old studio(s) even using standard wall construction. Of course, for a jazz session with acoustic bass along with drums, they would have to be in two isolated rooms, preferably with a sightline. In my old studios I got around this either by 1) giving up the sightline, or 2) I would play a scratch keyboard bass line from the control room while the drummer was recording, and then we would replace my keyboard bass line with the acoustic bass afterwards. My point in saying all of this is that I don't want to start obsessing about trying to get STC levels of 65 or more, especially if it's going to make this studio conversion impossible to afford. The contractor/designer that I'm working with has mentioned to me that I should have the desired STC goal in mind so that appropriate materials and techniques can be chosen. And of course a room is only as isolated as its weakest link (doorways. windows, wall penetrations for electrical boxes, etc.). At the moment I don't know exactly what STC value I'm aiming for -- it might be helpful to get some input on this. Am I getting a bit carried away here?
:) :!: Brian, please, please don't take this the wrong way, as it's not intended the way it might look, but instead of trying to crush and munge and mangle other designs into your space, it really might be a better idea to stop and learn the principles of studio design, so you can design your own studio, that meets YOUR needs. I hope I'm not offending you with that, since that isn't my intention at all, but seriously, it isn't a good idea to just distort the shape and size of other studios to try to force them into your building. Most studios are designed specifically for the available space, shape, size and ratios, so stretching and compressing that is not the best way of going about it, as the acoustics would be most likely be totally inappropriate for the shape you end up with. Even a relatively small change in the shape or dimensions of a studios can have large repercussions on how it sounds. Taking someone else's design and adapting it to your space is a good idea, but that's not what you are doing. Adapting means taking into account the shape and dimensions of YOUR space, and modifying the design accordingly. It would be far better to spend a few months learning the basics of studio design, so you can understand the implications of each part, and how you need to change it to make it work for your layout. You need to follow a plan, starting with the basics and working out from there. For example, when I design a studio, when it comes to the Control Room, I start with the speakers since those are the heart and soul of the entire purpose of what you are doing. You haven't even mentioned what speakers you are using, but that should be a key point in designing your control room. Or if you don't have the time to spend a few months learning acoustics, then maybe you could consider hiring a designer (such as John) to come up with something for you. The amount you spend on that would probably be well spent in the long run. Sorry if all this sounds a bit rough, and my intention is to help you, not offend you, but that really is the way I see it. Each studio is designed specifically for the space it has to fit in to, the purpose it has to perform, the equipment and people that will go into it, and even the type of music that will be recorded and mixed. Yours should be the same: specifically designed to make the best use of that space, for YOUR needs, and with the optimum layout, correct geometry, good dimensions, and suitable treatment, all designed together to work harmoniously. I just don't see that you are going to get to that point by following your current course of action. - Stuart -
