BriHar12 October 2011#2
The ratios can be used for both rooms and refers to the inner room - specifically to the hard boundaries, i.e. you should ignore the cork which will have only a slight modifying effect on the modes. Your outermost boundaries are already rectangular, the inner structures should use the space efficiently, so yes the walls should follow the same lines as the room adjacent (the minimum distance between any two leaves of an isolation wall determines the maximum isolation possible, so splaying walls in opposition to another is a waste of space). The most critical room is the control room which should be as symmetrical as possible, so design this room first. The live room can be asymmetric, so splayed walls here too are good. The reason for maintaining the hallway between the pillars is unclear as this hall doesn't seem to go anywhere, but perhaps your drawing is misleading (not showing everything). You describe the wall construction (concrete with 6" cork facing) but not how thick the concrete is. The ceiling is also described as poured concrete. Does this also have a cork surface? It would be advantageous to consult the owner or architectural plans to have a more detailed look at the construction of this ceiling - specifically in regard to the floor above. What you need to know is whether this already constitutes a double leaf, in which case, adding a third leaf in the form of a suspended ceiling for your rooms could in fact compromise the existing isolation between you and the club upstairs.

I have been reading several studio building books about the best room ratios to start off with before room correction.
Don't get too hung up on ratios: The idea is to stay away from the bad ones, and get close to a good one, but that's about it. Theoretical prediction of room modes is fine and fun, but reality will kick you r tail end anyway, and more than likely the actual modal behavior won't match the theory very well. You'll have to measure the room response once the shell is completed anyway, to decide on the treatment, so don't go overboard playing with rations, and trying to finesse things down to the last micron! Ball-park is good enough.
1:1.60:2.33 or 1:1.50:2.10 ratios. But as I started to map out where I could place walls to give me those ratios, I found myself with some un-answerable questions. Should those ratios noted by Sepmeyer and Louden
Actually, 1 : 1.6 : 2.33 is originally from Bolt, not Sepmeyer, ifi I recall correctly, but who cares! Both are good, and so are dozens of others. :)
Should those ratios noted by Sepmeyer and Louden be used for both the control room and the live room?
The control room is far more important, as the final goal is an even modal spread and a neutral room response. For the live room, the goal is usually to have some type of "character", perhaps variable, that enhances the recording, or at least doesn't detract from it, so there you might actually want some type of modal response... depending on what you want to do in there. But since live rooms are often non-parallel and non-rectangular, it's a moot point anyway: ratios go out the window as soon as things become non-rectangular.
Does the ratio describe the "inner room" or "the outer room"
Inner "room". The ratio refers to the inner surface of the final inner leaf. In other words, it refers to the hard, massive solid, rigid surfaces you see as you look around you, when you lay down your hammer after completing the isolation part of your build, right before you pick up the hammer again to start the treatment part of your build. (Isolation and treatment are two very different things.).
If I am building a room within a room, can the outside room be rectangular and the inside room have splayed walls?
The outside room is already rectangular! It is the "poured concrete walls and ceiling", plus the one last leaf that you still need to build. You can build that last one at an angle if you want, but fitting it around the pillars makes it more feasible to just build it straight across. But to answer your question: Yes, the inner-leaf can be splayed if you want it to be, regardless of the shape of the outer leaf. As Brian pointed out, isolation is limited by the smallest air gap, so you need to take that into account if you plan to splay your walls, ceilings, doors or windows: the shortest distance is the weakest link.
Or do I have to build both rooms (the room as well as room inside it) with matching splayed walls.
Not necessary. You splay your inner-leaf walls for very specific reasons: 1) To help create an RFZ design, if that is what you want. 2) To help eliminate flutter echo. 3) To move modal response around (assuming you have some type of FEM/FEA tool to do the analysis, and that you know how to use it!) 4) To help give the live room "character". 5) Because it looks damn cool! Apart from that, there aren't too many other valid reasons for splaying! #1 and #2 are the normal reasons. So if you splay, then great. But as soon as you do that, you can throw your modal calculators out the window, as the only apply to rectangular rooms. They are no use at all for non-rectangular rooms, except for the axial modes on any remaining parallel walls, but the axial response on the non-parallel walls, as well as some of the tangential response and all of the oblique response is no longer valid. Splaying the walls shifts the modes to different locations (and therefore different frequencies), which cannot be predicted with simple tools. But like I said before, prediction may or may not match reality, and you will have to measure the response of the complete inner-leaf anyway, prior to deciding on treatment, so it doesn't really matter if you splay or not. If you like splayed rooms (I do!) then base it on a good ratio, and go from there, bu realizing that the splay invalidates the predictions from that point on.
I have more questions but lets start there. Thanks. Sorry For the long winded post.
Long-winded, detailed posts are good! so are lots of smart questions, such as yours. The only dumb question is the one you don't ask! Regarding your layout: I think it can be improved a lot, and splaying will help to get through those pillars neatly, maximizing the CR size, which is one of your design goals, so I'd go for it, provided that you understand the reason why you are doing it, and the implications. But you are forgetting one very important point: Budget. Since this is a commercial venture, I guess you are fine there, but it would still help us to know what kind of budget you have in mind, as that can also affect the recommendations on building materials and techniques. For example, big budget means you can have nice big windows from the CR to LR, with good isolation, as well as lots of large doors. Low budget means you can't have any window, and only one small door. There are other issues you haven't mentioned yet that are critical: HVAC and electrical. Studios are hermetically sealed (at least twice), and surrounded by thick insulation, so they DO need a good HVAC system to both keep them cool and also keep the people inside them alive, by supplying fresh air and removing stale air, all while maintaining the isolation to the outside word, and the isolation between rooms. The electrical system is also a potential path for sound to get around between the rooms and to the outside world. All of these need to be considered very carefully in the design! One last thing. You said:
... until I build the "inner-room" (floated, decoupled, etc),
Yes, you totally do need to decouple, but no, you most likely do NOT need to float your room(s). Very few studios need that. And if you do need it, then add a couple of zeros to your budget, since fully floating a studio that size is not going to be cheap! It is a very common misconception that studios have to be floated: the majority do not. You might or might not need to do that, but this will be decided by the next stage in your design: figuring out how much isolation you really need, in terms of dB TL, by using a sound level meter and actually measuring (not guessing!). That number is the basis for the isolation design. - Stuart -
