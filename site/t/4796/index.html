sharward7 December 2005#6
Typically, each doubling of mass gains you about 6db of isolation... So you may have to also beef up that wimpy outer leaf as well. You may have to tear out all of the drywall -- including the original layer -- to gain access so that you can beef up between the studs. That's what I did... I beefed up with four layers of drywall plus a layer of OSB between the studs on my outer leaf, in addition to the original stucco.

knightfly8 December 2005#12
If you're up for massive amounts of tedium, the least waste of materials (already used) would be to carefully cut your two layers of inside gypsum off the studs with a razor knife (or a Roto-zip) - you'd need to use a stud finder (a good one) to find your exact stud location so you can cut the inside layers right along the studs, then use that gypsum to do inserts between your existing studs - 2 layers should do. (Only way I know to find your studs with that construction is to first find the furring strip, then re-zero the finder (off/on) and then move the finder horizontally til you find the stud - you can cut the gyp about 1/4" toward the center of the stud from its edge and still miss the fasteners (usually) - Check out sharward's thread on beefing up between studs. Then, a new frame about 6" in from the old one, and at LEAST two layers of 5/8 gypsum on that - real acoustic caulk, plus all the tricks in the REFERENCE section. Fill between the studs with standard fiberglass insulation, paper facing to the INSIDE of the room, etc - Inner frame should run about $400 or so, 2 layers of gyp for walls about $320, caulk about $80, ceiling gyp about $250, ceiling inner frame (for full room-in-room construction) 2x8's on 16" centers will span 18 feet and let you put two layers of 5/8 drywall (gyp) up for an inner ceiling... If you want to stop drums, you'll need all the help you can get - it's do-able, but not without using 2-leaf construction... Steve

len-morgan9 December 2005#21
nashtalon wrote:
In the following drawing there's three pieces of 5/8" rock in the cavity...nice and massy. However, at the point where the OSB is attached to the stud, wouldn't that be considered a weak link?
If you built it the way you drew it, yeah, your not going to improve your situation any. You are showing (at the "bottom" of your drawing) what looks like a layer of gyp. If you do it this way, you've really only added one layer of 5/8" and a little air space and that won't cut it for knocking down drums. You want to leave the wall OPEN with the insulation exposed then build another wall right in front of it a few inches away (as much as you can afford to loose but no more than 12" or so. That measurement by the way is from the BACK of the mass on the outer wall the the back of the mass (gyp) on the new inner wall so if you put your two layers of 5/8" beteen the studs, you'll have about 1 1/2" of gap to the inside of the stud. If you put your new wall just 3 1/2 inches away from the the current studs (i.e., one 2x4 width), you'll have an 8 1/2" gap to the back of the inner most sheet of gyp on the new wall. 8 1/2" is a very healthy gap you aren't really going to loose that much floor space. You'll want to rest new ceiling joists on these new walls (and ONLY on these new walls - don't touch the structure of the building). It's a lot of work but you should end up with a very useable room that won't bother the neighbors and I don't see why you couldn't stay within your budget (the original $3K). len

sharward12 December 2005#25
Sorry for the stickershock. :( It happens to all of us. For cooling, yes. Mini splits are awesome from what I've read. However, be prepared to spend big money for them. A single small cooling-only system will probably run you close to $3K installed. However, be aware that they only cool the air in the room -- there is no fresh air coming into the room from outside, nor is there any stale air from the room being flushed to the outside. (Most air conditioners don't cycle in fresh air, by the way.) Hence, you need ventilation. Your room won't have any windows, and code requires that you have either an window or mechanical ventilation that will exchange all of the air in the space a certain number of times per hour (the unit of measure is ACH -- Air Changes per Hour). My thread covers this challenge in detail. That was one of my major stumbling blocks in being granted a permit by my city's building department. I finally broke through the logjam by looking at comfort and ventilation as two separate requirements, and in my case, I designed two separate solutions. Good luck. :-)
