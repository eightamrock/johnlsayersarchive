Forgot to mention - drywall mud cures better in AIR; so instead of the method I suggested above, it's probably better (and faster cure) if you use a LONG straight edge and strike off the mud flush with your stiles and rails, let it cure, sand it, and THEN add MLV and ply. This way you'll know whe the mud is dry enough, and won't be tempted to stand the door up early, causing a sag and having to start over. Technically, the cable should be in a junction box. But given the whole unit will be encased in a silencer, I'm forgoing the box and walking on the wild side of danger :P . Here's why. Consider the old time electric conductor, Knob and Tube wires, which are allowed to exist in walls, attics, and spaces unlikely to be trespassed on by people. K&T in and of itself is relatively safe, even though most of the time, especially in old homes, the insulation is worn away leaving an exposed wire. But since the hot wire and the neutral are usually spaced a safe distance apart, chances are slim there will be a problem. Now modern electric cable, commonly called Romex, with it's insulation composed of synthetic material is very safe, as long as the circuit is not overloaded. Given the cable connections will be encased in the silencers, which themselves will act as giant junction boxes, albeit with limited firestopping ability, I see this setup to be sufficient. There will be an access panel to service the fans. Was I rambling? :P

Building codes evolve (read: get more strict over time) for a reason. Oftentimes the reasons are buried in six-foot depth plots. Using your logic, we should just forego boxes altogether and just attach outlets and switches to walls with the connections inside the wall cavity. Fans vibrate. Wirenuts loosen, particularly when subjected to vibration and numerous hot/cold cycles over time. Here's an article on the Better Homes & Gardens Web site about Choosing Electrical Junction Boxes. What causes electrical fires?
    Electrical fires most commonly occur at "weak points" in the system, these are where the cables have been cut and joined, or where they have been cut and connected to a power point, light or similar. These points can enable moisture or dirt to build up across "live" terminals, or, the connections may loosen with time and localised heating will occur.
...also...
    An electrical fire can be defined as a fire where an electric current or electrical fault is found to have been the ignition source. Some examples would be . . . Overloaded wiring - Where the electric current flowing in the wires exceeds the rating of the cables. The wiring heats up and melts the insulation and can set fire to flammable material nearby. . . Loose wiring connections - The current flowing through the wiring encounters resistance at the connection and generates heat. This can start a fire in the wall at the back of a power point, in a wiring junction box in the ceiling, above light fittings or inside a switchboard. . . Electrical "arcing" (or sparking) - Where wiring insulation has been damaged by an external occurrence**, and which lets the copper conductors inside a cable just touch one another, or to just make contact with the metal case of an appliance. . . (Source)

Actually...twisting conductors together for splices and then soldering them is still an acceptable method of making connections. In the NEC 110.14(B) this sentence appears: “Soldered splices shall first be spliced or joined so as to be mechanically and electrically secure without solder and then be soldered.” Tightly twisting the conductors together makes them mechanically and electrically secure and prepares them for solder. So soldering, IS permissible. Also, HMP solder has a melting point of 565-574°F. The insulation on NM cable (non-metallic cable commonly called Romex, which is a brand name) has a melting point of around 340°F degrees. If one were to exceed the ampacity of a given conductor, the insulation would go long before the solder (although the breaker or fuse should trip first). In addition, the conductor is mechanically secured, i.e. twisted, thereby preventing any arcing. While wire nuts are acceptable, I prefer soldering, as it's simply a better, stronger, more reliable connection...period.

Thanks for the compliments, Len. Good for you on painting. At that stage, the light at the end of the tunnel is very bright!!!! I know exactly the excitement you're feeling. The hook and eye closer is fairly tight and when I played some LOUD music yesterday, there was no rattling at all :P I suppose I could've fashioned a more elaborate way to keep the plug in place, but didn't see the need. It's not a vent hatch, but rather the breaker box is behind there. The orange sound absorbers/diffusers were made by me out of Luaan, as a backer, and 1"x2"s, as the rails. Basically I made a shallow box. I ripped the Luaan to two different sizes, 4'x4', and 3'x5'. Then attached the 1"x2"s. I then filled them with cotton batting and stretched fabric over them, which I found at Walmart for $1.00/yard. I added thick picture frame wire to the back to hang them. I fashioned some cleats on the walls, attaching to the studs (BTW, I marked every stud's location on the subfloor for easy reference, as the stud finder has a hard time penetrating two layers of 5/8" rock). The diffuser/absorbers worked sectacularly. Before, there was a lot of slap echo, now it's fairly dead in there but still has a nice sound. I can always add a bit of reverb to vocals, drums, or whatever through the soundboard. The flooring, which I think I failed to mention is that rubber mat that fits together, kind of like a jigsaw puzzle. One side is various colors and the other is black, which I used. This stuff is very hardy and laid down easily. Sam's Club had them in packs of 8 for $15, with each square being 2'x2' for a total of 32 square feet/pack. I used ten packs and got a nice floor for $150. It's easy on the feet and way better than standing on hard concrete. It cleans easily, resists stains, and is very durable. And if one piece becomes damaged, it's easy to pull it out and replace. I may rent this out on an informal basis to other groups or someone looking to record in a quiet environment. This room is nicer than most of the rehearsal spaces over on Music Row and more effective. The place I used to rent wasn't built properly and the metal band next door to us could be heard pretty easily through the walls. But I've had a lot of people asking if I'd consider renting it out, so I may. Be nice to recoup a little of the costs.
