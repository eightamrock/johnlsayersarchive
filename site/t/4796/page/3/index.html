knightfly15 December 2005#53
If your exterior doors are foam filled, adding MLV can help increase mass - filled doors also means they will each act as only one leaf (good thing) - but the most important thing you need to pay attention to is your SEALS. Without good seals, it's not gonna matter much WHAT doors you use. Also, don't worry about sound bouncing around between the wall leaves - the insulation will absorb it, and if you leave porous venting from the doorway into the wall cavity this will actually IMPROVE isolation of the doors a bit, helping them to "catch up" with the walls. Watch out for door knobs, etc - good way to lose whatever iso you've gained. Paying attention to detail and actually dismantling your door knobs to check for potential air leaks will help... Steve

knightfly17 December 2005#63
The basic idea will work; but not with 4" line, I'd go at least 8". And not PVC, you do NOT want any RIGID coupling between inner and outer leaves. The flex ducting will work, but you need to watch for any kinking that will reduce the cross-sectional area; this stuff is notorious for doing that. You'll need to figure out some braces, brackets, etc, that will keep it from collapsing at turn points. By going from 4" to 8", you'll cut your air velocity (assuming the same CFM) to 1/4 what it would have been (2x diameter = 4x area) - this will reduce any noise due to air movement by a HUGE amount while maintaining good air flow. Noise due to air movement is approximately = to the 4th or 5th POWER of the velocity, so a fairly small decrease in velocity = a LARGE decrease in noise. If you're in a dusty area, I'd put the fan on the outer end of the INLET side and put a furnace filter in front of it, which should be changed/blown out regularly - this will put a slight positive pressure in the room, so when you open the door it's more likely that dust will blow OUT of the room instead of INTO it. You might look for "booster fans" - these run quiet, they're round, and they can be inserted in the line anywhere (don't put one near your studio space, better at the OTHER end) - they're available in 4", 6", and 8" - possibly other sizes as well. HTH... Steve

sharward18 December 2005#65
You may have to have separate fans for fresh air intake and for exhaust. The sucking action at the exhaust fan is probably not sufficient to suck air in from the fresh air inlet. It will somewhat, but it might not be enough to meet code. The cool thing about the Fantech unit I'm getting is that the fans are integrated into a single unit. I believe there are also adjustments that can be made to keep the intake and exhaust pressure in balance. Remember that you may have to prove on paper that you'll have the proper amount of ACH -- air changes per hour -- in order to meet code. Don't count on the CFM ratings on the fan itself to get you over that hump -- ducting length, ducting type, ducting size, the number of bends, etc., all increase static pressure and thus reduce the effectiveness of the fan(s). In my case, I'm using a system that is actually designed for an entire house, yet I'm only using it for a single room -- I think that "overkill" factor prevented my having to provide detailed specs in terms of static pressure in my ducts. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward21 December 2005#71
Yes, the HRVs are pretty pricey... And as I've said before, they have two sizes: big and very big. They're intended for entire houses, not just a single room. However, entire houses have windows. Most studios do not. You're using a mechanical ventilation system to take the place of windows. Therefore, they have to be pretty powerful -- enough to exchange all of the air inside the room within a certain number of minutes when activated. That magic number is dependent upon building codes in effect in your township. Mine aren't necessarily the same as yours. Keep in mind that the fan's throughput rating (measured in CFM -- cubic feet per minute) is not the whole equation. Adding ducting, silencers, and bends will result in static pressure that reduces the efficiency/effectiveness of the fan. So, you start with a really high number, account for the losses, then end up with a smaller number that needs to exceed code. I admit to not doing those calculations in my case -- I think I was able to get away with not because I'm pretty sure the fan is overkill. (It does have different speed settings, so at a lower setting it may drop to down to just plain kill. ;-) ) The main reason I'm going with the HRV isn't the heat recovery aspect; it's the "all in one"ness of it that impresses me. Plus the filtering mechanism is nice. There's also the ability to tune it so that incoming and outgoing air flows are in balance (no negative or positive pressure in the room, in theory). It's also supposed to be fairly quiet. I am confident in the silencer plan because, well, what's good enough for Paul Woodlock is good enough for me! :lol: He had lots of great advice on his plan and great supervision during his build, and the results are nothing less than stellar. Mine's kind of the "baby Woodlock" version. ;-)
