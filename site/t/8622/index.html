Thanks for you understanding guys. Can I say - my main concern is for the many people who come to this site to get advice on how to fix up their basement studios. It's actually an American thing as we don't have basements in Australia and there's not many in Europe either. I can definitely see an application for Green Glue in basement ceilings as that is the major problem area and adding another frame is usually hindered by ceiling heights. What I'm concerned about is the overkill that's developing on this site. In most cases a double wall with one layer of 5/8" drywall is sufficient for isolation in a basement studio. If you need more, then two layers. I see no need for the triple layer, it's just overkill. Left Bank is just a double wall with a single layer of 5/8" and that studio can operate 24 hours if necessary. It's above ground and with 50 - 60 yards to the nearest neighbour. In Oasis we used a double 5/8" layer as the neighbours are closer and as expected the isolation is excellent. I'm pointing this out so that forum members don't feel obliged to build triple layer with green glue walls. It can get overtly expensive adding all those layers let alone having the added expense of GG. ;) cheers john

Brian Ravnaas17 April 2007#13
I don't doubt that there are some errors on the GGCo website. Almost all of the material that is on there has been for a while, and we're working on a total re-vamp. We had a fair ( :cry: ) amount of problems with "lost in translation", with translation being the process of going from some word document to the web. I will offer some basic thoughts on the cost calculations present on our site. The data should be stated as being taken from Craftsman publishings "National Construction Estmator" database, and derived from the national average or some specifically stated state. You can sort of browse the data that are present in the Estimator software by going to www.get-a-quote.net, which has state-by-state average material and labor costs for most everything you may want to use in construction (even including MLV). In some instances we have adjusted the labor rates DOWN for other products (MLV only comes to mind, as the Estimator had an estmated per-square-foot labor rate of >>$1/sq ft, which is just on the high side). The basic calculations are also at least a year (or much more) old and will be updated with the current estimates with the site changes. The Estimator software is just that and no more, but to make cost-calculations that are explorable and, well, based on something, we had to choose a standard reference for basic costs and that was it. Finally, the floor space cost adjustment is rarely relevant and should be ignored. But all that said, if anybody wants me to take a look at this data and put forth the piece-by-piece info from the software, i'd sure be happy to do so, but the chart seems reasonable. its 8 total layers of drywall in a conventional wall -vs- 6 with partial-coverage Green Glue in a GG wall. The costs of finishing, sealing, framing, insulation, etc. are the same, the drywall costs are the same in all instances, and the GG cost/labor rates would be based on our prices when the chart was made and wood floor adhesive rates from the software. I really do apologize if the info caused any confusion, but all prices are per square foot and based on the above source/calculation.

The question that you raise is without a doubt a very good one. I've only a brief amount of time to post this morning, but i would like to try to address that both from the standpoint of GG and not. The simplest and most relevant downside of a triple leaf system is that for a given amount of weight and space, you wind up with a higher frequency of MSM. And that means that whatever decuopling efforts you have will start to work for you at a HIGHER frequency, which is bad. The next downside is the potential for chaotic low-freq behavior (resonance behavior) as you introduce multiple systems. In some instances - such as the famous double stud system shown in quadruple, triple, and double leaves - this can be catastrophic. But moving past the basics and into your specific question, I do really feel that there is enough data and information available in the world and on my 'puter to address what you describe. What you need to do is build a wall in front of the existing wall with enough mass on both sides, and enough air space to force a predictable set of behavior from the now-triple leaf.
