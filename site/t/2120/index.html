knightfly14 August 2004#8
Panel resonators work by vibrating the front panel, which moves the air inside the (sealed) trap, which gets then absorbed partially by the insulation inside - bass losses occur first when expending the energy to move the front panel, then again when trying to navigate the interstices of the insulation. A light rear panel messes up the calculations, making it nearly impossible to predict the results. Probably the best and easiest FIRST thing to do to most rooms is to forget the "bass trap" paradigm, and just build some NON-backed frames (4' wide if possible) - large perforations in back board, cloth fronts, heavy/light insulation as before, and just stand them catty corner in the corners. Floor to ceiling if possible, two halves work as well as one tall one if it's easier to build/move. If tippy, a couple of screen door hooks and eyes or something to hold them against the wall; pieces of foam between frame and wall to kill rattles. If you need absorption at lower than maybe 200 hZ, and still want portable, build the panel traps to specific room mode frequencies but use a heavy (at least 3/4") MDF back so your calculations aren't totally out of whack... Steve

knightfly14 August 2004#10
Sorry, I know how you feel - every time I make the mistake of thinking I'm getting smarter about acoustics, I learn something new and more confusing. If I keep it up(and live long enough), I'll eventually graduate to "complete idiot" status... Even Ethan has discovered in recent years that broadband trapping is more effective in smoothing out a room than panel traps - his newer mini traps are totally different than the ones plans are available for. The simplest way to do your corners is to just put 4" material diagonally across them, preferably floor to ceiling, and as wide as you can get away with - the depth will be dictated by the width, wider/deeper is better but 3 feet will help a LOT. As on the other thread, if you can put a few inches of lighter weight fiberglass in front it will help for higher frequencies, which will improve stereo imaging. Anything else for these corners is just to hold the material in place, although you don't want solid backing if you build any sort of frame - typical pegboard has too small holes and will reduce low frequency absorption - one way to accomplish holding the absorbent in place might be to use 3-4 boards about 4" wide, running horizontally near top, middle and bottom of the trap across the back - mount impaling clips on these and just push the insulation onto the spikes to hold it in place - http://www.acousticalsolutions.com/prod ... _clips.asp These can be found locally at insulation contractors, or ordered from that link. In a pinch, you could just drill holes through the back boards slightly smaller than a 16d nail and angled slightly up toward the front, then drive 16d nails through from the rear and push the insulation onto them from the front. If you don't drill first, you might split the boards. I would drive 2-3 nails in a cluster, placed at each side and in the middle, and use 3 of these boards - bottom, middle, and top of a 4 foot high trap, two traps stacked per corner. flat against the wall versions of this, placed either side of the room on the walls, halfway between speakers and your head, will treat early reflections - finally, a "cloud" of the same material overhead does the same for the ceiling. More of the same on the wall behind you, preferably spaced several inches away from the wall, and you're ready to rock. If you need a sketch to visualize any of this, let me know... Steve

Sure, there are as many variations of this as there are builders - I'd be careful with diffusion in a small room, it takes about 8-10 feet distance from a diffusor (most of them, anyway) before you get consistend diffusion. Making one side of plywood can be good - one application would be to put two of these, plywood facing inward, one on each side of the mix position, and splayed inward at the fronts by about a foot - this can get rid of side reflections without deadening the sound, especially if the room already has enough soft stuff to kill higher freq's - Another thing - a couple of these units built with predominantly open backs can be spaced to different distances from walls to increase lower frequency absorption when necessary. I saw one design somewhere, where they built the frame from 1x4 or 1x6 (on edge, like a shadow box) and made stands from pipe floor flanges and lengths of pipe - a wider base (like 1x8 or 1x10) at the floor would help stability. knightfly14 August 2004#14
Yeah, quite a bit different; the plywood will reflect down to fairly low bass frequencies - there, the plywood should act somewhat as a panel resonator, frequency dependent on the thickness of the plywood mainly. It would be hard to calculate, I don't have formulas for open panel traps. Angle of incidence would also come into play. Two of these with plywood backs could be used during mixdown, one on either side of the console with plywood toward you, angled toward the rear of the room by about 25-30 degrees - this would fix early reflections from the sides, and the absorbent on the rear would tame reflections from other elements in the room that shouldn't be there acoustically. If you use the pipe stand idea, be aware that there could be unwanted resonances involved - I would plan to leave a pluggable hole in one end for filling with sand if necessary - you can find out about this a simple way; just hook up an audio oscillator to your monitoring system, crank it up to about 90 dB SPL, and slowly sweep from 20-20kHz - this will find any room rattles or resonances. Steel pipe will have them in the upper midrange, possibly as high as 5-6 kHz. If you don't have access to much in the way of audio test gear, here's a place you can get a few vital pieces in software, FREE - http://www.moonaudio.com/softwar5.htm Happy material mutilating... Steve
