It has been said, but might seem like it was not...your existing roof is your exterior leaf so anything you do within the confines of this project, like the massing up of the upper side of the joists and adding clips and hat-track to the lower side effectively produce a lower transmission assembly If you cannot install framing on the new interior wall assemblies then you best placement is this. Remove the interior ceiling sheathing if it exists, and install the clips and track, or RC2 and then install the mass for each individual room with no connection what-so-ever between these rooms. You will be installing a fire block at the top of wall to top of wall connection between the framing assemblies if ONLY for the purpose of eliminating/reducing sound transference from the exterior to the interior air space.

What size joist would I need to support 2 possible 3 layers of drywall? could I space them 24" on center?
That depends on a lot of factors, mostly the span, the load, and the type of wood. There are span tables that tell you what distance you can span with what live and dead load for what type of wood, for both 16" OC and 24" OC, but you should probably ask a local contractor or structural engineer, and also check your building code, to find out what is allowed. I doubt that you'll be able to go with 24" OC for three layers of 5/8": that's a lot of weight, even for 2x10, unless you are going over a very short span. But check the span tables and get professional advice in your area. You can't guess, and this is dangerous stuff: you don't want the whole roof coming down on your head because you misread a table or guess wrong! Your profile says you are in Hawaii, and that is earthquake territory, I think, so you don't want to get your structural integrity wrong... - Stuart -

Soundman202023 August 2011#48
Well, you WILL have a three-leaf system, but if you put enough mass on each leaf then it will still work, especially give the size of the air gaps. Another issue: on the inner leaf, where the drywall on the ceiling meets the drywall on the wall, don't do the joints like that: Stagger them. In other words, first a vertical, then a horizontal, then another vertical, then another horizontal. Not two verticals followed by two horizontals.
