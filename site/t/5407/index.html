sharward8 January 2006#4
In short, you need mass-air-mass. The concrete wall is pretty good mass by itself, so you probably won't have to do anything to increase the mass on that wall (something we often refer to as "beef-up"). So what you will need to do is have a good amount of space (air) between the concrete wall and your new wall, and then a good amount of mass on the new wall you construct. Your floating floor may or may not be needed. There are many, many considerations in the "to float or not to float" question. One factor, I would think, would be whether or not your floor vibrates when your neighbor is making noise. If it does not, then my guess is that you don't need to float a room. Do you have any measurements of the noise levels at various frequencies (especially the low ones) in your room when your neighbor is making noise? If you're sure their noise is going to be the determining factor, having these measurements will go a long way towards telling you (and us) just how much noise you have to stop, and thus how much air and mass you're likely to need to stop it. --Keith :mrgreen:

knightfly9 January 2006#22
Angled walls (as in, opposing walls NOT parallel to each other) has two main purposes - one, when angled a total of 12 degrees or more from each other, it stops flutter echo in the room WITHOUT the need for absorbents on those two walls, and (2) when angled even further, with the wider distance to the rear of the room, it can create a REflection Free Zone at the mix position - this is assuming that you are NOT mixing surround, and that you actually draw the geometric plan so any direct sound from either speaker reflects somewhere OTHER than back to the mix position. Splayed walls have NOTHING to do with isolation between rooms or to the outside, other than: if you make the air space between inner and outer masses LESS in order to splay walls, this narrower air gap will hurt isolation by raising the mass-air-mass resonance of that wall. If you want a more live sounding room, then properly angled walls can allow this WITHOUT the flutter than normally accompanies un-treated, parallel walls... Steve make the room as big as it can get and THEN treat it with absorbers in case I get "standing waves" or too much low end. There is no "in case", you WILL have standing waves in ANY room, regardless of shape. It's just harder to figure out WHERE they will be til the room is built, when you do non-parallel construction. Likewise, you WILL need bass traps in ANY room smaller tnan an auditorium, this is just part of "small room" acoustics. BTW, we're not talking about the inner and outer walls being non-parallel with each other, we're talking about the opposing INNER walls being non-parallel - IOW, your inner walls would form a trapezoid with the speaker end being narrower than the rear wall...
