sharward31 August 2005#5
I've become somewhat well-versed in this lately, and I had some of the same misconceptions as you at first... (Have you read through my thread starting at page 18 yet? ;-) ) In short, no, all the "blowing" going on at the heat pump (outdoor unit) is to allow the unit itself to breathe. The air being "returned" from the room/house is flowing back to the air handler, which heats (or cools) the air by blowing it over coils to change its temperature, then the same air is sent back to the room/house. Think of it this way -- the heat pump (or A/C) is for comfort (or, in some parts of the country, survival ;) ). But windows are for ventilation (and emergency exits) and natural light. If your room doesn't have a window, and it has "conditioned air," it's considered "habitable," and "habitable" needs a window, or mechanical ventilation, in order to be legal. The "natural light" requirements that are called for in building codes are usually easier to get around than the ventilation requirements. At least they allow for mechanical ventilation -- otherwise you'd have to have an operable window in your studio space -- think of what that would do to your iso! :shock: Page 18+ -- check it out... And if you're in a huge hurry, then skip to Sun Aug 14, 2005 on page 20. --Keith :mrgreen: P.S.: It's entirely possible that I'm "way off base" in my assessment of your situation... HVAC is certainly not the same everywhere and I'm certainly no expert. camistan wrote:
From what I was told, in this county, there is no code that says outside air has to be incorporated with inside or recirculating air for basement construction. I've asked 2 (of the 7 I called) and they both said the same thing.
I don't know if I believe them. :? Granted, I live in California, and my city has adopted California Building Code (CBC) as its model codes... And yes, they're stricter in some ways compared to other parts of the country. However, CBC is derived mostly from UBC (Universal Building Code). The code basically says that ventilation (mechanical or via window) may not be needed in non-habitable spaces, such as closets, utility rooms, etc. But non-habitable spaces can't be "conditioned," which means you're not allowed to heat or cool them for comfort. Once you condition the space, it becomes classified as habitable... And once it's habitable, it needs ventilation. Bedrooms and basements are even more strict because there must be an operable window for emergency egress. I leave room for the possibility that it may be true that your municipality does not require ventilation in habitable spaces. However, asking the contractors themselves may not be your best bet for getting the correct answer that applies to your situation. Ideally you should be contacting your municipality's building department yourself to determine (1) what model building codes they have adopted (probably UBC -- if so, look up 1203.2 & 1203.3) and (2) what that code calls for. Again, I'm not the code expert around here... even though I sometimes feel like I'm the in a minority of those who give a rat about it... ;-) If you care (and I hope for your sake that you do -- you seem to!), you'll get the official answer and you'll be able to work around whatever constraints that code requires. --Keith :mrgreen:

I'm not much with geometry, but I think 3.14 is pi and you'd be figuring the diameter of something. I think BTU to watts measurements need be calculated by 3.412. And that gets you BTU/hr. Actually though, well after some research, here's how I'd do it. (Amps the gear pulls) x (voltage it runs at)=VoltAmps(VA) x (power factor of .8 since 1VA doesn't equal 1WA) = Watts Then you'd have to take Watts x 3.412 BTU/Hr-W = BTU/Hr. So if the gear pulled 10 Amps at 120V, it'd look like this 10A x 120V = 1200VA x 0.8 (pf) = 960W. 960W x 3.412 BTU/HR-W = 3275.52 BTU/HR. I think 1200 BTU/Hr is equal to 1 ton of cooling capacity, but that might be different depending upon insulation or something, so that's very up for discussion.
