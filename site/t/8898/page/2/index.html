Nightmusic Be aware that this sheetblok is mainly an expensive mass with a low cost/effectiveness ratio. You can do this, but if you advice it someone else I feel the need to intervene. Certainly on that floor it's much cheaper and faster to glue the money meant for the sheetblok on the cement floor than the sheetblok itself. The acoustic effect will be similar. That Sylomer floor you linked somewhere is Paul Woodlock's studio (origin Studiotips), and he cut these pieces himself with a jigsaw. It's rather expensive if you order them cut, and it's not the standard manner Sylomer advises such application. They normally use densities adjusted to the static load they're to be used for. The wat Paul did it is to make it cheaper under the condition he did all that work himself. Lower densities of Sylomer are cheaper but not linear with the weight they can carry (hence 100% coverage with low density sylomer is more expensive than these blocks Paul made with fractional coverage). Sylomer comes from Getzner and is Austrian from origin. http://www.getzner.com/Werkstoffe/finished/index.php :? N & N This whole thing is very stylized and simplified. Floating floor and isolation: A floating floor is needed when in becomes a limiting factor in the isolation one wants to obtain. Some posts: http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.p ... ght=#36352 http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.p ... ght=#38728 Nikodemus:
Nikodemus wrote:
As i get it these products are put there in order to smooth the TL response at the coincidence dip, the resonance point of the partition, right?...so i guess that they all work pretty much the same way with slightly different results regarding the final STC of the wall....
This is about as wrong as could be. Nikodemus, I really am no good in writing stuff, bad in spelling, but if you can read over that, I only tell things I know, or make it clear that I assume stuff. I don't think that's what I was telling before. There is no real valid comparison between both whatsoever in this kind of application. One is mainly expensive mass almost dreaming it can control this resonance enough with PSEUDO CLD (poetic license), the other really works based on physical principles related to CLD (constrained layer damping). Just seeing and feeling GG and understanding the function of viscous material (it's the viscous part in the visco-elastic material) and CLD will immediately show you're talking about different things. I don't promote the use of GG unless really needed or cost-effective, but acoustically it's indeed something very good. :mrgreen: Rather than visco-elastic, one could give GG a new class name: a visco-sticky material About isolation and floating floors: I know the extensive topic here about floating floors: http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=8173 The content is very good, but I have some question marks as well. If you look at the 1st Studiotips link, you'll notice that I find these things influenced by people having lots of experience with drywall (typical US in as far as I understood), but less with the practices in countries where solid bricks and concrete are the traditional building materials. With drywall the overall insulation is mostly defined by the MSM, typical for double leaf systems. With solid single leaf brick walls you get a different behavior. You don't have this MSM dip. An overall isolation is strongly defined by the weak points of different isolation systems. The drywall is better than the solid wall in the mid and high frequencies but worse at the MSM. Hence since the drywall's overall isolation is mainly defined by this MSM it has little sense to improve a concrete floor as long as the sound transport (TL) through the drywall MSM point is worse. However, I also do believe that too easily now (influenced by this drywall approach) the sense of floating floors is underestimated. I do have experience with solid brick and concrete structures, since I live in area where that are the traditional building practices. But how much isolation is needed? Often people, when thinking about a studio, they automatically link that to extreme high isolation between rooms. If one has a TL of 40 to 50 dB music sound (not STC) between control room and tracking, in a studio where these rooms work exclusively together, that's enough (but not enough towards coupled neighbors). If you monitor a recording with a level difference of 40 to 50 dB you do a pure monitoring, hardly disturbed by that (about identical) low level sound coming trough. In lots of cases I have the feeling that people overestimate the TL they really need, just because that thing is called studio. And I also believe that lots are pleased with the obtained TL, not because it's that high, but because they don't need more, it works as it should and expected, and therefore assuming their TL is most likely rather to very high. Also the background noise in the room (ventilation, computer fans, other cooling) can give a false picture about the real obtained TL as a result of the masking effect. It becomes an entirely different matter when rooms can or must work independently from one another. If someone is practicing drums, while simultaneously you want to record a woodwind (classical with huge dynamic range and silent passages) or a vocal in another room that becomes an entirely different matter. Sometimes it seems to me that the needed isolation between studio rooms is over-estimated and the isolation needed towards neighbors underestimated (they need more than the isolation needed between control room and tracking, in lots of circumstances as in the case of coupled houses and so on). In such cases (individually to be studied) a floating floor can become easily a must. I also hear John often about the relativity of these floating floors, and the perfect isolation obtained with relative lightweight double leaf walls. I find such things easier to judge with real TL numbers versus frequency. I know how hard it is to obtain high isolation values mainly defined by the lows. I don't do detailed calculations anymore in groups. Nikodemus if you want to know how to calculate that without knowing Excel or comparable, you either need to find a friend doing it for you and use the suitable math, or ask the supplier to calculate this for you (CDM, Sylomer and E.A.R distributors all have designated software to help their clients with that). You of course can always hire someone as well. At the E.A.R. site (can't find it directly now = EDITED: see lower) you find VERY useful related white papers where the formulas are clearly explained. But the basis is all the same. It is the deflection under static load which defines the acoustic quality of that floor. Hence you can obtain that with lots of different stuff. 2.5 mm deflection under static load obtained with rockwool, fiberglass, foam, neoprene or rubber patches, CDM cork agglomerates, Sylomer from Getzner or from E.A.R. (that's comparable stuff) or solutions from Kinetics will all result in a comparable decoupling (read acoustic quality) of the floor. Hence the difference is mainly to be found in mechanical, chemical, etc. quality/properties in function of aging, mounting methods, allowed deflection and so on. There is lots written about this already, also here in the forum. Some links: not checked on validity now: 1] Proctor and Son. UK Sylomer Distributor http://www.proctorgroup.com http://www.proctorgroup.com/index.asp?c ... 0Isolation 2] CDM Elastomers http://www.cdm.be/ 3] Christie and grey - Lots of different anti-vibration products, for floors ceilings, etc, etc http://www.christiegrey.com/ 4] Concrete Forum - believe it or not http://www.concrete-home.com/discussion ... um.php?f=7 5] EAR - The basics of Vibration isolation - design your own floating floor!! Excellent site and papers http://www.earsc.com/HOME/engineering/T ... asp?SID=61 6] Sylomer Specifications - German http://www.telle.de/sylomer_telle/typen3.htm You'll notice that Sylomer in function of density and target load is color coded. The max static workload per type is noted on these pages. You have to aim to this max work load. The material itself can safely withstand higher short-term loads. However these numbers are for full coverage. With small pieces an additional parameter, the shape ratio comes into the picture. They have other non- or less-standard types as well (lighter & denser than on that page). In catalogs you'll find only/mainly the standard stuff. Nightmusic: Standard they will advice the nearest density suitable for your load assuming an about 100 % coverage. That's acoustically not better than the PW studio approach and often more expensive (if you do most of the work yourself). The reason they advice this as such is simply that they work mostly on an industrial scale where all this additional work costs a lot of money and is less practical. Therefore they produce all these densities.
