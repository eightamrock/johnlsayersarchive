Soundman202025 October 2010#6
The outer wall refers to the wall not already constructed (by taking the back of the garage/existing three walls and putting a fourth to make a room),
OK, that makes sense. You are referring to an outer leaf, not an outer wall. So you need to complete the fourth side of your outer leaf by building a stud frame down the middle of your garage. The other three sides will be the existing side wall, the existing rear wall, and the existing front wall, which most likely has a door in it. How are you going to deal with the door? Are you going to take it out and brick up the hole? Or are you going to seal it in place and beef it up from inside to complete your outer leaf?
Sound board itself is relatively inexpensive at $10.65 per 4x8 1/2 sheet, as well as drywall at $7.50 per 4x8 1/2 sheet.
In other words, the soundboard is 42% more expensive. :shock: That's quite some difference! Especially if you are on a tight budget. Forget the soundboard: it will not buy you 42% more isolation, despite hte hype that you might read in places on the internet. Mass is mass: the sound waves do not care how much you paid for that mass. They react in exactly the same way to the same amount of mass, regardless of how much it cost. So go with the cheapest mass you can get, which is plain old common ordinary drywall.
And I was asking if it would be more efficient to use a set of doubled sound board, since all I can come across locally is 1/2 making a total of 1" thick walls, or use one 1/2 sheet of drywall and one 1/2 sheet of soundboard for both the wall I will construct and the ceiling I will construct.
You live in Seatlle, and you cannot find any 5/8" drywall? :shock: :?: That sounds strange! In any event, 1/2" drywall (sound board or not) is too thin to be of much use for isolating drums. It doesn't matter if you put on two or three layers of it: the issue is not the thickness of the entire leaf, but rather the thickness of the individual sheets. The properties of 1/2" drywall make it unsuitable for isolating low frequencies, such as come from drums and guitar amps. Two layers of 5/8" drywall will beat 3 layers of 1/2" drywall any day. I'd suggest that you should look around a bit more: there has to be someone that sells ordinary 5/8" drywall in Seattle!
And by "mock" I mean coming down from the already constructed ceiling to build another ceiling coinciding with the constructed wall. Leaving a dead space in between the already constructed ceiling/floor of the upstairs bedrooms.
OK, so you are talking about a decoupled ceiling. Great! But you seem to be talking about suspending it from the existing floor above, and that is most likely not a good idea, for a couple of reasons. One is that you wont get maximum isolation like that, another is that you might overload the existing floor above you: there will be several thousand pounds of drywall and other stuff up there, and it is likely that your existing floor cannot safely support that. Therefore the usual (and best) way to do that, is to support the new ceiling directly on top of the new inner-leaf framing, such that it does not touch the floor above or the outer leaf walls.

Soundman202026 October 2010#9
Yes the ceiling would connect to the frame I build and the back wall completely unattached to the existing ceiling mostly for dead air which will control sound..?
Good. That makes it fully decoupled, which is excellent. Technically, the air space above your ceiling and inside your walls is not "dead air", but rather is part of the tune MSM system (Mass-Spring-Mass) that defines how well your wall stops sound. You can improve the isolation ability of your wall in two ways: 1) Add more mass (more layers of drywall), and 2) make the air space bigger. You can also improve the isolation by placing mineral wool or fiberglass insulation in the air cavity.
from what I understood the sound board was good but seems its not for this application?
The slight benefit you might get from it is not worth the extra 42% price premium.
I realize without purely professional construction I will never get fully sound proof, just looking for a drastic increase over nothing.
Do it the way we outline here, and it will be pretty darn good! There are only a couple of basic rules that you need to follow carefully: 1) Decouple everything, 2) seal everything airtight, 3) use lots of mass, 4) leave lots of air gap between leaves, 5) use only two leaves in walls and ceiling (never one leaf, never three leaves). That's it!
I will stagger the studs to create dead air as well,
You don't need to do that: you already have your outer leaf in place on some sides, so you only need to complete that around your area with some framing and drywall. Then you build another frame, about an inch or so away from that outer leaf frame, and put drywall on that. This is your inner leaf. You can stagger studs if you want, but you will lose isolation like that: Better to build separate frames, if you have enough space, and you certainly do have the space.
the door is another concern because it will connect the walls defeating the need or use of dead air space.
Use two doors, back-to-back, one on each leaf. The most important thing about doors is to seal them very well, with at least two sets of seals all around each door. - Stuart -
