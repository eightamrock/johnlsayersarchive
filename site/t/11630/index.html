Seriously, how about a few pics of your studio, as is? ( I promise, we'll turn down the flamethrower to "medium rare" for your benefit ... )
Well... OK then... just donning my nomex balaclava and fireproof suit.... :lol: Studio consists of two rooms - Control Room and "Live" Room. The CR dimensions are approx 4m W x 6m L x 2.4m H, flat ceiling and generally rectangular with some angled sections in two corners. The LR dimensions are approx 4.75m W x 6m H with a vaulted ceiling that starts at 2.4m and peaks at 3.1m. There are exposed beams. The two rooms are seperated by double glass sliding doors. Entry to each room is via double solid-core timber doors with seals all around. General construction is timber hardwood framing, 19mm "yellow tongue" flooring particleboard (on walls), double layer of CSR Gyprock Soundchek drywall, CSR Bradford Soundcreen 90mm rockwool in all the cavities and a timber laminate "floating" floor on concrete slab. I'm yet to do any actual STC measurements as I'm still swinging a hammer and paint brush, but audibly the noise reduction outside of the rooms is huge and a few friends (a radio station manager and an SAE graduate among them) have indicated that I've achieved excellent isolation, considering that I play in a rock band and the SPL inside the studio is up around 100dB or so. The closest neighbour is approx 5m from the outside of the shed and it is barely audible at their fenceline. However.... since I discovered this forum, I now know that I could have done better!!! Back when I started, "leaves" grew on trees! In some places, namely between the two rooms, my MSM profile is more like MSMSM. As I have discovered from this forum, it is probably reducing my desired STC as a 3-leaf system. And there are flanking issues there too, no decoupling :( As these are supporting walls and solid as a rock, I am now forced to live with it... so the 90% design rule is so true. I'm also addressing some weaknesses in the sliding door seals and will most probably change the thickness of the glass in one set. The control room has originally been set up with the monitors firing at the short side. Although it is symmetrical and sounds quite OK, I now realise that this is acoustically undesirable and am currently in the process of "turning it around" and hence, modifying the interior walls to achieve symmetry and the required acoustic balances. Originally, the place was a mass of foam acoustic tiles, but now rockwool traps and slat resonators abound! Thanks to you guys :D (I've actually become quite a gun at Sketchup but the files are huge... so have attached some exported jpegs so you can see what I'm talking about)

Hi Rob, Thanks for asking. If they are done well then they are great. I saw the idea "somewhere on the internet" but didn't know the specifics about how to do it correctly. So I basically bought two 2nd-hand sets of doors and almost butted them up back-to-back, except... the frames don't actually touch each other, they are seperated by a neoprene rubber strip all round about 1cm thick. The glass in both sets is approx the same thickness. However... I DON'T recommend that you actually do what I did! :oops: I only discovered this forum long after I'd installed the doors. It's recommended that you differ the thickness of the glass between the 2 sets of doors. Also, I have read that you can splay them slightly. Also, also... :) leave a larger air gap and bridge it with acoustic materials. If you do a search of this site for 'double glass sliding doors' there are heaps of references to threads with valuable info. There are some basic tips here too... http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/audio/pages/fullindex.htm I'd certainly recommend using them though as they are easier to install than a set of doors and a window - and serve both functions in one opening. Hope that helps! Cheers, Glenn.

Soundman20208 February 2009#13
Hey Glenn, 1/3 octave bands are probably way too broadband to tell you much. You could have a dozen deep dips and tall peaks inside a 1/3 octave range. I'd suggest that you you use Ethan's trick of playing back a sequence of specific 1 Hz. spaced tones, hitting every integer from 20 Hz up to 300 Hz with a second or so of each tone, then use pink noise above that, and just record the result to your DAW with a decent flat response mic, or a mic with known response. I think Ethan even has the set of files that you need on his website, for download, and a video of how he uses that to test room response. If not, then you can make them yourself with any decent DAW, albeit painstakingly! Ethan uses that for testing room response, but it will work just fine for testing isolation between two rooms as well. Do the test twice, once with doors open, then again with doors closed (or thrice, if you prefer, with one-door / two-doors), and you'll get a pretty good idea of how well your doors (and walls!) work across the toughest range of the spectrum (low end). You might be surprised at where your problem frequencies are! Now, if you just want a very general, rough ball park figure, then just go with pink noise and an SPL meter: It will give you a basic idea of how well your isolation works, but without any details as to frequencies. Well, that would be my $0.02 (not sure what the exchange rate is to your neck of the woods!) - Stuart -
