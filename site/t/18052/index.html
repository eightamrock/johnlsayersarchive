xSpace25 December 2012#8
"Can anyone advise if there will be a substantial benefit in removing the middle leaf? " Yes. The common term is "three leaf assembly" or like in your case a coupled three leaf, same thing different effect still poor performance. What happens is what you have in a mass/spring/mass (typically known as a double barrier assembly) an air cavity, one air cavity. This air acts like the "spring" (think of a shock absorber) which when sonic pressure is placed on the one side the air interacts with the opposite side. Basically "slowing" the penetration down of sound according to the resonance frequency of the assembly. That just means that the frequency level is raised somewhat rather than reduced. The mechanical aspects of the assembly, e.g. the panels and the framing help to add the rigidity, the insulation helps to damping the panels (reduce the interaction of the sound) from creating even more noise once introduced into the interior of the cavity i.e. the spring element. A typical three leaf has 2(two) air cavities/springs. The typical three leaf is much like your current situation in that you have the two springs that are smaller than the air cavity would be if it were only one space. So the multiple springs not only creates an acoustic hole that would not have otherwise been there but also cuts the ability of the assembly to isolate the frequencies it would have been better at isolating if it were one spring. You could open up the air space(s) in a three leaf and get it to perform better but at what cost? Real estate is one, additional material/labor is the other. So it is common to recommend to remove interior existing barriers or be prepared to open up the air space to a couple of feet or better.

Soundman2020 wrote:
In any event, you do not need to demolish the entire wall: careful removal of the sheet rock from the side of the wall that will face the cavity, is all that is required. Said "carefully-removed-sheetrock" can then be used to beef up the other side of that wall, from within, between the studs. Then you can proceed to build the inner leaf, on its own studs. And if you ever DO need to demolish your entire studio (a major and rather expensive undertaking in any case) due to moving to a new location, then all you need to do is put new sheetrock back on those original studs right where the original sheetrock was, then tape, mud and paint. Presto! Right back the way it was. Not a huge expense, and not even very complicated, compared with demolishing the rest of the studio first. - Stuart -
