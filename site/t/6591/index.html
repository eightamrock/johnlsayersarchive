As a former graveyard shift person myself, and as Steve ("Knightfly") is as well part of the time, we can definitely sympathize with Dr. Daysleeper. I recommend you determine the noise ordinances that are in effect in your area. Find out what the law actually says. (Don't depend on someone to summarize it for you -- the the actual language.) Is this “Box in a Box” concept going to do the trick??
Not only is the answer "yes," but I can't imagine anything else working for you.
Would it be as effective if I only did the double wall thing on the wall facing the neighbors and maybe the rear wall (as in the picture) or should/do I need to do it all the way around (and I am including the ceiling as well)?
You need to do it all the way around. You may be able to get away with less of an air gap in the less critical areas, but you seem to be able to afford a few inches, so therefore, you should.
If I make the double walls (“box in a box”), how far apart should they be? How big an air space between them? Does a space of a couple inches wider make a difference (ex: a 4” space as opposed to a 6” space?)
The wider the air gap, the better the isolation. Understand that the measurement is taken from the back surface of the drywall (or whatever)... So, if you're using 2x4 frames, separating the frames 1 inch gives you 8 inches of air gap (3 1/2" stud, plus 1", plus 3 1/2" stud). Increasing the distance between frames to 3" gives you 10". Increasing to 5" gives you 12". In my project it will be about 12" around most of my room. If you can afford more, do it -- you won't be disappointed. Don't underestimate the power of the air gap -- it makes a huge difference.]
Instead of the Box in a Box concept, can I just use 2X6X8 base plates and top plates, and then stagger 2x4x8’s studs in the wall . . .
Forget staggering. While better than nothing and perhaps a good idea for separating, say, a family room from a master bedroom, the noise levels you'll be generating in your space and the level of quietness that will need to be maintained outside are worlds apart. Sorry, there's no shortcut to success here.
I know there is a difference between Sound Absorption and Sound Isolation, but will adding the 703 home made 4x8 ft absorbers (bass traps) around the room and in the corners and ceilings. Is this going to help with my situation once the Studio is built as to noise going to the outside world? (Obviously Bass and Kick drum are all I am worried about)
Nope. Well, maybe ever-so-slightly. However, usually that slight benefit translates to it sounding so good inside that you tend to turn things up and/or play louder/harder, thereby negating the positive effect (isolationwise). I think it's fair to assume for the sake of construction planning that nothing you put on the walls after it's built will make the room any quieter to the outside world.
As far as the drum riser, do I even need one???
Probably not. In fact, you could actually make things worse with it.
The double doors, in the double walls between the Tracking room and the Control room, do they have to both be solid core?
Yes... If you care about isolation... And it appears you do. Don't assume that compromising on the doors between rooms will only affect the isolation between the rooms. You'd essentially be compromising your walls, and that would have an affect on what leaks through to the outside. Really.

On your selection of outer building materials and insulation, here's the rule; ANY air gap, whether filled with BREATHABLE insulation or just air, will act as an air space; you only want ONE of these, it needs to be WIDE. Adding insulation, unless it has a paper face thats away from one of the masses, or it's closed cell foam, doesn't lessen your effective air gap; it's still considered "air". It will, however, affect things like damping. Also, you don't want insulation up against masonry in a double framed wall - it needs to have REAL air for moisture control. Unfaced insulation between studs
Well, the main thing to remember about kraft paper on insulation as a vapor retarder is that it has to be applied to the correct side -- against the living space wall in cold climiates (north) and against the outside wall in warm climates (south). Nobody here has ever said (or should have ever said) that you should never use kraft paper insulation. However, it has been said (correctly) that you should never use kraft paper insulation with the kraft paper not placed firmly against a leaf. Done incorrectly, you could end up with M-A-m-A-M, with the lower case "m" representing the kraft paper. That effectively divides your "air" into two separate pockets which would be quite detrimental to your isolation goals. All of that basically says, you probably need to have a vapor ratarder -- and it's probably required by code -- so don't skimp on that. Learn more about vapor retarders.

They just did an impressive overhaul of the Green Glue Web site -- go to http://www.GreenGlueCompany.com and learn all about it. Green Glue is not caulking. It is a constrained dampening layer to be sandwiched between two layers of drywall. It is also not actually glue -- don't mistake it as an adhesive -- you still need to use drywall screws -- just as many of them as you would need otherwise. Be aware that Green Glue is expensive. It costs about $15 per tube, not including shipping, sold by the case (12 tubes) and each tube covers a 4' x 4' section of drywall (i.e., two tubes per 4' x 8' sheet). Their marketing materials are credible and their lab tests are legitimate. You will save a lot of time and materials, but you pay for the privilege.
