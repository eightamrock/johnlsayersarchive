sharward27 October 2006#30
You'll want to make sure that your permit won't expire before you have a chance to get your first inspection. Here in Sacramento, permits expire six months after issuance, and each inspection causes the expiration date to be moved six months from then. So, in my case, as long as I get an inspection every six months or less, I'm fine. My city also allows one courtesy six month extension, which I already took advantage of -- my concrete forms inspection took place more than six months after my permit was issued. The ramifications of allowing a permit to expire (again, in my case) can be significant. In order to reinstate the permit, I'd have to pay a fee equal to half the initial permit and plan check fee (about $150), and then I'd be subject to be in compliance with any new codes that went into effect after my permit was issued. In my case, there are some -- mostly related to energy efficiency. Getting into compliance on things that are already partially built could become problematic, depending on what changes were made to the codes. For these reasons, if you decide to wait until Spring to build, you'll probably want to talk with your permit office to strategize on this. They may advise you to wait until the spring to get your plans approved. In fact, you may learn so much between now and then that your plans may change significantly more than you think. On the other hand, they may be privy to upcoming code changes that could have a detrimental impact on what you intend to do. Have fun with that decision. :) Stirring things up... as usual... ;-) --Keith :mrgreen:

Hi AJ - I thought I'd toss out a couple comments.. 1) I did the staggered stud routine and now wish I hadn't. Your decision on double wall is the right way to go. 2) I'd wait until spring to dig. I dug in November and watched the rain fill my excavation every other day. I was out 4 or 5 times a week with the sump pump draining the swamp (pretty depressing)... constant mud... the wife was elated. Just make sure you have your people lined up for concrete and framing in the spring and it won't be so bad. 2a) Your concrete reinforcement should be centered (heightwise) in your slab. Obviously it doesn't have to be exact, but concrete guys are notorious for just letting it sit on top of the sand and then pouring. Don't do that! I don't know how thick your slab is but if it's less than 5" thick I'd certainly consider making thicker. I made mine 6" thick with a monster stiffener all the way around. I'm very happy with it... I put my #4 and #5 rebar at 12" o.c. both ways, I have no cracking. If you use a pumper truck, don't let them discharge all their waste water into your pour. Be prepared to tell them where to dump it. Have a place picked out because they WILL dump it! 3) I can't see your post from here for your final wall makeup... but on the outside you've got stucco the OSB and studs. I would consider a bead of QuietGlue ($10) between the studs and the OSB. (Save the Green Glue for the inside.) Also put caulk or sealant on the top and bottom edge of your blocking between rafters. If not the dampening glue on the studs, perhaps a layer of drywall on the inside face of the OSB, cut to fit between studs. 4) I ran my stucco all the way up between my rafter tails. I'm not done with the inside of my walls yet. Time will tell if it was a good idea or not. 5) For windows, it may be easier to put in the headers now. You can always frame inside them and treat it like a solid wall if you want... but the rough framing would be done for later on. Or you can build a two-pane sound window with dark glass on the outside (that doesn't open) and solid wall on the inside. 6) Regarding your doors, figure out now what kind of threshold and soundproofing you are going to do. (I wish I had.) It will dictate some wall and stud locations. good luck. creatch

sharward27 October 2006#33
Not much time this morning, so a couple of quick thoughts... The slab I poured is 6" thick and I used #4 rebar 12 o.c. in addition to fibermesh. That concrete is perfect. 8) I'm going to officially recommend that you wait until the spring because you can use the following months to make final decisions about your layout. Since you'll most likely be doing double walls with independent ceilings, you should have deep haunches in the slab under your heavy walls. If you pour your slab now, then decide to move your walls, your haunches (if you do any) won't align with your walls. You also haven't talked at all about doing an isolated slab design, which is covered in Rod's book. I'm not saying you need that, but if you pour in a hurry now, you won't have that option later. Finally, I'm afraid I'm going to vote "no" on the vinyl siding. :( I understand the desire to match the house. However, vinyl siding is lightweight and has a lot of air behind it, so it could be argued that it might act as a third leaf. I'm especially concerned that it might rattle, vibrate, and hum in sympathy with the low-end frequencies taking place inside the studio! :shock: If it were clapboard siding, I'd think differently... Perhaps you could do clapboard, which from a slight distance would look the same as (or, more than likely, better than) the vinyl siding on your house. I think there's less risk of clapboards (firmly attached to the framing and each other) resonating than with lightweight vinyl siding panels loosely assembled as prescribed by the manufacturers. Be prepared for additional expense, though, as I'm sure real clapboard siding is more expensive than imitation clapboard vinyl siding. --Keith :mrgreen:

knightfly30 October 2006#37
I'll have to go back and look at all your other postings, just want to address the window thing before it's too late - do NOT buy "over the counter" windows til you understand fully the mass-air-mass principle - virtually all "normal" windows these days are intended for "normal" house installation, and are "energy conservation" oriented. This means that virtually all will have DOUBLE panes of glass of some kind or other, either a 1-piece sealed double pane with inert gas inside, or something else; but ALL will have 2 masses involved, and nowhere NEAR a wide enough air space between these two WIMPY masses to make much of a sound barrier. IF you need SERIOUS sound isolation in windows, you have very little choice but to either spend $5k or more EACH at Overly or a similar manufacturer, or find a local glazier and have custom panes cut or ordered and then place them far enough apart in separate frames to accomplish a barrier that is as good as your wall... knightfly31 October 2006#39
Air space above tracking - this all follows mass-air-mass construction, so if you have mass for your inner ceiling, then insulated air space, then mass for your roof, you're DONE. Things that improve isolation (given these conditions) are more mass in either leaf, wider air gap, insulation density (optimum is around 2.5 PCF). Insulation should be unfaced, with the possible exception of innermost batts if they are touching the inner ceiling layers - if you were in Florida then the ONLY vapor barrier should go to the OUTSIDE - in northern states it goes to the inside. There is only a minimal concern that paper backing would do much as far as acoustics (3-leaf) but you do NOT want a second vapor barrier ANYWHERE or you'll end up with rotted construction. Loft area - depends on what ELSE you'll have for sound barrier. If you'll already have 2 heavy leaves of mass, then the lighter your windows the better (and the further from your sound wall the better) - if that glass will be one of your two leaves of mass between inside and outside, then it WILL need to be heavy. No problem letting your concrete winter over, as long as it's poured by someone who knows what your locale needs as far as air entrainment, etc - Steve
