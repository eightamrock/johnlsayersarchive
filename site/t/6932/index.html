len-morgan29 August 2006#6
peteys wrote:
I don't think I'll be needing that much isolation as my neighbors are kind of far away, but I would like to do some. Is it an all or nothing thing, isolation?
Pretty much. Even the smallest opening can destroy your isolation. Rod talks about this in his book. As to your ceiling question, if it were me, I wouldn't leave it open just for astetics. If you had nice old timber beams there, it might be different. What you might consider doing (pending structural engineer approval) is to first beef up the underside of your roof with a couple of layers of drywall and calk it well. That will give you a nice tight top shell to keep the sound in. Don't discount your neighbor's annoyance too quickly because they might move and you'll get new (less tolerant) ones. Remember that isolation (i.e., the annoyance factor to those that don't share your passion for music), is inversely proportional to both the types of music you play and they like, and the hour of the day (or night) - Morgan's First Law of Acoustics. :-) After you beef up the inside of the roof, you could put bass hangers in the space above the cross beams, 4" inches of mineral wool between them (horizontal), and then cover the whole ceiling with [flame retardant] cloth to make it look pretty. This would probably end up with a pretty tame (dead?) space which is better than one with a lot of reverb since you can always add reverb after the fact. It's next to impossible to remove it once you've recorded it. len

len-morgan30 August 2006#8
peteys wrote:
I'd have to disagree with you about the ceiling, though. I do think the room will sound better left open, at least for tracking purposes; not sure about mixing though.
Well, great minds CAN disagree and still be friends. :-) You kind of hit the nail on the head though with the "... not sure about mixing though..." That was exactly my point. IF you want a comfortable place to play music that sounds great while you are playing, by all means, keep the open ceiling. The natural reverb and reflections that the high ceiling create will make a good performance environment. The problem is when you go to mix what you've recorded. What you are hearing from your monitors will ALSO be "treated" by the acoustics of the room and what you end up with might sound great IN THE ROOM, when you go listen to it in the car or on a boom box, it's not going to sound anywhere close to what you mixed because you "left the room behind." Since you only want a single multifunction room, you might be able to comrimise and make 2' wide by 4' or 6' long absorbtion boxes (just a simple frame with mineral wool or OC703 and covered with cloth that you mount BETWEEN the ceiling joists but hinge on one side on the top of the joist. That way, you could raise or lower them to "reveal" more or less of the upper ceiling area depending on the sound you wanted or what you were doing (i.e., tracking or mixing) at the time. Just my 2 cents worth. Remember, it's much easier for me to suggest treatments like this when I'm spending your money and not mine. :-) len

len-morgan31 August 2006#15
peteys wrote:
I actually thing that my basement would be a better place to mix. It would be easier to isolate, ...
It seems (to me) you might be confusing your requirements. Generally speaking: Isolation is important when you tracking so that you only record the instruments/voices you want (and not the traffic on the street outside) and to keep from annoying neighbors and family. The acoustics of the room you mix in are more important when it comes time to mix. Isolation is less of a problem at this stage because you already have the sound recorded so external noise isn't going to get into the finished product. Also, it is recomended that mixing be done at 85 dB (which "dB" are we talking about here, btw? dBa? dBm? dBspl?) because that is where the human ear's response is the flatest. Isolation isn't as important because you don't have as much signal to isolate in the first place. As a final point, if a big truck happens to drive by while you're mixing, you always have the option of stopping, rewinding, and trying again. When you're tracking a brilliant guitar solo, you don't have that option. len
