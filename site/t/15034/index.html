Soundman202028 October 2010#8
I see, thanks Stuart. Based on what I read, and the DIY walkthroughs on how to build your own bass traps/broad band absorbers, they seemed similar, and since they were called 'absorbers', i thought they might help keep the sound within the room. If they're "trapping" bass, and "abosrbing" sound, I guess its a logical assumption.
Yes, it is logical, and intuitive even, and its common for people to think that way, but it turns out to not be correct. It's one of those "intuitive" things about sound that everyone "knows" to be true, but actually turn out to not be true, because so much about acoustics just isn't intuitive at all! Think of fit this way: A sponge is a really good absorber of water. But you cannot stop water coming out your kitchen tap by putting a sponge over it! Same with bass traps: they are really good absorbers of sound, but you cannot stop sound getting out of your room by lining it with bass traps. But you CAN stop water coming out the tap by putting your thumb over the end and pressing tightly. On the other hand, trying to use your thumb to mop up spilled water probably won't be very effective... :) (OK, that's a very simplified and not very accurate analogy, but it gives you the basic idea: two different problems need two different, diametrically opposed solutions). In reality, materials that are really good for acoustic treatment are also really lousy for acoustic isolation, and vice versa. To isolate a room, you need solid, rigid, massive barriers, such as drywall, glass, thick wood, etc. It has to be dense, solid, and rigid. It is all about mass and flexibility, not about dimensions. So a piece of cardboard can be the exact same size and thickness as piece of glass, but the glass will isolate many, many times better simply because it is far more rigid and far more massive. Isolation works by preventing the sound from getting out of the room. That implies that it keeps the sound in the room. Keeping the sound in the room is not good for the acoustics. If you have the same sound bouncing around inside the room because it cannot get out, that is called "resonance" (or more accurately "reverberation"), and it makes the room sound bad. You don't want too much reverb and resonance in a room. In order to deal with these, you need light, fluffy, soft, thick, flexible absorbent materials, that are good at absorbing sound, but really lousy at stopping it. Hence, things like fiberglass, mineral wool, acoustic foam, and suchlike. Those kinds of things are really good at treating rooms, since they damp the resonance. The bigger they are, the more effective they are down to lower frequencies. So basically there are two steps in creating a studio: First, you isolate it which means keeping the sound in and making it sound bad. Then you treat it by absorbing the sounds that are "making it sound bad". In simple terms, the treatment compensates for the isolation. The best possible studio would be one that has no walls or ceiling at all, such that all of the sound leaves and never comes back: Such a studio would require no treatment at all. But since that is rather impractical ( :shock: ) we do have to put walls around studios to isolate them, and that creates the need for treatment. Yes, there are certain things you can do in designing those walls that will help to reduce their negative effects, but you will always need some kind of treatment.

Soundman202028 October 2010#10
Could you please just clarify what you mean by "two independent seals around the entire perimeter"? I have used weather stripping on a door before and lined the entire thing where the door contacted the jamb/frame (sorry if Im using the wrong term), and it made a big difference as far as air drafts. However, since the door only contacts the jamb/frame on 1 side, Im confused as how you could double the seals.
Once you get Rod's book you'll see how to do that, but the basic idea is that your door is not a single flat surface going into one jamb, but rather TWO flat surfaces mating with TWO jambs. TO do that, you add a sheet of plywood on top of the door surface, on that face that closes into the existing jamb, but you cut it a bit smaller than the jamb. In other words, it sticks out beyond the existing jamb, without touching it. The first seal goes in that gap, between the original door surface and the original jamb. Then you add a second jamb, which is LARGER than the existing jamb, and put the second seal on that one, so that it snugs up tight against the plywood. It's much harder to describe in words than it is to understand form looking at a diagram! The seal has to go all round the door, on all four sides, of course: left, right top and bottom. It's easier to buy a commercial seal for the floor part than to try to build your own. - Stuart -
