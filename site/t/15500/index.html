While your budget isn't much the thing working against you right now is the wooden frame...specifically the floor. Your idea about inverting the insulation to place the retarder against the exterior is ill advised and wins you nothing :) Matter of fact, a double frame in this wooden framed structure is not going to gain you much at all since the floor is the weak link and a direct flanking path. If I were doing this, I guess I would have to take the carpet and anything off the wall that had no mass, and install mass directly to the existing wall, get a solid core wood door and make damn certain I get a good seal on the perimeter of the door. Same with the ceiling, just add mass directly to it. The addition you want to add needs to be changed so that you have more of a rectangle and less of a square, maybe go more for a deeper length but as small as it is, might not make much difference one way or the other, deeper or wider, but rectangular is better than a square.

shanesix wrote:
Check for floor construction here http://johnlsayers.com/Recmanual/Titles/Acoustics3.htm
You can check it, but it doesn't apply to this shed. A wooden floor built on top of a wooden floor is not going to make your isolation better. A wooden floor on top of a concrete floor might help, but even that is essentially adding less mass to an already well massed and damped piece of concrete, which is what that picture represents. An alternative would be to install 703 rigid type insulation or something comparable to it, on the existing floor and install several layers of dense substrate, MDF, 3/4" OSB, etc. The rigid insulation will completely decouple the new floor which is going to be a better procedure than another framed floor assembly on top of an existing wood floor assembly that will allow vibration a path in either direction. It is too easy to short circuit these things.

Sharwoods build was a carport, concrete slab so things that might help in that build may not help you. For instance, let's bring the wooden structure back into view...it is one big flanking path. Me, I would not open any walls but would install mass (OSB/sheetrock, etc) directly to the existing walls/boundaries AFTER getting all the carpet and other materials that have no mass, get it back down to the original sheetrock is what I am talking about. Now this presupposes several things, one is how bad do you want it? If you are highly motivated then you can cut the existing sheetrock out, remove the wall insulation and re-install the sheetrock you cut out to the exterior wall( back side of the sheathing) Resilient channel on the walls and ceiling (after removal of the interior existing sheetrock would be a better option than installing another framed wall coupled to the floor and most likely back to the ceiling as well...making yet another flanking path (the new wooden wall) that defeats what you are trying to do, which is to isolate that thang:) Motivation, money and skill, these things are part and parcel to any kind of job as you are aware of I am certain. Me, adding mass directly to the walls and floor and ceiling that is what I would do. Just a little for instance, if you added one layer of 1/2" OSB and two layers or 5/8" rock to the existing walls, you just lost, let's see...carry the one, ah, 3 and 1/2" of floor space in an already tight room. So adding a framed wall, to both sides of this already tight room, wow, closer to 12 " +.

Silicone is not adequate for a constrained layer dampening system. As a cheaper alternative, I do not know it that information exists, cheaper being the operative word. While silicone has it's uses...it is not recommended as the shearing element within a CLD. Folks have been making speaker cabinets for decades using 30 pound felt as the interior shearing element in a supposed constrained layer damping system. It is not a true cld but a dry friction damping (Douglas Thorby, "Structural Dynamics and Vibration in Practice", ISBN: 978-0-7506-8002-8) As to using silicone as a glue, in a profession that has little to no interest in the acoustic value of an assembly, you are kind of correct in that it is common place. The difference is that a 100% silicone is more expensive so way more often then not, a latex type will do the job.
