Design 2: This is definitely an improvement, at least with what you've got for a framework. There's still some obvious concerns, but some of them are unavoidable with this space. The sight lines would be absolutely horrible, but I'm assuming you're just trying to be able to lay down tracks, not run a business. Last suggestion, I'm a big fan of side facing control rooms... I dunno why, but I've liked them best when I've been in studios... Granted one of the studios that was front facing that I worked in, was one of those who totally misunderstand some of the principles of good acoustic design, and just basically carpeted plywood walls as their idea of absorption. Soundman202026 November 2013#11
Hi Yavor, and welcome! Just a few comments on your initial post:
With close to 600mm. of insulation between me and my neighbor downstairs, I am not so worried about him getting irritated
Actually, insulation does not isolate very well. That's a common myth. Insulation is great as part of an isolation system, but by itself it does not do much. Insulation by itself is very good for room treatment, but that's a very different thing. Isolating a room acoustically is one thing, and treating a room acoustically is another thing. To isolate a room using only insulation, you would have to make the insulation about 4.5 meters thick, and it would have to be a 100% perfect absorber at all frequencies from 20 Hz to 20 kHz. There is no such material. So 60 cm of insulation will help a bit with reducing the transmission of high frequencies, but won't do much at all for lows. Your neighbor will still hear all the low frequencies, and most of the mids as well.
On the back wall I will have to place an existing bookshelf, full of books. Should be a pretty good absorber anyway:
It will be much more of a diffuser than an absorber! It will diffuse mids and highs, and scatter some of the low mids, but it won't absorb much.
Here is the two initial designs I came to
#2 is better, but there is a bog problem with both of them: your room is not symmetrical. It is critical that the front half of the room must be perfectly symmetrical (the left half must be a mirror-image of the right half).
he declares that rectangular shapes are OK, and angling of walls is nothing more than "waste of space"
That's for his concept of room design, which I believe he calls NER. For those of us who follow the RFZ design principle, angling the front walls is a critical part of that. There are several different design philosophies, and those are just two of them. So it depends on which philosophy you plan to follow for your room.
as anyway low frequencies are impossible to reflect.
Well, I wouldn't agree with that: A concrete wall 18" thick does a pretty good job of reflecting low frequencies. I'd agree that it is harder to reflect low frequencies than it is to reflect highs, but not impossible.
is that angling of walls is important only in rooms with poor treatment
Once again, that's for his design philosophy, which might not be applicable to other design philosophies.
I suppose you meant something like that:
Yes! That's a better design from the symmetry point of view, and you could even angle your walls slightly, to make better use of space in both rooms: you don't need as much space at the front of the room, where the speakers are, so narrowing that down means you can make the booth wider at that end, which is a good idea. However, there's a major problem with that layout: the bookshelves will have to go. You cannot have one side of the room completely diffusive and the other side completely reflective. Remember, the room must be acoustically symmetrical: both sides exactly the same.
I am still not sure whether to make bass traps on corners of wall and ceiling
Yes, definitely! Your bass traps MUST be in the corners, from floor to ceiling, and you will need to make them very large, since it is a small room.
- Floor (booth): 50mm. ROCKWOOL (150kg/m3) / chipboard / 50x50mm. wood studs w.ISOVER DOMO (13kg/m3) inbetween / 3x1.25 g.board / BITUVER FONAS deadsheet / 2xchipboard / 20mm.oak floor.;
What is the existing floor made of? You probably do not need to do that in your booth.
- Ceiling (room): perforated g.board (if basstraps are made) / or whole ceiling with ECOPHON Focus A;
Make your ceiling fully absorptive. I would suggest 10cm of OC-703 over the entire surface, except where you have the bass traps in the corners, of course.
Massive layers bellow the room do make the difference (in my opinion).
According to mass law, you will get a 6 dB increase in isolation if you DOUBLE the mass of your existing floor. 6 dB is hardly noticeable. If you double the mass again (total of four times the existing mass) then you get an increase of 12 dB. Still not a lot, but that's a huge amount of mass you have down there! But that is not what you are proposing: you said "- Floor (booth): 50mm. ROCKWOOL (150kg/m3) / chipboard / 50x50mm. wood studs w.ISOVER DOMO (13kg/m3) inbetween / 3x1.25 g.board / BITUVER FONAS deadsheet / 2xchipboard / 20mm.oak floor.;" That is NOT a massive floor: it is a RESONANT floor, since it is mass OVER AN AIR GAP. What you have there basically is a drum: a membrane stretched over an air cavity. It will act exactly like a drum, resonating at certain frequencies. Even worse, in your case you are proposing to build TWO different air cavities with two different membranes, so you will actually have a 3-leaf resonant system, which has two sets of resonances! You might want to read this: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8173
So having >600mm mineral wool and >200mm concrete between me and my cousin on the 2nd floor.... helps a bit
The 200mm of concrete DOES help.... but adding thin, lightweight membranes (chipboard, gypsum board, etc), above it over an air cavity does NOT help much: it can improve high frequency isolation, but it can also make low frequency isolation worse than it was with just the 200mm of concrete, because you are creating a resonant system.
I am almost sure the floating floor and the window should be OK,
And I'm almost sure you didn't do the math to calculate it correctly! What is the resonant frequency? What is the resilience of your floor? How much deflection do you need on your resilient springs to obtain that resonance? How much mass do you need on the springs to obtain that deflection? How much mass do you have? Rod and others provide the principles for floating a floor in their books, but you still have to do the math and work through the equations for YOUR specific floor, in order to ensure that you get it right. Once again, I'd suggest that you read this thread: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8173
I see no real chance for a "room inside a room" construction without a floating floor
Why not? If the floor already has enough mass and damping, then all you need to do is to build the walls and ceiling! Many, many people have built "room-in-a-room" studios that way, WITHOUT floating their floors, very successfully, with excellent isolation (and they did the math first, to find out if it would work). Then there are quite a few people here on the forum who tried to float their floors like you are proposing, and later on discovered that it was not working, or that it had made things even worse: they then had to decide if they wanted to rip out the floor again, or just live with the problems...
Actually, at the end I will have two FF. You might notice that the room itself has one
Yup, I did notice that, which is why I mentioned that you have an even WORSE problem, since you have a three-leaf system, and a three-leaf system will ALWAYS give you WORSE isolation in low frequencies, as compared to a two-leaf system, or even a one leaf system, of equivalent mass and overall thickness. So by making that 3-leaf "floated", you would have two different sets of resonant frequencies (technically: f- and f+), and they would BOTH be much higher than the equivalent resonant frequency for a two-leaf floor ("f0"), so the floor would isolate worse. Acoustics is a science, and there are equations for calculating these things. It's not about what you "believe" might work: it is about what you calculated. Science works despite what you think about it! :) Curiously enough, you even show an image that very clearly demonstrates the exact issue I am talking about: You show a four-leaf wall on the left with STC-40, then a 3-leaf wall in the middle with STC-50, then a 2-leaf wall on the right with STC-57. Your own image proves, conclusively, that a 2-leaf system provides BETTER isolation than a 3-leaf or a 4-leaf, yet you designed your floor as a 4-leaf???? :shock: :!: :) - Stuart -
