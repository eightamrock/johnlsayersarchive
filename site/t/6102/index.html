SonicClang26 April 2006#2
The foundation of the house shouldn't rest on the concrete floor. Even 80 years ago they didn't do something that stupid. The only thing you have to worry about is the floor drain... I'm assumming you have one, and any other pipes that may be under the concrete. Check with your local codes to make sure you can lower the floor. It doesn't sound like a crazy idea to me. About the space between the walls you're going to build and the foundation block walls, yeah, fill that space with insulation. Don't leave it open. For the ceiling, if you're not worried about soundproofing, just a layer of drywall. That's the minimum amount of overhead you'd be able to take away. But, if you're like the rest of us that visit this forum, you ARE interested in soundproofing so your wife can watch TV without having to turn it up full blast. In that case, the best option would be to put 6" insulation between the floor joists. Then put up resilient channel (Home Depot sells that by the drywall) and screw two layers of drywall to that. Make sure the drywall that's mounted on the resilient channel doesn't touch anything else but the resilient channel. Caulk around the perimeter at the walls. Stagger the seams of the drywall. the easiest way to do that is to put the first layer "the long way" in the room, and the next layer put "the wide way"... if you get what I mean. Also, make sure you use the correct length screws for the job. If you're screwing up 1/2" drywall, only use 3/4" screws for the first layer. The second layer gets 1 1/4" screws. You'll probably need to pre drill the holes for the screws. Use the smallest bit possible for that, like 7/32" or something real small like that. Buy enough to last a while because you'll break a couple. The resilient channel pretty much takes up 1/2", and the two layers of drywall will take up 1", so you'll lose 1 1/2" total, which isn't too bad, and you'll get really good isolation between the downstairs and upstairs. PS - nice name. :)

len-morgan27 April 2006#12
As to RC and it's "bad points": The primary reason it "fails" is because people use screws that are too long and they go through the drywall, THROUGH the RC and then touch the joists that the RC is attached to. That directly connects your inner drywall to the structure and completely wipes out any gain you get with RC. If you use the right length screws, it should work fine. Quietrock, if I've got my facts straight, is just a sandwich of thin drywall with a sheet of tin/galvanized steel as the "meat" in the sandwich. It "works" by increasing the mass (because steel is more dense than gypsum). You can increase the isolation of a door if you sandwich a thin layer of sheet lead between layers in the door. As to lowering the floor, you really should call in a structural engineer to answer this IN YOUR SPECIFIC CASE. I think the poored floor in your basement acts a little like a "spacer" to hold the bottoms of the walls apart from each other. There is also a little wider footing below the surface along the walls to keep them from settling. You'd want to make sure that you didn't cut off these footings to maintain the structural integrity of the foundation. You MIGHT be able to dig a little extra and put some floor beams between the walls to do the same thing, cover those with dirt, and then put in your new floor. I've never seen that done but is sounds like it might work to me. Again, get a structural engineer to look at this for you. It's probably the best money you'll spend on this project! Adding another 1 1/2 feet of head room (or even more) would be great for you but not at the expense of your house collapsing around you a few years down the road. len

sharward29 April 2006#16
A few random thoughts. Beware of QuietRock claims. Notice that their "one equals eight" claim is based on an STC rating. That would be great if you were planning to hold Toastmasters meetings down there, but you're going to be making a studio for music. (You didn't elaborate on the amount and types of noise you'll be making, which are important factors. Assuming it's loud...) STC numbers are meaningless in this context. Can you dig down to gain headroom? The answer (assuming your city will allow you) is yes. It's been done. The example that comes to mind is Paul Woodlock's project. He did it to accommodate a massive floating slab. You can see his very extensive studio build diary. Don't miss his excavation and (lower) slab pour photos. I have done something kind of in the same category -- I busted out about 200 square feet of garage floor, excavated several inches of clay, then poured a new monolithic structural-grade slab in its place. My reason was not to increase head room (since I have ten feet ceilings), but rather, to have a stronger slab and a quality earth base to support the structure, to level the slab, and to isolate it from the rest of the house. It's been an enormous amount of work, though still far less work than Paul did. That being said... I don't want to rain on your parade, but it's obvious that you're embarking on a gigantic project, and I think you would be wise to reconsider your plan. You deserve credit for doing your homework to get a basic sense of what you need to do to make your environment work for you. However, as I've said to others before you now and then, sometimes the obstacles are just too great. Sometimes we live in houses/buildings/neighborhoods/towns/areas that are just not conducive to fulfilling our dreams. This doesn't mean we need to give up our dreams... But it sometimes does require us to reconsider how we're going to make those dreams come true. The most ideal conditions you could hope for to build a studio, budget permitting, would be to build a standalone building from scratch. This gives you the benefit of being able to engineer the thing right from the beginning, foregoing all the headaches of having to retrofit something that is far from ideal for the purpose. Do you have room on your lot to do this, and if so, would your city allow an auxiliary structure to be built on it? If not, maybe a little househunting is in order. Yes, I'm actually proposing moving if necessary. I'm not saying what you want to do is impossible, but I think you owe it to yourself to do all the research necessary to determine the wisdom of your idea. There's always an effort/dollar mark that defines when you'd be better off going to a different environment altogether vs. adapting your current one. Figure out what that is, and then you'll know whether or not it's worth the price you'll pay. One last thing... Don't assume that you'll be able to add the appropriate amount of mass to your existing ceiling. You need to do the calculations to ensure you won't overload and cause a deadly catastrophy. I don't mean to rain on your parade, but look before you leap. :) --Keith :mrgreen:
