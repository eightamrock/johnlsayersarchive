Acoustics? Mmm, probably...not. :wink: I am in the stage of acoustically treating the space I am in, minus splayed walls and concrete construction. If you care to check it out: http://johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4091 To try and state this in an inadequate and overly simplified way: in addition to the correct room geometry and room dimensions, it is also desirable to obtain a similar reverberation time across the frequency spectrum. Some of the treatments used to accomplish this are low, mid and high frequency absorbers, panel absorbers, and resonators to name a few. From the SAE site:
By using different construction techniques it is possible to treat the walls so they absorb sound at various frequencies. The following pages deal with each of the frequency bands-high, mid and low. By using these construction techniques you can dramatically change the acoustics of a room. High frequencies are the easiest to absorb and it gets harder as the frequency lowers. Most home studio enthusiasts only seem to treat the high frequencies in the room yet it is the mid and low frequencies that cause all the room problems. If you study the treatment for the low and mid frequencies you will notice that the high frequencies are not affected by this construction and it is possible to have a room where all frequencies have a similar reverberation time which is the idea of the whole exercise.
Here's a good place to start: http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html Click on the left hand tab that says "absorbers" and then check out the tabs at the top of the page. Here's a few diagrams for DIY wall units: http://johnlsayers.com/HR/index1.htm And finally, here's some important info from the forum guidelines: "Edit your profile to include your location. This is very important, because this is a worldwide resource, and as such, material costs and availability vary widely. For example, masonry is cheaper than gypsum in some parts of the globe, whereas it's the exact opposite in other regions." Thanks! Hope this helps to give you a good place to start. :D Aloha 8)

My floors are red oak slats over plywood sub flooring over post and pier. In addition to the side and rear absorbers, I have an absorbent cloud treatment planned for over the mixing area. I think that some of the pros of having a hard reflective floor/absorbent ceiling are: - first and foremost, having worked in rooms that were, shall we say, "less live," I prefer the sound of a room that retains some of the "liveness" and "airiness" qualities, but it is just my personal preference, yours may differ and be perfectly fine for your needs. - it is easier to lay down (and remove) area rugs to attenuate highs if necessary than it is to rip up an installed carpet or the messyness of laying down plywood/flooring in order to liven up a dead room. - the mix chair rolls around from mix position to equipment rack to PC station alot easier! :D - it is alot easier to vacuum up after my dog shedding her fur all over the place I have to say that I love the look and feel of a nice, plush carpet, but prefer having a livelier sound when it comes to recording/mixdown. Can you get Thermafiber Sound Attenuation Fire Blankets (SAFB) - Nominal Density (2.5 pcf)? They're equivalent to Roxul Acoustical Fire Batt (AFB) - ≥2" (2.5 pcf). Even if you can't, print out these spec sheets and bring them to your insulation supplier so that they can do their best to come up with a good match. --Keith :mrgreen:
