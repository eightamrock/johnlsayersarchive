Luftweg9 July 2006#891
Keith: Wow, that's almost too pretty (in a utilitarian way) to cover up... my guess is that you are sort of a perfectionist... Of course, this can most usually be a good thing with studio construction (as Rod's book stresses in several places).... (I guess the only time it's bad is when it slows down production too much, while not showing a benefit for that slow down... I'VE got the slow down part down to a tee, myself :lol: , now just gotta work on the benefit part... ) As for the corner seems, I don't know if it would be much of a worry... From what I'm led to believe, there is mostly really no such thing as a perfect vapor barrier -- some amount of vapor/moisture always gets through (I wonder if this is the reason sometimes for some peoples' support of vapor retarders over barriers?) So, if some moisture will make it's way above the barrier, it will be in a more controlled way than simply if there were no barrier at all (it's not as if any pinhole will let through EVERYTHING that's behind it; there is no head of liquid water pressure that the barrier is attempting to hold back)... Actually, in a sense, I think it's good that the moisture could be allowed to slowly dissipate, no?... (I'd be more concerned with soil gases myself... ) In times where the vapor pressure below the shield is low, moisture coming through the gaps would be nil... In times where the vapor pressure below the shield is high, since the gaps are very tiny and can't let as much through as the rate of buildup, much of this vapor buildup should recondense BELOW the shield (or actually prevent further water from vaporizing at that given temperature; from physics, the maximum vapor pressure at any specific temperature forces the liquid to remain a liquid -- or rather, an equilibrium exists between the amount of liquid vaporizing and the amount of liquid condensing). So, I think it all comes down to how much vapor comes through the gaps at the highest vapor pressure that the system will see -- this will depend on the soil temps and moisture, and the temps and moisture above the vapor shield, AND to how much vapor is allowed to escape (or is removed from) the between leaf space... As long as at least the same amount of vapor is allowed to escape the dead air space, as the maximum amount that is put into it -- before recondensing on a cold surface -- the system should work well... Is this not correct thinking? Keep up the good work, K

I answered a question in cyeazel's own thread, so I thought I'd post both the question and the answer here as well.
cyeazel wrote:
. . . I was wondering why you plan on removing your new inner leaf soleplates . . .
I plan to build my inner walls flat and then tip them into place -- so the sole plates that are bolted down are only there temporarily, to apply pressure on the silicone caulk and to cut down on my tripping over the J-bolts. ;-) It also helps in keeping the green stuff protected, not kicked and torn. The outer leaf sole plates will most likely stay in position, as I'll be framing those walls in place. Tipping them into position won't be practical, since the wall frames will be slightly taller than the existing ceiling (the top plates must be affixed to the bottom chord of the trusses -- I'll be removing parts of the ceiling to expose the trusses for this). --Keith :mrgreen:
