Hi Ro, Always good to have skilled people looking over my shoulder. :) Actually, you might be misinterpreting some of what you're seeing, and I also suspect we do things differently here. First of all, here in the US, we run 120 volts for most circuits. The wiring you're seeing is extremely common in residential construction here in North America. It's known as "NM-B" (non-metallic, variant B) and is often sold under its brand name, Romex. The yellow (12 guage) and white (14 guage) jackets you're seeing is a plastic sheath, inside of which are the insulated wires: black for "hot" (or +), white for neutral (or -), and a bare copper wire for ground. Commercial construction calls for metal conduit here, but for houses, NM-B is just fine. Notice all of the white jacketed wire coming out of the box, up the wall, and into the attic. That's the rest of the wiring for my house. Here's a little video clip from the wire manufacturer's Web site that shows Romex wire being "bare" inside the wall framing. (Calling it "bare" isn't really fair, because it does have a flame resistant PVC jacket that protects it.) The blue tube is a "raceway" that already contains a 6 guage 240 volt cable for a back yard spa that the previous owner of the house had put in. The spa is gone, so I will be using that wiring for my mini split air conditioning sytem. In nearly all cases, the wiring for each outlet and switch goes into a box mounted to the sides of the studs. Rather than doing that, I'll be mounting my boxes on the surface of the finished walls. That's the reason for my not having any boxes on the studs of the room. I hope that clears things up a bit. :) --Keith :mrgreen:

I ran into trouble making a strip of drywall narrow enough to fit in the area along the laundry room, which has a smaller gap than the rest of the perimeter (about 8" instead of 12+"). So for this 8 foot section of wall, I will triple-up on the 3" batts to result in a complete fill of Thermafiber SAFB insulation. That is an acceptable fire break according to California Building Code. I did an interesting test yesterday -- I'm surprised I hadn't thought of doing this before. I placed my ear firmly against one of the studs and I knocked on the nearby framing members. Hitting a stud of the same framing assembly, even if it is a different wall of the assembly, was very noticable, and sometimes even almost deafeningly loud! But knocking on an opposing frame's stud resulted in very slight or even zero noise on the "listener" stud! 8) Separate frames are the way to go! :twisted: After finishing the fire blocks, I was able to remove the temporary "bridge" pieces connecting inner leaf to outer leaf frames. My inner leaf no longer squeaks when leaning on the studs -- something that I figured would go away once the sheathing goes up, but the fire blocks are holding the inner leaf really steady so it doesn't sway at all anymore. :-) As I type this I am awaiting my framing and rough electrical inspection! I'm less confident about this one -- I have funny feelings about missing some things on the electrical piece. We'll see what happens! My last inspection was just five days short of six months ago, so I didn't have a choice -- I need an inspection this week in order to keep the permit from lapsing! :shock: Wish me luck on today's inspection! 8) --Keith :mrgreen:

Hey Sharward, Just thought I'd share something that a supplier of mechanical ventilation systems told me. He said that even though I'd be using an efficient heat recovery system, I would lose way too much energy when using such a system with such a small room. He said these systems are designed for an entire home, not for a room that is only 1350 cubic feet. He recommended that I not use this and recommended a fan system (which was honest of him since the fans cost significantly less). Of course the issue with that is how will I get fresh air, and then how can I avoid the energy loss. (my goal is to avoid having to run heating and air-conditioning to my basement room since it's not too bad down there at the moment). I know that you were planning on using one... don't remember if you were going to have heating and air conditioning as well though. So just wanted to share that with you in case you wanted to consider how cold or hot it might get in your room and maybe ways you to avoid that. Ideally, I guess it's best to have fresh air being supplied a little above the minimum required. The company that gave me this info was Airiva. I've also sent an email to Fantech to see what they say about it. I'll let you know.. Tom

sharward11 February 2007#1210
I was certainly busy yesterday... Not busy doing manual labor, which always seems to lend itself to a feeling of more accomplishment than what I was doing. I was busy yesterday thinking... Thinking about my mini-split air conditioner plan. Thinking about my ventilation plan. Researching the latest models of mini-splits on the market today. Dreaming about ducting. Yeah, I know it's not as sexy as framing or electrical, but very important nevertheless! I've always advocated having a solid plan before beginning to construct... And I pretty much have one, which was instrumental in my being granted my building permit back in August of 2005. However, before beginning a phase of work, I always stop and think about the approach, anticipate obstacles, and sometimes tweak the plan to fit the new discoveries. Those who have followed this project since the beginning know about my plan to use a mini-split air conditioner for my cooling and a heat recovery ventilator (HRV) for my fresh air requirements. That basic plan is still intact, but I'm making some refinements that will save me money and make the installation a lot easier than I had initially planned. Certainly, TomM's comments above lead me to reconsider my intent to use an HRV. I'll probably stick with the HRV idea, but I will talk to someone at Fantech before I make my purchase. I posted back in August of 2005 that code requires me to have 15 cfm of fresh air per person. I also read today that 20 cfm per person is considered "standard" for a conference room. I figure I need about 100 cfm net maximum performance out of my ventilation system, assuming the room is loaded with 5 people who are much more lively than people sitting still in a boring business meeting. The Fantech VHR1404 (or equivalent) with 6" ducts still seems like a good fit. (The smaller VHR704 model with 4" ducts just doesn't have the capacity.) The fan has three speeds, so I expect the unit will be running on the lowest speed most of the time... However, for the times that I do have multiple guests or just need to flush the room out of its stale air, the higher fan speeds will do that quite nicely. I was also stressing a bit about penetrating the ducts to the outside. I really didn't want to have to cut through my stucco and risk water intrusion at the ports. Roof-mounted aren't really a viable option to me as DIY, given my tile roof. Then, while I was perusing Fantech's site, I saw this little beauty: [url=http://www.fantech.net/accessories1.htm]
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.fantech.net/halogen_onblack.jpg

But look -- it's a combination 50 watt halogen light fixture and vent grille! They're even UL rated for use in wet locations! 8) I figure that I can use two of these over my front porch, where I already have two 6" round recessed light fixtures, which I can simply remove and then be left with two perfectly sized round holes! :twisted: They come equipped with dampers that flip open when air flow is sucked away from the grille. I'll obviously have to remove the damper on the one for exhaust, and perhaps use an in-line damper a little bit downline. Unless anyone raises an objection, I'm really pleased about this approach, as I won't have to cut through stucco or have any more ugly ports on the house. :D Now, back to ducting... Master Flow 6 In. Metalized R6 Insulated Flexible Duct (F6IFD Series) ...plus a combination of 6" flexible and rigid uninsulated duct for the exhaust air run. I know, I know... Rod's gone on record recommending against flex ducting... :roll: ...I figure I can get away with it in this case, since it's just for fresh air (not A/C) and exhaust, and I can afford some air flow drop due to the static pressure associated with this type of ducting. Back to the mini-split... I've discovered that there are now much more energy efficient mini-split systems on the market today than there were just a year and a half ago when I first found the Mitsubishi Mr. Slim units. They've gone from 12.3 SEER to 16 and even 17 SEER! I'm looking seriously at the Sanyo 09KHS71 system, spending the extra couple hundred dollars to get the heat pump version. I had originally intended to hire a pro to do all of the mini split work, but I'm now planning to purchase the system online and run the lines myself. I will have to hire a pro to do the final hook-up and charging. This will give me the opportunity to be as meticulous as I need to be about seals and placement of the lines. I won't bore you with all the details, but I've got a brilliant plan to allow for maximum air-tightness while also preserving the integrity of the drywall at the penetration points. :twisted: Today I'm going to head out to the building supply to see if I can buy some of what I need, and I'll probably be spending thousands of dollars via the Internet this week! :shock: --Keith :mrgreen:
