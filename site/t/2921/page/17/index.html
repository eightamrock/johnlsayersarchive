knightfly26 September 2005#406
My SW can only do two leaves with 2 material constants per leaf, and only one layer of the second material per leaf; so I calc'd your existing wall using 1/2" OSB one side plus 4 layers 5/8 gypsum, and the office side using 1/2" gypsum, on 2x4's 24" OC - results were m-a-m of 101 hZ - this would be your weak spot. Next, I changed the single gyp side to 4 layers of 5/8 and changed from single stud to double, from 88mm air gap to 300 (12") and recalculated - 27 hZ m-a-m, about 54 dB TL @ 50 hZ, and the dip at 100 hZ is gone. From that, I am "SWAG"ing that you'd be fine with adding 4 layers of gyp to the OSB, then doing your inner wall frame with 4 more - estimated STC of 82 ignoring the third (office) leaf. If anything, you might hear a bit more 100 hZ in the office than without the office side gypsum in place... Steve

This weekend I focused my efforts on the west wall in preparation for adding lots of beef! 8) The west wall is adjacent to the laundry room. It's eight feet long and, of course, ten feet tall. There was a slight complication in that there was an outlet on that wall. Actually, the original location was inside the garage. Of course it became necessary to move that outlet to an area where it can be accessed -- it's the GFCI outlet that supplies and protects four other outdoor outlets. (If you're not familiar with GFCI, you can learn about it here.) Many months ago, long before I finalized my plans, I relocated the outlet to the laundry room side of the wall. However, I realized that's not ideal -- I would rather have a totally flat wall and not have to worry about sealing around an electrical box. Plus, it occurred to me that it would be great to have a dedicated outlet for the heat recovery ventilator (hereinafter referred to has "HRV") that will be installed inside the laundry room. So I moved the outlet yet again to the same wall as the door that leads to/from the garage. It's pictured below -- you can see the original location, then the old location (after I moved it the first time), then the new location where it sits today. The red lines indicate the original wiring path, and the blue lines indicate the new wiring path. You'll also see the attic access inside the laundry room. I was fortunate that I was able to do this outlet relocation without having to move my entire body into the attic -- I was able to stand on the top of my 8 foot ladder, supporting myself on the joists around the opening. Note to self: Drilling through a double top-plate with a half-inch blade bit using an underpowered cordless drill while in an awkward position atop an eight foot ladder is not fun! :? I'm pleased with the outcome though. I didn't have to extend the wiring at all -- I had more than enough slack just from the existing wiring. I may improve upon things by extending the circuit downward, moving the GFCI outlet down about three feet, and putting a regular outlet in its place upstream from the current. First, it would be nice to be able to actually reach the GFCI because our pond pumps and lights out back sometimes trip it. It would also be good not to have the ventilator cut of in such an event. You'll also see that the garage side is now covered in OSB strips cleated in with the 1x2s. The only thing I did differently this time was to pre-drill the holes in the cleats. I split so many cleats on the other side. I think I read an account from someone here about the benefit of predrilling holes in the cleats, but I rushed it last time. This time I took the time to predrill, and the result was no split wood! 8) I also went to both Lowe's and Home Depot looking for 4' x 10' drywall. No luck. :evil: They only have 4' x 8' or 4' x 12'. The geometry works better with 4x10s -- less waste. However, I figured out some cutting patterns that would minimize waste and seams, so I'll probably settle for 4x8s later this week. Also pictured below is my revised wall anatomy. On the east and west walls, I'm going to use three strips of 1/2" between the cleats, then I'll top everything off with a layer of 5/8" strips with screws directly into the cleats and green glue in the field. I have more confidence that the last layer of drywall will hold better with the screws directly and deeply into the cleats (and perhaps further, into the studs), versus into the OSB. Hopefully next weekend I'll be done with the east and west wall beef-up! I'm going to hold off on beefing up the south wall (back) where all the contractor graffiti is -- I think I'm better off waiting until my concrete work is complete, because that beef-up will add about 2 1/2" to its thickness and doing the beef-up first might make it difficult to remove the concrete close to that wall. knightfly4 October 2005#423
Got your PM, and good thing; you are heading for trouble with your screw plans. You need to keep in mind that ALL screws need to be into STUDS, not just the OSB, and that With that many layers, it's important to have DEEP penetration, especially for the subsequent layers. I would NOT use any screw that gives less than 3/4" penetration into the STUDS for the weight of wall you're building. Because you're building separate frames and insulating well, you don't need to worry about the minor flanking that can happen with screws when using single frames and no RC. Speaking of studs, what's your spacing for the inner 2x6 frame? If not 24", it SHOULD be - see this for a reminder http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.p ... 280e1d399c You'll need a few rolls of masking tape and a few "sharpie" markers, but you WILL need to completely map your screw schedule to avoid collisions from layer to layer. No doubt your "seismic consciousness" will want a full schedule for each layer, which means you need to divide the screw spacing by the number of layers and build a "map" for each layer. This will be almost "surgical" for edges, since schedule calls for 8" spacing at edges PER LAYER. The 12" spacing in the panel's field won't put your fasteners quite so close together, but it's still easy to goof. Simplest way to get this right is to snap chalk lines across each layer in both axes, so you just drive the correct length screws at each intersection. Another biggie - is your old concrete GONE, and the new floor finished? If not, why are you doing drywall? Murphy's law WILL get you on this - if there is ANY way to destroy your new drywall with ANYTHING HEAVY (like chunks of concrete, jackhammers, pry bars slipping, etc, it WILL HAPPEN. The less NEW construction you have in close proximity to any DEMO work, the better.
