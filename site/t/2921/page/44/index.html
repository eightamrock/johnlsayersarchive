jwl29 September 2006#1077
Hi Sharward, I've been thinking more about how you are going to treat this room acoustically. The Superchunks/RFZ/cloud concept is good. But I think if it were me, I'd do something like this: 1. Once the room is finished, and with superchunks installed on as many corners as you plan to do, but without any additional acoustic treatment installed, I'd experiment with where in your room your drum kit sounds best. Start walking around the room with the floor tom, tuned up, hitting it solidly. It will sound different as you walk through the room. Once you find a sweet spot that you like, drop the floor tom, and set up the rest of the kit around it. 2. Then, I would cover the furthest wall from you with lots of thick absorption. 3. Next, I would build John's angled side wall absorbers and place them along the walls near where the drum kit will be, with the angles facing toward the wall with tons of absorption, so that the reflections will be aimed toward the absorptive end of the room. 4. Lastly, I would cover the wall opposite the absorptive wall with a slot resonator tuned to one of your problematic frequency ranges (should be able to calculate and/or measure this). This would keep a liveness to the room, but the echoes will tend to settle in the rear of the room where they will be absorbed. Just an idea.... I also love the look of slot resonators if they are done well, and obviously your attention to detail would make it look really good. That nice woodwork with an acid stained concrete floor... mmmmmm.

len-morgan2 October 2006#1081
Sharward, As far as marking your stud locations (something I wish I'd paid a little more attention to in my studio), why not put masking tape on the floor (just temporarily). You could also use pencil marks (nice dark ones!) Then when you put down your baseboard, make sure you put a nail in exactly in the center of the stud (yes, I know you nail into the bottom plate but put the nail in so the center of the stud is right above it. When you go back to putty over the nail heads, use a different color of putty on those nails that indicate stud positions. It shouldn't be too hard to locate those nails later when you need to mount something to the walls. Alternatively, I found a little "tool" at some hardware store that looks like a small pencil sharpener. There is a little yellow bar mounted in a groove that swivels back and forth and there is a magnet in the end of it. When you run it along a wall, it is laying to one side. As you approach a nail head, then end of the yellow bar starts to stand up. When you are right over the nail, it's standing straight up. This will only work on the last layer of screws and it would be very helpful if you had written down exactly how high the screws are from the floor. You can waste a lot of time "wandering" around the wall trying to find a nail in two axises (sp?). If you know the nail is 6" off the floor, you only have one axis to worry about. I should note that this little tool is totally worthless for finding nails in the ceiling since it is always hainging straight down. len

rod gervais9 October 2006#1093
cadesignr wrote:
After 40 yrs of building things, I'll NEVER believe nails are stronger.
fitZ, Then you would be both wrong and right. The word "stronger" is subjective - and that's what you seem to be missing. Your hammer test is not reflective of the types of forces that take place in seismic or wind events. Perhaps you can apply hundreds of pound of force per square inch in a hammer strike - but a seismic event creates insantaneous forces in the thousands of pounds per square inch - and with heavy wind loads the forces are also in the thousands of psi on a building envelope. Any wood screw that does not require you to first drill a pilot hole (i.e. deck screws, drywall screws, etc.) are made of case hardened steel. The steel is (by its very nature) extremely brittle – and does not respond well at all to shear forces. This is one of the reasons that (quite often) if driven into a very dense area of a piece of wood – a case hardened screw will shear off just through the act of being drawn into the wood. So although (from one perspective) you might be right about a screw being stronger than a nail – with that perspective being ONLY that the screw will handle a much greater load for withdrawal than a nail – you are very VERY wrong in making a general statement of a screw being stronger (in and of itself). Interestingly you also help – in your last comment – to prove the point of using nails in the fastening of structural members. You said:
Ps. After inspecting the attic in my current home, I saw EVERY rafter, joist etc, with loose joints caused by warping pulling the connection loose from nails being EASILY pulled by simple twist. I had to SCREW them to pull em up tight. And that ain't the half of it....nails my ass.
And this proves the point exactly……….. Even though the rafters and joist did some twisting as they aged over time – the nails did the job they were designed to do – which is that they held the building together regardless of the fact that the wood was not in a state of constant contact – the shear value of the nail was the only thing stopping a collapse – and it sounds like they did their job well. It is understood in engineering that structures move constantly – and this is especially true with wood structures. Not only does a wood structure move with wind and seismic loads……… The wood swells up with every rise in humidity and shrinks when those humidity levels drop. I’ve seen studs shrink as much a 1/4” in length between the start of a framing project to the end – when the frame is completely closed in. Screws are not going to stop this from happening – nor are they going to work with it when it does (as you noticed with your nails in your comment above). Screws won’t stop wood from swelling and contracting – nor will they stop if from twisting during the process (if that is in its nature) – but the head of the screw will tear through the wood when that does happen – which not only weakens the screw – but weakens the wood as well……… There have been years and years of engineering research done on the best ways to go about fastening structural wood members together – and you can’t change that just because you believe “A” to be better than “B”. Sincerely, Rod
