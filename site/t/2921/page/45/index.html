A note to all planning to use Simpson Strong Tie products, such as those I showed on the previous page: Make sure you review the specs to determine the correct size nails! It's best to look up the product on their Web site first before you buy, so that you know you are using the correct piece for the job, and so you can determine the correct nails to use. Mark Blasco12 October 2006#1106
Keith, I just wanted to say something about recording in that space - I was recording in a 9X12 room for 5 years, and had a drum kit, keyboard, guitar amp, rack of guitars, and my mixing desk setup 24/7. It was tight, but was workable. I couldn't fit a band in there (which I know you are planning to do), but I do think that your space will be big enough to do some recording in the future if you decide to go this route. The key to this is to take advantage of as much of the upper space in your room as possible for acoustic treatment, so that the lower part is as empty as possible. Make sure to attach whatever acostic treatment you do get in the corners between the walls and ceiling - this will give you the best performance in the lower frequencies, but will not reduce your floor space. If most of your acoustic panels start at about the 4-5ft high mark, that will not significantly change the usable space in the room. Even positioning them so that they start 1 foot above the floor gives you enough space for the feet of stands to slide underneath. Just something to consider when you are putting up panels - they don't all have to be on the walls. The ceiling is a great place to put a little extra treatment.

len-morgan15 October 2006#1113
Having done a bunch of these electrical boxes in my studio, I can tell you that doing recessed boxes is tricky (depending on how much drywall you put up. We thought we had it right (and I was working with a Master Electrician) but they ended up being TOO recessed in the end (2 layers of 5/8" DW). You can get extender rings that will make the boxes appear deeper and I think they make deeper than normal boxes. If I were to do it again, I'd get a couple of left-over hunks of 2x4, attach the same number of drywall scraps to it, then mount a box to one of the 2x4s so you know exactly how deep to set them on the 2x4. I know you could probably measure and calculate but I did that twice and still ended up with boxes too deep. I'm sure there is some sort of building code too that tells you how far back from the front of the stud the nails/screws that hold the box have to be. If they're too close to the edge, you'll split the wood. Just my thoughts... len

Keith, It's looking like my inspector is going to let me just fill the entire cavity between my two sets of wall framing with regular fluffy insulation (R21 in each wall, so that they touch), which should satisfy the fire blocking requirement. You might want to check with your permit folks to see if this would work for you, although it sounds like you've already started on the gypboard/thermafiber solution. Another thought is that you may be required to supply a fire block every 10 linear feet of wall - that is (if I'm understanding this correctly, which I may not be) you need to supply a vertically oriented fire block (as opposed to horizontally oriented, which you're already taking care of), from the top to the bottom of the wall, every 10 feet. In a regular wall, the studs and bays full of insulation satisfy this; with double-wall construction, the gap between the walls necessitates this requirement. To satisfy it, you may be able to completely fill one back-to-back set of bays with insulation (so that there is no gap). Again, if I'm not understanding this correctly, someone please let me know. ---Devin

        708.2 Fire blocks.
          708.2.1 Where required. ...snip...

          708.2.2 Fire block construction. Except as provided in Item 4 above, fireblocking shall consist of 2 inches (51 mm) nominal lumber or two thicknesses of 1-inch (25m mm) nominal lumber with broken lap joints or one thickness of 23/32-inch (18.3 mm) wood structural panel with joints backed by 23/32-inch (18.3 mm) wood structural panel or one thickness of 3/4-inch (19.1 mm) Type 2-M particleboard with joints backed by 3/4-inch (19.1 mm) Type 2-M particleboard. Fire blocks may also be of gypsum board, cement fiber board, batts or blankets of :twisted: mineral :twisted: or glass fiber, or other approved materials installed in such a manner as to be securely retained in place. Loose-fill insulation shall not be used as a fire block unless specifically tested in the form and manner intended for use to demonstrate its ability to remain in place and to retard the spread of fire and hot gasses.
