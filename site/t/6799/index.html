cyeazel9 August 2006#2
How's it going? I'm in a similar situation as you. The first thing you will need to do is find out how much weight your floor can hold. Sound isolation construction requires heavier than average walls, ceilings, floors, etc, etc.. Even if you won't be tracking drums, you'll still need beefier than average partitions. So get those weight ratings for your floor before you even think about floating.
If so then the roof seems to be the big issue since it doesn't go full height and I'm sure the sound will 'filter in' through the homosote tiles from the adjacent offices, so how does one them make a 'soundproof' roof.
The best solution for the roof is to rip out the tiles all together and frame a new solid multi layered drywall ceiling with the new ceiling joists resting on top of your new wall topplates. Again though, you need to find out how much weight your existing floor is capable of holding before you can begin doing anything.

sharward9 August 2006#4
Good sound isolation construction techniques can weigh a number of tons, so yes, I agree, it is critical that you determine how much weight that upper floor can hold before you can design a solution. More than likely, a structural engineer will need to be consulted. The engineer's report will include the total number of kilograms per square meter of dead load that the floor is capable of supporting. Once you have that, you'll know how much mass you can safely add. Then you should be able to calculate your expected isolation performance and then decide if it's good enough for your needs. If it's not, then you'll need to find a space that is compatible with them. ...and I suspect the former is the likely outcome... :roll: ...Sorry, just being realistic. sharward9 August 2006#9
You're not alone in your befuddledness. :) A wall can be one leaf (e.g., a solid brick wall), or two leaves (e.g., a concrete block wall, or a stick frame with gypsum boards on each side), or (bad!) three leaves, or (worse!) four leaves. In fact, I could probably design a single wall that had ten leaves if I were stupid enough. ;-) In short, a wall is one or more leaves. If multiple layers of material, such as gypsum wallboard, are applied firmly against each other with no air pocket in between, then those multiple layers constitute a single leaf. A simple way of looking at it is this: pretend you are sound and your mission is to try to penetrate walls. If you pass through mass, then air, then mass, you passed through two leaves. If three separate masses, then it's three leaves. Etc...... --Keith :mrgreen:
