Soundman202014 July 2014#2
Ben, I would say that you basically have two options here: 1) Open up your ceiling completely, so you have full access to the "floor" above you, and beef that up with plenty of additional mass as well as careful air-tight sealing all over, and also take all your outer-leaf walls fully up to that, once again with careful sealing. This assumes that the existing structure is able to withstand the huge extra mass you'd need to add to it: 3,500 ft2 at, for example, 7 PSF (3 layers of 5/8" drywall, roughly) works out to well over 24,000 pounds of extra mass... :shock: :!: Can the structure up there handle that? Clearly, you cannot answer that yourself: you will need your structural engineer to figure that out for you: It also assumes that you are even allowed to do that: You'd be modifying the structure of the building itself, and your contract very likely does not allow that, if you are sharing the building with others. There's also the issue of insurance: will your insurance cover the liability and risk you take on by doing such a major modification of the building structure? 2) Forget all of the above, and just build a new ceiling over the entire isolate section of your studio, with sufficient mass to provide the isolation you need. Yes, this will be a 3-leaf system, but sometimes that is the only reasonable solution. Yes, a 3-leaf system does potentially reduce isolation in the low end of the spectrum, which you very definitely need to isolate greatly, but there are ways you can compensate for the lost isolation. With this plan, you will still need to put up those 20-something-thousand pounds of mass (and probably much more) in order to build that "middle leaf", but it will be all within "your" area of the building, and likely not subject to the same problems as trying to modify the parts of the building shared by others tenants. And you'll still need your structural engineer to do all the math for you, figuring out how to support that while spanning the distances you need to span, etc. But overall, I suspect this might be the better way to go. And since your isolated space is basically split in two by the central corridor, I suspect that your best bet would be to do two independent isolated areas, one on each side of the central corridor, so that you only need to span that much shorter distances on the sides (less than half the building width), and you'd not need to isolate the corridor at all like this, since it would be outside the isolation area. Save you some money! In other words, you would not need to isolate the full 3,500 ft2 of floor area: only the exact amount that actually needs isolation, which I suspect is a whole lot less! Personally, I'd go with option #2! :) It will need some careful design (way, way more details than what you are showing in your very basic models so far), and it will need major attention to that detail in the HVAC system, which will now be split in tow as well, and still needs to penetrate all of the walls while providing the extreme isolation you need, but overall it looks like the best solution to me, by far. Also the cheapest... :) - Stuart -

Select the six individual faces that form one joists, right-click and select "make component" (with "replace selection" turned on). Now select the component joist, tap the "ctrl" key on your keyboard, and a small "+" sign should appear next to the cursor, indicating that you are going to copy the joists, not move it. Drag cursor over a bit in the direction you want to copy it, just a couple of inches, then left click once. Now type the real distance you want in the "length" box (bottom right corner of screen), such as for example ' 16" ', and hit enter. Another option for your "middle-leaf" ceiling might be "beam and block", which is basically just pre-stressed concrete joists that are specially shaped to take concrete blocks in between them. Like this: http://www.milbank.co.uk/beam-and-block.php That gives you the best possible isolation you could ever hope for, since it retains the high mass of your walls all the way around the enclosure, and also solves the issue of how to get a lot of mass on the "middle-leaf ceiling" easily, fast and at at reasonable cost. Then you build your inner-leaf as you described above, with stud-and-drywall for both the walls and ceilings. With enough mass on those leaves too, and a good air gap, you will get major isolation, both between rooms and certainly to the outside world. Just make sure you leave enough space between the inner-leaf ceiling and the middle-leaf ceiling to fit in all your HVAC ducts and silencer boxes.... :) That's a whole different kettle of fish that you haven't even looked at yet...

Soundman202017 July 2014#8
In theory does this sound like a plan?
Well.... In theory, there's the issue of the math. The density of brick is very roughly 2300 kg/m3, so a 9" thick brick wall weighs about 525 kg for every square meter of surface. Drywall, on the other hand, has a density of around 650 kg/m3, so two layers of 15mm will weigh roughly 20 kg for every square meter. In other words, the surface density of your walls would be roughly twenty-six TIMES higher than the surface density of the ceiling. So you'd need about 54 layers of 15mm drywall to get the same mass up there... :shock: :!: Conclusion: theory is not on your side here! There is no point in doing the walls 26 times better than the ceiling, especially when it is the CEILING that needs the most mass, as it is the middle-leaf of a 3-leaf system. In theory, that middle-leaf ceiling should have twice as much mass as each of the other two leaves (or in other words: the mass of the inner leaf and outer leaf combined, should be the same as the mass of the middle leaf). The conclusions that you can draw from this are that either 1) there is no point at all in doing brick walls, when stud-and-drywall will match the same mass as the ceiling. 2) you need a hell of a lot more mass on the ceiling. Those are your two potions. So which way should you go from here? Increase the mass on the ceiling, or reduce the mass on the walls? The only real answer I can give is: "Beats me! I have no idea. Not a clue." Not because I can't figure it out, but simply because I don't have the basic data I need for figuring it out. Once again, it boils down to not having a number: You still don't know what your goal is! You really should settle on the amount of isolation that you need, in terms of how many decibels of transmission loss you want and the lowest frequency you need it at, then work forward from there. Right now, it is all just guess work, with no actually goal in sight. It's like you are saying "I want to get in my car and drive to my destination: please tell me how to get there.", but when people ask you "OK, but where is your destination?"; all you can say is "I'm not sure: some nice place that I'd like to be that isn't here where I am right now!" :shock: :!: I think you get the point. At present, you have no real idea where you are going, or what you are aiming for, other than "It is different from what I have right now". If you settle on a figure, then I can do the math and tell you which of your current two options will get you there, but without a goal, it's better to just toss a coin: regardless of which way it lands, the answer will be equally "right" since there is no "wrong" when you don't have anything to aim for. The tossed coin will give the "right" answer, simply because there is no meaningful question! There are equations for calculating what you want to know, in terms of what density materials you need, and how you should arrange them, but in order for the equations to give a valid answer, you have to feed them valid data! I can plug in a number like "61 dB" or "45 Hz" but I cannot plug in "I'd like some isolation please" and "musical frequencies that the band likes". The equations only work for objective numbers, not subjective feelings. So I really cannot say which option is the best for you! If you only need low to moderate isolation, and are fine with lots of bleed between rooms and to the outside world, then by all means, skip the brick walls and just go with simple stud-and-drywall everywhere. Or if rally DO you need the very high isolation that you would have got from bricks, then you need to figure out a way to get similar mass on the middle-leaf ceiling. There's no way I can give you better advice than that, since I don't know where you are going, or what you are aiming for. If you have no clear destination, then telling you "Drive north" is just as good as "Drive south": both will get you to a destination: it just might not be the one you wanted to arrive at... All I can say is that doing massive 500 kg/m2 walls along flimsy 20 kg/m2 ceilings, makes no sense, acoustically speaking. - Stuart -
