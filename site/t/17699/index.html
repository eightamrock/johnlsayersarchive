Hi Mike, and Welcome! :) Please post your photos and SketchUp files directly in the forum, so that they can be seen in your post, instead of linking to them off-site.
The north wall has a large window on it, and I think that's the wall I want to put my speakers on.
Looks like the best plan to me! That will have your speakers firing down the long axis of the room, which is good.
The room is fairly large for a home studio, approximately 13' by 20'.
Height? That's necessary for calculating the modal response of the room. And that means the height to the actual solid boundary of the room, not to the soft ceiling tiles that you mention. The acoustic boundary of the room is the solid, hard, massive, rigid ceiling beyond those tiles. That's what low frequency sound waves "see", so that's what is important for calculating modes.
There is an air return on the ceiling that protrudes most of the way into the room. This is the only real asymmetrical part of the listening environment.
That's a problem. Symmetry in the front half of the room is critical, and that is going to mess things up. So you should be thinking about how to deal with that. There are a couple of options.
In terms of speaker placement, it seems like putting the listening position 37% off the front (north) wall would be a bit far. This would put the listening posiiton at approximately 7.5' from the front wall. Does that seem like it's a bit far to anyone else?
It's probably about right, depending on your speakers and your plan for setting them up, but 38% is just a guideline, a good starting point, and not written in stone. As long as you stay away from the really bad locations at 25% and 50%, and stay reasonably close to 38%, you should be OK.
In terms of determining listening position, I know I should measure for low frequency response to make sure I'm not in a null.
That's a good plan, but don't forget that treatment and furniture can also change the modal response of a room. And so can the way you set up your speakers. What speakers are they? (Brand, model). Do you plan to flush-mount them ("soffit mount") or are you just going to have them on stands?
Should I set up both speakers, measure from a listening position, move speakers, measure from the same position, etc? Then move the listening position and measure from potential speaker positions? (necessitating probably 3-5 measurements and moving speakers/stands per each listening position)
One really good way of doing it is to set up one speaker only, in one of the front tri-corners of the room, play loud sine sweeps and also music through that, then listen/measure at several potential listening positions down the centerline of the room, at a height of 1.2m above the floor, to find the one with the least objectionable response. All modes terminate in corners, so setting up a speaker in a tri-corner is guaranteed to excite all of them. With that done, then set up both speakers with correct geometry and play music, to fine tune the location. But once again, don't forget that furniture and treatment can change that, so you might need to refine once again, after everything is in place.
As you can see in the photos, the room is carpeted.

I am using JBL LSR4328's on stands. The stands are 34" tall. I also use monitor isolation pads which add about 1.5" to the height.
That sounds like it might be ending up too high. "Standard" height is 1.2m above the floor (47.25"), but that refers to the acoustic axis of the speakers (not the top or bottom of the cabinet). So you should check that, and see if you can figure a way to get the acoustic axis to the correct height. Also, the stands should be massive, heavy, and very solid. If they are hollow metal frames, then you could fill them with dry sand to gain the mass that you need.
Unfortunately, it's a rental, so I don't have the liberty of removing the carpet. I could put a laminate down or something over the top of the carpet, if you think that would help.
The issue with carpet is that it is "randomly selective high frequency absorption". What all small rooms need is lots of bass trapping (and I mean LOTS of it), a bit in the mids, and probably little to nothing in the high end. What carpet gives you, is plenty in the high end, fading out through the mid range, and nothing at all in the lows. In other words, it does exactly the opposite of what you need for a small room. Also, the general rule in studios is "hard floor, soft ceiling". Of course, rules are made to be broken, but take a look around high-end professional studios and you'll see that very, very few of them have carpet on the floor. There are several reasons for that, and many of those reasons are related to acoustics. So a hard floor is preferably You could cover your carpet with a layer of thick plywood (say 3/4" OSB, MDF, etc) and then laminate on top of that. You normally cannot lay laminate directly on carpet, as the carpet "gives" to much, and doesn't provide the firm surface that laminate needs. So that would be an option, but it does mean losing probably an inch of your precious room height. However, if that is your only option, then that's what it will have to be!
I should also mention that I don't care about sound transmission out of the space. I'm only concerned with treatment inside the space.
I thought that might be the case, and you don't really have enough space in there to isolate properly anyway, so "treatment only" it is!
I'm really just looking to create a space in which I can make translatable mixes. :-) I suppose just like everyone else that tried to treat a room. :-)
