bassman5 March 2009#2
Your method is much stronger, although you might have one too many studs in that corner. You want a stiff wall if you need good TL in the lower octaves. Her method looks harder to do, IMHO, but YMMV. Those are all 2x4 lumber, right? You might not need double top plates either. I would go with double bottom plates though. Doors in studios are REALLY heavy and should have reinforcement in their framing. Double king studs with a cripple and a decent header will help keep the door true and keep the seals in place longer as the place settles. Do it right now since it can save you trouble later. -ashley
Sandersd5 March 2009#3
I would have her explain the reasoning behind her framing. I don't see the purpose of the blocks. Usually there are two studs in the corner so the drywall can be secured along the edge. Her method would leave the edges unsupported which is not typical. If she has a rational explanation that would give you something to evaluate. Your method is not quite typical either, at least not for the USA, perhaps it is in Europe. Typical framing (again for USA) would use two studs with blocking in between on one wall and a single stud on the other. There are other methods that use less materials and leave more room for insulation. For instance, you could use drywall clips and one stud or simply use two studs. I sure you can google typical corner stud framing for ideas. When researching, the most important question you should ask is "why?" Good luck.

Soundman20205 March 2009#8
Ok, now I guess either one will be ok.
Huh??? Hang on a sec! I didn't see anyone say that "her" design was good! I saw everyone say that YOURS was good, and "hers" was questionable... Where are you going to attach your drywall corners, in "her" design? Answer: nowhere! Your corners will "float", unsupported. That doesn't sound like a good design to me, (especially if you decide be inside-out as in xSpace's layout)! You need surfaces underneath the edges of the drywall to nail it to and support it firmly. "Her" design has none. The edges of the drywall will only have each other for support, and I certainly wouldn't like to rely only on flimsy, vibrating drywall edges for the airtight seal around my studio! One question: You do plan to support your inner leaf ceiling from those walls, right? If so, then you will have lots of MASS up there.... mass that needs firm support. I'd go with double top plates and xSpace's layout for the corners. That saves you one stud per corner (three instead of four), and still gives you good support and nailing surfaces. It is only one more stud than "she" plans to use, and is a lot easier to construct. Another question: Are you using 24" OC spacing, or 16"? Going with 24" will reduce your total stud count considerably, and also improve the acoustic properties of the wall itself, as it brings down the resonant frequency slightly, as well as being slightly more resilient. If you were planning 16" OC, then you can save a lot by going to 24" OC. But stick with the double top plates and the sturdy corners, as in xSpace's diagram. My $0.02 - Stuart -

Stu, I didn't have a layout ;) Further clarification for carlston; When the walls are framed and stood upright, you(she) will have to nail these corners together...just to keep them up in the air so they do not fall on your head. Framing does not change just because it is a "studio". The reasons it is done in your house is the same reason it is done in a home theater or studio build. Food for thought, sound frequency as we all have learned or are still learning builds up in corners. Pick a corner ;) It is a common phenomenon that pressure, on most anything you can speak of, will be applied most dramatically, at the corners. A water tank filled of water has more pressure at the corners. When forming concrete, it is a known fact that, all things being equal, if the form is going to blow, it's going to be at the corner. Build a strong corner, not a girly corner :) Brien
