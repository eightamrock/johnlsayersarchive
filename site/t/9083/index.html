Miles10 July 2007#8
Philosophical question. I've seen many requests like this one, and the proposed design is almost always the same: carve a number of small rooms out of the available space, even if it results in tiny rooms. Not that the BBC is the final authority, but I think their 1500 cubic foot minimum is a reasonable rule. Very small rooms have serious modal problems, don't they? Sure, we can throw up a lot of absorption to attenuate the modal problems, but then we run the risk of creating a dead, unpleasant room. Why not start with a bigger space that will almost certainly have a better distribution of modal waves? I recently read the Gervais book, and it's interesting to me that he himself uses a larger single-room space, but he provides construction details for--the smaller multiple room plan! It just seems to me there are obvious acoustic advantages to constructing one 3600 cubic foot space as opposed to three 1200 cubic foot spaces. Am I missing something? Miles
jwl10 July 2007#9
That's a really good question, Miles. In general I agree, small rooms are to be avoided, though a "vocal booth" type of room that is small and dead and isolated can be useful in certain recording situations. After much thought I decided to go with one room in my 12x20x8 garage. Though I do think about adding a vocal booth a lot. I mostly record rock bands, and I pretty much always prefer to record the band in the same room. You usually get a better performance. With gobos and good mic placement you can get the best of both worlds: good isolation on close mics, and well-balanced room mics. Vocals are another story. I generally prefer to overdub vocals, which does require some isolation.

You know, that's a very good question, Miles. While I most certainly don't have the answer as to "why" or presume to speak for John and the others that are contributing to this forum, what I do know is that Ewell's request was for a design based on Ratty 7's, and so that is what I tried to do. Personally, I am happy for (and envy) those with larger studio environments. My reality however, is that I only have a rather small space, as do many of us here on this forum; as home studios often are. And having had to record bands in "one room" settings, my preference is to have a bit more isolation from the instruments being recorded - especially acoustic guitars, acoustic drums, and acoustic pianos - in order to hear the subtleties in mic choice and placement, and dynamic processing as necessary. If one were recording primarily MIDI or sampled instruments, a larger one room approach might be just fine. Ultimately, what we try to do here is take the design and construction principles that John, Knightfly, Rod, Glenn and others have shared, and incorporate them as much as is possible and within our means into our oftentimes modest settings in order to make the best of what we have available at the time. Wishing the best of success to all, regardless of your studio size. :wink: Aloha 8)
