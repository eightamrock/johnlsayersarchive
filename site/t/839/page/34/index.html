Greetings and welcome to the site.
1) I am thinking about using external sliding glass doors for some areas (due to relativley decent STC rating - not fantastic, but pretty good for the money spent), and the modles I've been looking at have a pretty good-sized bottom threshold (like, 3.5"). Sliding glass doors are common for a lot of studio's. I would suggest doing a double set of doors though if you do. Either in a double wall scenario or soundlock application.
2) As usual, I'm running out of cash (the rest of the remodel did that), and am looking for REALLY inexpensive alternatives for just about everything. Would neoprene pucks under 2x4 (on edge) packed with pretty inexpensive insolation covered with a single layer of 3/4" ply afford enough ridgidity and sound iso between control and recording rooms? This build is in a 100% underground basement, so not that concerned with sound getting to the outside world. Pretty dry area as well (entire house perimeter is french drained, sloped, re-parged, and about 100 other things done to address water) so not sure if I need dehumids?
First off, there is no "inexpensive" way to build a floating floor. If you don't need to (especially in a basement), don't. There were a few reasons for floating my control room. 1: The floor sloped nearly 3" from one corner to the other. 2: I needed to run lots of conduits. 3: Additional isolation 4: Moisture Second, 1/2" neoprene is what you need if you do float the floor (around 60 duro). Third, What's your definition of cheap insulation? Fourth, I would highly recommend 2 layers of 3/4". One, you want to deaden the floor as much as possible (mass). Second, the weight you'll have on the floor (especially if you construct walls on top) as well as all your gear. And also, give's better isolation by staggering the joints between the second and first layers.

Luftweg25 September 2006#844
Conduit is the only way to go... even for ALL the residential wiring runs: it's much neater and organized (you can see which conduit goes what direction); prevents gnawing damage from rodents; protects against accidental piercing of wires/cables (from, say, drilling a hole in the wall); provides a grounding 'shell' around conductors; ALSO, conduits allow much easier addition/changes of circuits by being able to pull wires through... The downside is the price of the condiut and fittings, and having to hickey the conduit to the proper shapes... Usually though, compared to romex cable, single conductors on large rolls are more economical... so the cost of the conduit is balanced off. At least, that's the way it was when I had done such electrical work... If you have to pay other people to run conduit, though, it may be expensive....

Luftweg25 September 2006#846
ah, you are right... yes, thinwall is quite cheap, really.... It's labor costs that might kill you with having conduit run -- but if you do it yourself, you'll save that. I would never use 1/2 inch, unless it was only from a junction to a single light fixture or switch, or something of that nature... large appliances, and any circuit homeruns would be best with at least 3/4 inch -- if not for just the ease of pulling alone. 3/4 inch is probably the thickest that you can practically use a hand hickey to bend... It's not too too bad, but certainly alot harder than 1/2 inch.
