Greetings Steve :) While the idea is great in principle, I cannot see how this can be constructed without the danger of making things worse. For example, even if you knew the modulus of elasticity of the elastomer being used, there's no easy way of determining the load on each side block with this design. And if you don't know the exact load on the elastomer you're in great danger of ending up with a natural freqeuncy within the audio band. If this happens sound will not be isolated but indeed amplified at an around this natural freqeuncy. If you clamp it too tight = waste of effort. If you clamp it too loose = waste of effort. To design a decoupling system that works properly these following factors have to be taken into consideration.... 1] Modulus of elasticity of the elastomer, which varies according to load time and temprature 2] The load on the elastomer per unit area 3] The shape factor of the elastomer blocks ( ratio of load area v. compressible area ) 4] the size of the elastomer blocks 5] The lowest frequency you want to Isolate The natural freqeuncy should be at least to 4 octaves below the lowest freqeuncy you want to isolate. Indeed up until 1.4 times the natural freqeuncy there is not only NO isoaltion there is amplification. The higher the load, the LOWER the natural frequency, but ONLY upto a certain point. Increase the load over this and the system will either not work, or only work for a short period of time. Higher loads shorten the usable life span of elastomers, so you need to keep the load within a range where you're at least going to get a few years of servicable life out of it. It's quite easy to overload an elastomer to the extent where it only works for a month or less. The bottom line is this...... Decoupling HAS to be calculated properly. Anything else is a gamble. And believe me the odds are NOT in your favour. This site will tell you how to calculate things http://www.earsc.com/HOME/engineering/TechnicalWhitePapers/Vibration/index.asp?SID=61 hope that helps Paul p.s why is this forum only accesible at certain times of the day?

knightfly26 October 2004#285
Whether to build soffits straight or leaning, depends a lot on the geometry of your room; if, considering your console/desk/chair arrangement, you can get your soffited speakers at a height that will both allow direct line of sight without things between your ears and the monitors, AND you can get both your ears and your monitors NOT at specific nulls/peaks vertically, AND this allows the speakers to be aimed directly at your head (still in the VERTICAL measurement, AND you're more concerned with a mix position sweet spot than a "producer's table" or "client couch" sweet spot, then (whew) the soffits should be plumb and not slanted. If ANY of these conditions aren't true, so that raising the monitors above ear height (while seated) becomes necessary, then the angle of tilt should be such that the center line of the speaker propagation falls somewhere between being pointed directly at your ears and being pointed directly at the producer's/client's ears; if these two angles would be different, I'd favor the mix position by about 60-70% - even more if the rearward positions aren't as important to you (as in, you mainly do your own projects and occasionally hire out yourself and/or the studio) So, bottom line - you'd need a side elevation of the room, including console, couch, producer's desk, front wall(s), to scale; this will tell you more of what you need to do than almost anything else... Steve

Greetings Aaron :) You should read the Genelec Technical papers on soffit mounting. There's about 3 or 4 papers on the subject on the Genelec Site. btw - I've thoroughly searched the Internet for soffit mounting advice over the last 2 years, and the Genelec Site is the BEST, and probably only decent advice I can find. Rear ported speakers are NOT suitable for soffit mounting. Speakers with amps on the back, should have the amps remotely positioned for the obvious heat reasons. Soffit mounted speakers will require the bass to be reduced, as soffit mounting increase bass. Genelec also advise a LOT of rear wall absorption, as soffit mounting excites the room modes more than free standing speakers. You should also avoid any part of the soffits/soffit wall touching your main wall behind it for isolation reasons. regarding fitting larger speakers in the future.... I have the same issue. Luckily it's ok to make the soffits larger than the speakers themselves. You stuff the gap with rockwool. However the front bezel/soffit wall MUST be exactly flush with the front of the speaker cabinet ( to prevent diffraction ), and the BEZEL around the speaker MUST be as close fitting as possible without touching it Have a look at my studio build diary http://forum.studiotips.com/viewforum.php?f=1 Apart from the drywalling I've just finished my soffit wall, and have designed it according to Genelec's recommendations. Hope that helps :) Paul
