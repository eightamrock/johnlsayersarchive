Hi there "WheresTheDug", and Welcome to the forum!
I now know enough to be dangerous
Then that makes you fully qualified to build a studio! :) I'm going to give you some quick answers here on the forum, mostly for others who might have the same questions, but I'll also PM you with more details.
Given the clean sheet of paper, what are my options and what order should I do things? e.g. Do I need to engage an architect first to design a structure and then fit a studio inside or design the studio first and then have an architect design a structure around it? Can I engage one person to do it all? When should I involve planning authorities?
First, most architects do get some acoustic training during their studies... but not very much! Over the entire four or five years of their degree courses, they get just a few hours of acoustics, to cover the basics. So I would suggest that it would not be a good idea to let an architect do a studio design. In fact, late last year I was called in by a church that was in the final stages of building their new sanctuary, and had discovered some, shall we say "acoustic oversights" by their architect. The church looked beautiful, but sounded awful and did not isolate. Architects... :) It was a bit late to be calling in an acoustic guy with the walls up and the roof already on ( :shock: :!: ), but that's when they realized how big the problem was. We managed to fix it mostly, but it would have been a lot easier (and much cheaper for them!) if they would have gotten an acoustic guy in at the beginning. So to answer your first question: Get a studio designer to do the basic design and layout for the entire building, except for the service areas (lobby, office, bathroom, green room, storage, etc.) then get your architect to do those non-acoustic parts, pretty up the overall design, check the structural design, draw up the plans, walk you through the local maze of permits and regulations and inspections, and oversee the construction, jointly with your studio designer. Architect and designer should work together as a team. The only time you would hire one single guy to do it all is if there is a local architect who knows the ins and outs of your local building code and other applicable laws, and who is also an expert studio designer with many completed studios under his belt, and happy customers that you can talk to. To be honest, the chances of finding a guy like that are not good... Finally: Should you involve planning authorities? Absolutely YES! You very likely must get permits to do this, and you must get all your inspections signed off properly. That's what you need the local architect for mostly: to walk you through the legal tangle of red tape, in getting those permits and passing your inspections. Every municipality on the planet is different, and they all have strange quirks in their regulations, bylaws, rules, and guidelines, so you need a LOCAL guy who knows the ropes to guide you through that. If you don't have everything fully signed off, then you are letting yourself in for some major legal liability. For example, if you build without permits and something goes wrong, then your home-owners insurance will not cover any of it, and you will be fully liable. Let's say you calculated something wrong, part of the ceiling collapses and injures one of your clients, band members, a friend, or even just a guy trespassing on your property: with no permits, you'll have the wonderful privilege of getting to cover all of their medical bills out of your own pocket, as well as damage to their property (cars, equipment, etc), as well as the cost of your own repairs. There may even be criminal liability in such cases. So yeah, you totally, absolutely, entirely must do this right, with everything correctly documented and inspected, and with your permits duly arranged.

One of my biggest questions is how small can I go and still be able to use the space as intended? I know that the minimum size for a control room is generally considered 1500sqft.
I think you might be confusing floor area (ft2) with room volume (ft3). EBU specs call for a minimum floor area of 320 ft2 (actually, 30 m2), and ITU says 20m2 (215 ft2) with an 8 foot ceiling, the ITU spec works out to a room volume of roughly 1700 cubic feet, which seems to be where the general recommendation of 1500 ft3 comes from.
Given the desired usage of the live room how small do you think is realistic?
Ideally, the LR should have about 5 times the room volume of the CR. That's in order to guarantee that the decay time of the LR is substantially longer than that of the CR, such that you can actually hear the real room reverberation and decay of the LR while listening to tracks in the CR, without it being masked by the natural decay of the CR itself. That's the theory, at least. However, that does NOT mean that you can't build a great studio in a smaller building, or that you can't do great recordings in a studio where the LR is less than 5 times the volume of the CR. John has built an entire studio inside a shipping container! So you can go small and still be good.
The other thing I am deliberating over is whether to opt for the single room option instead of a control room and separate live room? I know Rod says he uses this layout for his own studio.
some people are fine with that. Personally, I find it really hard to track drums and other loud instruments in the same room. I need to be able to hear how each mic on the drum kits sounds, all by itself on the CR monitors, without also hearing the direct sound from the drums right next to me in the same room, or even substantial bleed from them in the other room. I just like to be able to hear what my mics are picking up and nothing else, as much as possible. So I prefer a separate tracking room. I also don't like having to wear earplugs while I am tracking, or heavily isolated headphones: I like to hear the monitors alone, cleanly. But that's just me: other folks are just fine with tracking loud things right next to them.
