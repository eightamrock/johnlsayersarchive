... will make the A/C for my studio as depicted on the book basically a simple box on each side for air circulation covered with insulation and going through the division...
If I understand you correctly, you plan to have just one single silencer that is fitted between the two rooms? I can't see how that would work. The silencer box is not meant to be used to pass air between the control room and live room. There should be two different air paths, one for the live room and one for the control room, and each path needs two silencers: one on the incoming fresh air path, and the other on the outgoing stale air path. If you have to move enough air for BOTH rooms through only ONE silencer box, then it will have to be a very large silencer, and the fan will have to move a large volume of air. The air conditioner unit will also be a problem, since it will have to cover BOTH rooms, regardless of whether only one room is in use, or both together. Figuring that out is not going to be easy. It is a much better idea to have two separate systems, one for each room. Also: where are you going to get fresh air from, if you only have one silencer between the rooms? You need to bring in fresh air from outside. And where are you going to exhaust the stale air to? The stale air has to go back to the outside world. Each air path to the outside world must have a silencer on it. So you need at least, and preferably four. You can't just circulate air between the two rooms, with no fresh air inlet or stale air outlet.
You think I should decouple the hole room?? Just for te ceiling?
Yes. If you don't decouple the entire room, then why bother building it in the first place? If your inner leaf walls are coupled to the outer leaf ceiling, and vice-versa, then you actually don't really have a two-leaf MSM wall at all! And it won't isolate very well. You say that you plan to play live drums in there, so it is going to be LOUD. so you do need isolation, it seems.
I wanted it to be 4 meters high but they wouldn't allow me... My neighbours are a bit far and I have nothing above it, would my sound isolation be reduced that much?
The height does not have much affect the isolation of the room: It affects the acoustics of the room. If you want the room to sound good, then more height is better. 4 m would have been great. 2.5 is not that great, but not terrible either. You mean that the finished height will be 2.5 m, right? After the room is completed, with the new ceiling in place? If you mean that the OUTER leaf ceiling is at 2.5 m, then you are going to loose around 15 to 20 cm of that for the inner leaf, so you'll be down to around 2.3 m, maybe even less. - Stuart -

Soundman20207 October 2009#19
From the sequence of photos you posted, you started out with one long room, put up inner-leaf walls down both sides of the long room, one long continuous wall down each side. You also put one single continuous ceiling over both rooms, and that ceiling is attached to the outer leaf walls, as well as the inner leaf walls: So there you have your first issue: The outer leaf is directly attached to the inner leaf via the ceiling structure. There is no isolation there. In order to isolate, the inner leaf ceiling must be attached ONLY to the inner leaf walls, not touching the outer leaf at any point. Not even one single nail: Total separation. Yes, even a single nail or screw that bridges from leaf to leaf will mess up your isolation, but in your case you have not only nails but the entire ceiling structure bridging between the leaves. Sound waves hitting the wall (or ceiling) will cause it to vibrate, and since there is a direct mechanical connection, those vibrations will travel along the connection and cause the wall on the other side to vibrate as well: A vibrating wall is a very good loudspeaker. That's your first problem. Your SECOND problem is with the "one long room" construction. Like I said above, you built a single wall down each side of the long room, and a single ceiling too. Now you are putting a partition ("division") across the middle of the room, from side to side and floor to ceiling. But that partition simply butts up against the walls on each side, and the ceiling above! There is no break in the walls between rooms. It is the SAME wall in both rooms, and the SAME ceiling, with just with a partition touching them. That partition will not do much to stop sound, since the sound will be IN the walls and the ceiling. Sound will flank AROUND your partition, through the walls and ceiling. Sound hitting the wall in one room will cause the entire wall to vibrate, in BOTH rooms, since it is just one single wall. Same for the ceiling. What you SHOULD have done, is to build separate walls for each room, and a separate ceiling for each room. Each wall should end where it attaches to one side of the partition, then there should be an air gap between the two sides of the partition (and each side on its own separate from), then the wall for the other room starts at the other side of the partition. The same for the ceiling. That's the issue. To isolate, you MUST decouple, fully. If you don0t then you have not isolated, and will get sound transmission in, out and between rooms via the flanking paths, which in your case is the entire structure. In order to isolate, you have to build each room independently, totally separate from the other room, and totally separate from the outer leaf that surrounds both rooms. Nothing can touch. NOTHING! Each room should consist of four walls and a ceiling. The walls stand on the floor, and the ceilings rests on the walls, but neither the walls nor the ceilings can touch anything else. This is often referred to as "room within a room" construction. Think of building two totally independent "small rooms within the one big room", but nothing touches. Take a close look at some of the designs on this forum for studios, and you will see how careful people are to ensure that NOTHING touches. No part of the inner leaf can ever touch part of the outer leaf, and no part of the inner leaf for one room can ever touch part of the inner leaf for another room. Everything is separate, independent, decoupled, unconnected, unbridged, etc. If you want to isolate your rooms, then that is the only way to do it for a reasonable cost. There are no short cuts, no magic tricks to get around the laws of physics, no magic products that can help you. I'd really suggest that you need to halt your construction right now, and spend some time learning about these concepts, and re-designing your studio so that it will actually work. The way you are doing it right now is not going to work. - Stuart -

Soundman20207 October 2009#25
You still seem to be not understanding the underlying concepts: If you hang the ceiling rigidly from ANY part of the outer leaf, it is STILL coupled to the outer leaf. Bolts, brackets, solid wire, screws, nails. Anything solid like that is a flanking path, and couples the inner leaf to the outer leaf. There must be NO solid, rigid, mechanical attachments AT ALL, between the inner leaf and the outer leaf. The ONLY way that you could hang your ceiling correctly from the outer leaf, is to buy some proper acoustic ceiling hangers to do it. They have specially designed springs or special rubber pads to correctly suspend the ceiling while decoupling it. You can use those, but you have to buy them: You cannot make them yourself. Even then, you MUST do the calculations to make sure that you use the correct type of acoustic hanger, and that you use the correct number of them, in the correct locations. You CANNOT simply put up a few bolts with wire on them and hope that it will work. It won't. You have to calculate the total weight of your ceiling, get a qualified engineer to tell you where and in how many places that ceiling needs to be supported, then calculate how much weight will be hanging from each point, then purchase the correct hanger for each point that will compress to the correct amount for the amount of weight that it has to support at that point. It is not that easy! You seem to be building this studio without having planed it at all, and without understanding anything about acoustics or studio construction. You have made some big mistakes, and now you don't want to do the right things to fix them, which is to stop, back up, learn, plan, and then start again. Like I said, there are NO MAGIC SOLUTIONS to studio construction.
