Soundman202017 January 2010#6
I'm trying to figure out something about ideal ratios for my CR. Are these ratios for inner walls? outer walls?
Inner walls. The ratio refers to the inside dimensions of the final finished surfaces of your inner leaf, prior to installing acoustic treatment. In other words, it is the walls that you will see when you stand inside the room right after you put up the final layer of drywall, but before you hang absorbers, resonators, etc.
I plan to use a standard rectangular shape for inner walls with side walls and ceilling angled a bit (10° approx, larger at the back wall). I guess this ratio would be for maximum length and height, so for the back wall. Am I right?
That's a real hard question to answer! I've been asking that myself for a while now, but nobody seems to be able to give an absolute answer. Personally, I reckon the best idea is to go with an average for your non-parallel surfaces. My reasoning (and I may be wrong here) is that for the wavelengths that are of the most concern, at the low end of the spectrum, the difference between the dimension at one end of the wall and at the other end doesn't seem to be significant. It's just a small fraction of the wavelength, so averaging out seems to be the reasonable thing to do. However "reasonable" isn't always "right" in acoustics! By the way, your side walls need to be angled at 6 degrees each (total of 12°), and your ceiling needs to be angled at 12° too. Everything starts narrow at the front (where the speakers are) and gets wider/higher at the back. Assuming that your speaker axis intersect angle will be 60°, of course. If the speaker intersect angle is higher, then the wall and ceiling angles also need to increase. I don't have the exact figures on hand, but I seem to recall that you need 22° ceiling and 11° walls if your speaker intersect goes up to 90°. Basically, the entire room geometry needs to be based around your speakers, so that's the first item you need to get right: Speaker location vs. mix position. Once that is in place, the rest of the room geometry kind of falls into place fairly easily. - Stuart -

That's fine for your booth / live room, but for your control room it really does need to be "absolutely under control". If not, your mixes will never translate well.
As I don't master the theory I would like to improve stuff step by step, by being in the room and listening to it, feeling it, trying stuff.
That's also fine, as long as you start with a basic layout that will allow you to do that! If you start with an acoustically bad layout, there is absolutely nothing that you can do afterwards to "get it sounding good", no matter how hard you try. For example, if you start with a cube-shaped control room, or one that has a curved front wall, or is asymmetric, or if the mix position is at the 50% point, or if the speakers are right in the corners a million other potentially really bad designs. Start with one of those and you will never make it sound good, regardless of what you do. You have to start with at least some understanding of acoustics for your basic layout, or you'll waste a lot of money and end up with a disaster. Maybe what I'm about to say here will appear blunt and insulting, but your approach to design is equivalent to a hopeful "musician" coming along and saying "I don't master the theory, so I'd like to improve my guitar playing step by step, just listening to it, feeling it, trying stuff, but never bothering to learn what chords are, what scales are, or music theory, never taking any formal classes (or even informal), just kind of muddling along until I get it right".... In other words, just as in learning to play the guitar, for studio building there are some very basic principles that one just HAS to learn and understand.

this does not sound like a good plan to me. That's just my opinion, of course, for whatever you think it is worth, but having a half dozen people working on a studio, none of whom has any experience or even inclination to learn about basic acoustic principles, sounds like a recipe for failure. Just because someone knows how to build an apartment does NOT qualify them in the least for building a studio. Just because some knows how to wire a normal house does NOT qualify him to wire a studio. etc. Just as with basic design, ignorance here exposes the build to numerous serious errors, any one of which could potentially destroy the acoustics of the rooms. For example, if the drywallers try to build the walls the way they normally would for an office, then they will be wasting all of your money, period. All other efforts at good isolation and good acoustics will be pointless. If the electrician tried to install the light switches, plugs, wiring, distribution panels, grounding, etc. in the way he normally does, he'd trash thousands of dollars worth of carefully constructed isolation and also wreck your chances of a hum-free studio. If the glaziers tried to install the windows the way they normally do for a house, there would be totally ineffective isolation between your rooms and to the outside, so all your money invested in walls, floors, ceilings and doors would be wasted. If the carpenters were to install doors the way they normally do for an apartment, ditto: No isolation, wasted thousands of dollars. If the HVAC company tries to install the HVAC system like they normally do for a commercial building, the live room and control room will both be unusable for their intended purposes, even with the HVAC system turned off. etc. Building a studio without knowledge, and without even the desire to learn, seems like a 100% guarantee of failure, to me.
