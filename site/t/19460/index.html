Later down the track, I hope to be able to provide a separate space where my wife can teach piano, and which could also function as a small live room/booth. My current band plays rockabilly roots, with double bass, acoustic guitar, electric guitar and just a snare drum, so it’s not too loud. Occasionally though I like to jam with some other buddies who are into reggae and death metal, so at times it will get bass heavy in there! I’m hoping to get that down to a more neighbour friendly level, but not exactly sure what I should be aiming for. I want a room where I can play my drums without annoying the neighbours, and where I can jam, record and mix with my band. ... at a guess I’d say I need to bring the outside volume down to about 40 - 50dB, so I will need 55 - 65db Transmission Loss. Does that sound about correct?
That sounds about right, but 65 dB is a tall order. It can be done, but it ain't easy....
I gave the drums a good belting and measured the SPL from about 3 feet away and got 105db on an A-weighted SPL meter.
Essentially that means that you have no isolation at all right now. However, you should be measuring on "C" weighting, not "A". "A" is no use for measuring full-spectrum transmission loss, since it does not consider low frequencies. The curve rolls off steeply at the low end. "C" also rolls off, but much less steeply, and more closely resembles the perception of the human ear.
THE EXISTING SHED...
... is basically not giving you any usable isolation at all: maybe 10 to 20 dB, from your tests.
I already have a pile of 10mm Soundchek Gyprock which a builder friend salvaged from a shop re-fit for me.
You need to somehow convert that to 15mm (or 16mm, if possible) somehow. Maybe do a swap with someone? Or sell it and use the money to buy the thicker stuff? Unfortunately, 10mm is too thin for good studio isolation. Too flexible, mass too low, not so great resonant characteristics, etc. Also, looking at your photo of that stack of drywall, it seems like you have every pair of sheets attached to each other with thin wood strips. What's with that? It wouldn't be any use to you like that, even if it was 16mm.
Phase 1: Beef up and seal the current frame-work with as much gyprock as it can support.
You could do that, but frankly I wouldn't bother: Too much work and hassle. I would suggest rather going with the dreaded "three-leaf" route: Leave what you have there as the outer-leaf, as is, no changes, since it is not much use at all acoustically, then build a two-leaf structure inside it,with a lot of mass on the "middle" leaf. Yes, that would waste some of the space that you have, but it would greatly simplify the build.
I would also like to remove the current colourbond cladding on the outside walls and replace it with a flat surface, as both my wife and I like to paint and want to decorate the outside of the studio with a mural.
Ahhh! Well, that's different then! In that case, you could just use something very massive for that new outer sheathing, such as thick OSB or plywood as the base layer with thick fiber-cement board as the outer later. That would get you lots of mass, plus the surface that yo want for your murals. And then just build a single leaf inside, for the actual studio.
I’m happy to include this window, and was thinking perhaps I could knock up some sort of insulated “insert” for when I’m mixing, to deaden any reflections off the glass…
Not necessary: the room can be designed such that it isn't needed. In any case, if you want to keep light coming in, then you'll need a matching piece of glass on your inner leaf. And if you want high isolation, you'll need to replace the existing glass with thick laminate glass.

I’m hoping to move the joists up to 2.8 high, but that’s yet to be verified by a structural engineer. If it can’t be done, I thought perhaps I could “build around” the current joists. Has anyone here had experience doing this sort of thing?
I did a place in LA last year where we did exactly that: put in a new very large ridge beam supported only at the ends, then replace the trusses one by one with stick-built trusses with higher cross bars. Allowed us to raise the inner-leaf ceiling a couple of feet, and fit in the HVAC much better. IT can be done...
When I plug these dimensions into Bob Golds mode calculator
You show your room as being non-rectangular: therefore Bob's calculator won't produce accurate results. It only works for rectangles. However, you can plug in the average dimensions of the inner-leaf surfaces to get a rough idea of how it will behave.
it reveals several serious modes between 33.5 - 86.2hz,
I'm not seeing that! With the dimensions you gave, there are no issues at 33.5 Hz, nor at 86.2 Hz. The real issues are at 62 Hz, even more so at 69 Hz, where you have 4 modes all practically on top of each other, then at 76 Hz, and 93 Hz. Those are the issues under 100 Hz. I agree that the ratio is not good (the length is twice the height! :shock: ), but there are no modal issues at 33.5 or 86.2 Hz.
I wondered if its possible to utilize some the airgap created by the splayed walls as storage.
You are wasting a lot of room volume with those splayed walls. You only need to splay the front section of the side wall, from the mix position forwards. The rear does not need to be splayed.
Does anyone know of a good way to achieve this without compromising the M-S-M structure?
If you shrink the room enough, you can put a storage area down one side of it, but you still need to retain the MSM integrity.
I’ll splay the side walls 6 degrees each
6° is for flutter echo, but there are other ways of dealing with that. You did mention inside-out walls, so you wouldn't even have a flutter echo problem if you did that. However, you seem to be showing an RFZ design, which does need splayed walls at the front. But they will need to be angled a lot more than 6° to produce a true RFZ. So I'd suggest re-doing the inner-leaf as a proper RFZ with only the front part of the side walls splayed, and make your speaker soffits big enough to do the job properly, while re-positioning them to the locations where they should be. - Stuart -
