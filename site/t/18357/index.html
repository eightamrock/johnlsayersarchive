You seem to be missing a couple of things! :) That said, I'm having a hard time picturing how the room is done as a whole: which wall done how, with what materials, etc. Maybe you could do a clear diagram, showing the details of each wall, and the floor, and the ceiling? But in general, you cannot treat just one or two sides of a room. Isolation is an "all or nothing" proposition. It makes no sense to just isolate one or two sides of the room. Sound is like water: it takes the easiest way out. If you have two good walls, and two bad, it will escape through the bad ones, and ignore the good ones. That's just the way sound is. Also, since this is a drum booth, it needs much more attention to design and construction detail than other rooms, since drums are the hardest instrument of all to isolate anyway, as they are the loudest (by far) and put out a lot of energy in low frequencies (which are the hardest to stop). You probably don't want to hear this, but most likely the only solution that will work, is to take down the existing walls and re-do them properly, as fully-decoupled two-leaf MSM systems, tuned below the lowest frequency of concern.

Soundman202022 May 2013#7
If you are certain that it is ONLY the windows that are the culprit, then definitely that's the place to attack. But from what you say, I'm not so convinced that they are all that is wrong... I suspect that after you fix the windows, you are still going to have issues. Anyway, I can't tell you what to do: I can just tell you what I would do, if that were my place, and I had a very friendly bank manager as well... :) Yes, I would rip out the window plugs, and also take off the "insulation(sheetrock" from the "they are cinder block/normal insulation/Sheetrock" that you mentioned, so you are down to bare cinderblock. Then I would either seal that with a good quality masonry sealant paint, or even better plaster over it (mortar, stucco, whatever you call it), to make sure that it is sealed airtight, and has good mass. Then I would carefully examine that window, to see how thick the glass is, how it is fitted on place, and if it is operable or not. If operable, I would remove the mechanism and block it all tightly in place. Then ether way (operable or not), I would seal around the edges of the glass with good quality flexible caulk, and do the same around the framing, where it meets the walls: That guy has to be totally hermetic, absolutely air-tight. Then, depending on how thick the glass is, and how the window is framed, I would probably end up stuffing a panel of 703 up against the glass, then building a proper plug that hopefully fits into the frame, or if not then on top of it, with rubber seals around the edges, and major mass on it: at least a couple of layers of 3/4" plywood, with Green Glue in between, screwed into the frame and/or into the walls (tapcon?), and carefully sealed with copious amounts of acoustic sealant. You have no choice but to do three-leaf here, but it has to be done the right way: with most of the mass on the middle leaf, and large air gaps, with damping. So that plug needs lots of mass on it. Then I would put up a new stud frame a couple of inches away from all that newly-plastered wall with its newly plugged window, and not touching it at any point! In fact, since this is a drum room, I would probably bid that wall "inside out", with a layer of 3/4" OBS or plywood first (on the studs), then a layer of 5/8" drywall over that, with Green Glue in between, leaving an air cavity of at least 6 inches, and filling it with fiberglass insulation (30 kg/m2 / 2 pcf). Then I would seal that inner-leaf very carefully to the rest of the inner-leaf, making sure that no part of the entire inner-leaf touches any part of the outer leaf. That should do the trick! At least, that would be my approach. - Stuart -
Soundman202022 May 2013#8
Just need to tame down those kick/bass guitar etc freq's that are still transfering from the windows and 2 exterior walls i mentioned.
:) Yup. And those are, indeed, the hardest of all to tame! It's real easy to get 60 or 70 db of isolation up at the top of the spectrum (splash cymbals, for example). A lot harder to get it for the mid range (crash, ride, high toms), and actually impossible to get it for the lows (snare, kick, floor tom). The best you can hope for in the lows is maybe 30 dB of isolation, give or take a few. The lower the frequency, the harder it is. The only way to effectively deal with lows at low cost, is with a two-leaf MSM wall as outlined above, tuned low enough that the resonant frequency is no more than half of the lowest frequency that needs isolating. As outlined above, you should get pretty good isolation in the lows. There's enough mass and air gap to tune it down low. - Stuart -

So it's your call on that: spend a lot of time and do it partly, with mediocre results, or spend even more time and get good results. Only you can make that call, since only you know how badly you need that room to be producing money, albeit not under optimum conditions. So, if you choose to go the "faster, cheaper, but not very effective" route, here's what I would do: kill it with mass. Seal that wall as it is right now with a coat of paint (anything will do: you just want to get it airtight). Seal all cracks too, even if it looks like it might not be a crack, seal it anyway. Air tight seals are key to isolation. Then screw some 3/4" plywood (or OSB, or even better MDF) to that entire wall, and seal all the joints with backer rod and acoustic caulk. Then do a second layer over the first, but offset from it, so the joints between sheets do not line up from layer to layer. You could even rotate the panels: do one layer with the panels standing up vertically, the next with them running across horizontally. Now test: if that is enough extra isolation (it probably won't be) then fine: add your acoustic treatment and you are done! If not, then add a layer of 5/8" drywall (also offset) and test again. That's about as good as it will ever get, with a coupled mass-only solution. Beyond that, you are into diminishing returns. However! Big "but" here: the above assumes that the building structure can handle all that extra weight! You need to check that with a structural engineer: There will be many, many hundreds of pounds of mass in that extra stuff, so you must make certain that the structure can handle it safely. And as I implied at the start: this probably won't solve your problem entirely, but it might take the edge off enough to be workable. I would also build a drum riser for your drums: in cases like this, it is essential to get as much isolation between the drums and the building as possible. A simple drum riser will do that, and it won't break the bank: two panels of 2" OC-703 laid flat on the floor side by side (to make an 8' x 8' base), and two sheets of 3/4" plywood on top (also side by side, but rotated 90°) then another two sheets of 3/4" play on top of those, rotated 90° again, screwed and glued. Done! Simple, cheap, effective.

As far as OSB or sheetrock...would you personally do sheetrock/sheetrock or OSB/sheetrock
Personally, I would always do OSB + sheetrock, for several reasons, the most important of which are: 1) OSB is structural: it provides strength to your wall in "sheer", which drywall cannot do (drywall is pretty lousy, structurally). 2) It gives you a nailing surface around the entire room, so you can hang any acoustic device in any location without needing to search for studs..
all i can find at home depot here in OSB is 7/16 and 1/2" . either of those work or strictly 3/4?
If you can't find 3/4", then 5/8" is fine. And if your local Home Depot doesn't have that, then try some place else that specializes in supplying building materials.
for the top and bottom of the newly framed walls we will be putting up, how do i tie them into the floor/celing?
Floor: Just bolt, screw or tapcon, as required by code, in the normal way. The only "special" thing you need to do, is to put three beads of acoustic sealant along the sole plates before you put them down on the floor, to get a good seal. One bead goes right down the middle of the 2x4, then another bead about an inch to each side. Then seal again under each layer of OSB / drywall / whatever after it is nailed in place: one seal for each layer. Did I mention that seals are critical? :) Ceiling: Hmmmm... you can't tie into a drop ceiling, and a drop ceiling is no use for isolation in any case! This is probably also part of your problem with lack of isolation. The NORMAL way of doing a ceiling for a drum booth (or any other place where you need high levels of isolation) is to put joists across the new inner-leaf walls (all four of them), and hang drywall from that, such that this ceiling only touches the inner-leaf walls, nothing else. - Stuart -
