jwl12 April 2006#8
Hi Audiotracker, What I'd investigate is something like this: float the floor of the booth using an appropriate amount of rubber and/or neoprene pucks (I don't know which ones are better, others on this forum do), with your floor joists (probably 2x4s to maximize the inner height of the booth), putting rockwool between the floor joists, and then beef the floor up with a few layers of MDF or plywood. I'd build the walls of the booth up from the floor, and cover the OUTSIDE ONLY of the walls with a couple-three layers of sheetrock, possibly adding green blue between the layers if you have the budget and want the extra isolation. On the inside of the booth, I'd line between the joists with some sort of insulation, the rockwool you mention would probably work fine, and then cover it with cloth. This way you can finish the outside of the booth in sheetrock, and the inside of the booth will have some absorption so it will probably sound better. I'd probably build the ceiling similarly to the walls, with absorptive insulation on the inside and sheetrock on the outside. I'd install a nice hollow-core door that's been beefed up with MDF. Note that this idea is definitely big-picture, and the devil is in the details, as they say. There are people on this forum MUCH more experienced than I who can help you in the details, ie, what kind of pucks and how many, etc. But again, Sharward is right; you'll have to plan very carefully for weight. You'll need to know how much the entire booth will weigh so that you can a) make sure the concrete can support it, and b) use the correct number and spacing with the pucks. And while Sharward's warnings represent probably a worst-case scenario, you should definitely think about this stuff. Have a structural engineer double-check it. If you did this carefully, you might even be able to come up with some sort of modular design, so that it can be disassembled and moved in the future if need be, though the more mass it is the better it will isolate. If it's light enough to be truly portable, you probably won't get much isolation from it.

sharward13 April 2006#14
Honestly, the amount of weight you can add to an existing upper floor is probably going to be minimal, based on the fact that home builders (and condominium builders) and architects tend to build based on minimum requirements to meet code. Learn about dead load vs. live load. Code typically calls for 30 or 40 pounds per square feet (denoted as "psf") dead load. Builders are typically going to use the cheapest materails to achieve that. This means if they can get by using 2x6s instead of 2x8s, they will, and that will leave you with negligible wiggle room to add a lot of extra dead load. Roofers go through this all the time. You can't keep adding layers of composition shingle over and over onto the top of a house. Eventually layers will have to be removed, else the roof will surely cave in. You need a structural engineer to tell you how much dead load weight you can safely add to your structure. Period. You need one. Once you have the answer to that question, you then have an upper limit in terms of weight, and then you'll be able to figure out how much mass you can afford to use in your solution. This forum (and a couple of others) can give you good feedback on how well that solution will perform for you. Then you can decide if it's worth the effort. Otherwise, we're "guessing" how heavy it can be not to kill anyone, and we're "guessing" how heavy it has to be to achieve your isolation requirements. Think of the total weight allowance as a budget -- not a monetary one, but a laws of physics one. Find out what you can afford (weightwise), then you can make a plan for what you want to buy (build). Imagine shopping for a car and not having any idea how much money you can afford to spend......... :roll: A couple of other points... Yes, not killing anyone is #1 priority, but coming home from work one day and finding your upper floor on your lower floor, even if there are no injuries, would be disasterous beyond description. And don't think that you'd see it coming -- catastrophes like that don't send warning signals... They happen faster than you can say "boo." --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward14 April 2006#17
By the way, I looked at the noise reduction specifications for the Enhanced version of the WhisperRoom (assuming that is the manufacturer you were considering)... 
Frequency ( Hertz ):	125   250   500   1000   2000   4000
Reduction ( dB ):	   30    44    53      61     67     68
What this means in plain English is that your isolation at 125 Hz is about 30 decibels. You can compare those numbers with the hundreds of different wall assembly configurations tested by the Institute for Research in Construction. You'll notice that the whereas WhisperRoom publishes six frequencies between 125 Hz and 4,000 Hz, the IRC publishes 22 frequencies between 50 Hz and 6,300 Hz. 125 to 4000 is the range for speech. The manufacturer is only publishing specs for the speech range. This should concern anyone intending to use the rooms for isolating drums. Also, the weight of their 102102E model (8'8" x 8'8") weighs about 2985 pounds, which is about 40 pounds per square foot. That's about the weight of an empty compact pickup truck and is heavier than the "drywall only" numbers I posted yesterday. Therefore, my warnings about making sure you don't overload your floor stand true even if you decide to purchase a WhisperRoom system. These "whisper rooms" do have the apparent benefit of modularity and semi portability (good for semiperminent implementations), but they may be lacking in their performance compared to the low tech, semi-conventional methods that are most often discussed on this forum and described in the IRC document. --Keith :mrgreen:
