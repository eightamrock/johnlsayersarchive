I am trying to improve the layout and acoustics of a little basement studio in my home in Chicago. The room is below grade, carpeted wall to wall, and pretty solid-feeling due to the fact that two walls are heavy exterior foundation. The building itself is brick. With few exceptions, the recording setup is just me sitting in a chair with an acoustic guitar and two or three mikes in front of me. Most of what I do is solo fingerstyle guitar, which I usually mike with a pair of small-diaphragm condensors (Oktava MK-012s) and/or a single large-diaphragm condensor (ADK A51TC). One of my guitars is a jumbo Goodall with a big throaty sound and powerful bass response. Now that I have invested in a good preamp and some really nice mikes, I am concerned to get the best acoustics out of my room that I can. I have tried lots of mike placements etc. but the result is too often brittle sounding and lacking in depth. I also get a low-grade hiss which I suspect might be stray high-freq activity bouncing around the room. There is no issue with keeping my sound in; I'm quiet and the neighbors are out of range. It's all about keeping outside sounds out and improving the acoustic behavior of the space. My budget is flexible but I would like to see how close I can get to a professional sound for < $1,000. Got the gear, now need the right space. Here's the current setup such as it is:

Particular acoustic challenges I am aware of include: 1. The usual issues of hard walls reflecting sound artifacts back and forth, standing waves, faint high-freq hiss, etc. - it's a small room so this is all pretty evident. 2. Two windows, one of them a glass block affair opening onto a gangway (not a real acoustic issue), the other a small window that opens just below ground level onto a sidewalk, next to which is a street, next to which is an embankment for some train tracks. Uh-huh, I said train tracks. Great thing to have outside your studio window! Fortunately the trains are not frequent and I know the schedule, so rather than try and soundproof them out of the picture I just wait for them to pass and go on with my business. There is, however, random people and traffic noise coming through that window at unpredictable moments. 3. A large overhead HVAC duct enclosed in a dropped portion of the ceiling. There are two overhead vents set into the duct. As with the train, my approach has been to just turn the heat or AC off rather than try and "correct" this sound source. 4. A double door that opens into a den area. This door is not sound insulated, but on the other hand no one is usually downstairs when I am recording, so the noise bleed is not that great. I do keep my recording PC outside the room with a Firewire cable connection to the gear inside the room, so there is no computer noise issue. 5. Low ceiling.... So I guess the main thing I am concerned about is sweetening the tone of the guitar by correcting standing waves and unwanted reflections. I would also like to nail that high-freq hiss. Some additional localized soundproofing would be good too, but short of building an iso booth I'm not sure how much I gain from that (apart from the obvious move of blocking the windows). WHAT I'VE DONE SO FAR: I've built two portable baffles in the shape of 4' high hinged panels covered in Auralex; picture a shoji-screen affair with two 2' x 4' panels hinged together. These help with reflections for sure. One question I have is where to optimally position them and whether I need to supplement this with some more permanent treatment of the room corners etc. I have no bass traps per se. My specific questions: 1. Where should I position myself, my mikes and my gear given the configuration of this room? 2. Where should I ideally position my two baffled "shojis?" My original idea was one in front of me, some distance behind the mike array, and one immediately behind my back to catch any stray reflections not killed by the front baffle. But I am unclear on how far in front of me the one trap should be in order to allow optimal breathing room around my cardioid mikes. 3. Do I need to install bass traps in the corners or elsewhere, or would correct placement of the baffle screens neutralize bass issues? 4. Is 4' high enough for the traps to cover me if I am in a sitting position? 5. Anything I need to do about that ceiling? 6. Any thoughts on where that hiss is coming from? It isn't my gear, I am pretty convinced. I think it's the room. Many, many thanks in advance for whatever guidance you all can lend.

From the SAE website (Acoustics/Absorption page):
For an instrument to sound good you must hear a balance of its high and low frequencies. If your room is too dead in the high frequencies the instrument sounds lifeless and tubby, whereas if it's too live in the high frequencies the instrument sounds distant and has no presence. To balance a room you must have a true balance of the high frequencies and the low frequencies with the right amount of deadness to liveness.
and:
the major problem experienced by studios who treat their rooms by putting drapes, carpet or egg cartons on the walls. Basically they are lowering the reflections (reverberation time) of the room BUT ONLY MARGINALLY IN THE HIGH FREQUENCY RANGE! The lows are still humming around the room and the resultant reverberation field creates what we call a muddy - woofy sound. The solution is always to add highs to the recorded signal to return the top end yet really what needs to happen is the low end (100 - 1000Hz) reverberation time needs to be lowered in the recording room and probably in the control room for you to even hear the difference.
