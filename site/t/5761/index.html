guitardad724 March 2006#6
My 2 cents: I/we just bought house 2 ½ years ago. I had some concern about studio area and thought “what ever” I’ll deal with that when it comes. Now that I am familiar with sound isolation techniques: 1. I would have tried to buy house with as large of an unfinished area as possible whether it be a 3 car garage or unfinished basement. 2. I would go for solid poured concrete (foundation) walls over hollow concrete block. Hollow CB walls need cavities filled to avoid triple leaf sound isolation loss. 3. I would read through this entire site and others like it to determine my isolation and mixing needs. 4. Make sure electric service panel other appliances and windows doors not in the way of studio. 5. Make sure electric service is 150 to 200 amps and that panel has spare spaces for new breakers. 6. Look into what’s above unfinished (or finished) area with regards to load weight of Truss or rafters. You should have 2 ceilings in the end 7. I am probably missing a few things. 8. Oh yeah Load Bearing Walls…. if there is a wall which looks liek you can knock down, determine 1st if it is load bearing or not… sometimes it’s not an easy answer. 9. In the end I would give into my wife’s opinion on which house to buy anyways. Be careful when choosing a finished basement especially if it was done by previous owner (maybe with out a permit) or if it was finished before 1980… look into asbestos and other dangerous chemicals. I’ve posted in the safety thread in construction forum with info. guitardad726 March 2006#8
1st take a brief look at this thread taking note of diagram with STC values on various wall types & Kendale’s post: [url=http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?p=39618]Better Soundproofing of existing walls [/url] If you haven’t already please make sure you read through the reference section to understand all the terms used in this forum: http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=2125 Also: http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html My simple minded way of explaining three leaf isolation loss, using a window as an example. A single pane window provides X amount of sound isolation, this is just mass OR one leaf. Double pane window provides better isolation; this is mass-air-mass OR leaf, air, leaf. You would think a triple pane window would provide more sound isolation then a double pane window, but it does not. This is mass-air-mass-air-mass.... this is an example of triple leaf, what should be avoided for sound isolation...but with acoustic absorbers and obtsicles in existing conditions it is sometimes created... then you live with the loss. I’ve done a brief search on this site trying to find a better answer for you. I’ve just settled to the understanding this is the way it is… avoid triple leaf and make everything Mass-Air-Mass. AND from Kightfly, a post on my thread:
For a classic example of this (triple leaf) effect, put a loud boombox in another room, separated from you by concrete block (hollow) - Hopefully this room has a door for more isolation from the room YOU are in - pick up a 4x4 foot section of plywood or gypsum, hold it in front of you like you're gonna hang a picture on the intervening wall, and slowly walk toward this wall with the board in front of you - note which frequencies change in loudness as you get closer, right up til you actually TOUCH the block wall with the 4x4 board.
I've not done this test yet but am planning to just for the hell of it so that I can get a real life example of triple leaf. Please let me know if this helps as it's the 1st time I've tried to explain it in my own words on this forum. Marc
