Resonating wall.

Started by url on 16 September 2007. 4 replies.

Originally posted at johnlsayers.com, topic 9420.

url16 September 2007#1
Hi, i'm looking for some advice on a problem I have. I have renovated a small studio I have access to, a small room roughly 4m x 5m, it previously consisted of a wooden frame with fibre glass covered in cloth. I replaced that by fixing the frame, creating more panels, filling with fibre glass and insulation batts and covering with yellow tongue flooring (19mm). With the idea of using that as a firm backing for adding extra treatment. This was done in summer and y'know, did the job. As it goes, come winter the building sounds pretty noisy and the studio is droning. It turns out the central heating for the building has been turned on and a pipe from that which feeds my side of the building comes into one of the studio walls. The walls are double brick with say a 2 inch gap but the wall is vibrating, what's more it has created sympathetic resonance and there is a bloom or flame that extends to at least the middle of the room. It's really full on. I'm not sure what to do about it. I didn't notice it so much when the studio was "cloth" so I guess the renovation caused the problem, the frame is attached to the wall and the yellow tongue is screwed and nailed to that and the cavity is pretty packed. I'm wondering what I need to do to at least puncture the sympathetic resonance. Should i cut holes in the offending panels to release the tension? A friend suggested adding mass to the wall. I'd very much appreciate some advice. thanks, simon

Hi Eric, thanks for considering the post, sorry it isn't very clear. Yes, the concept of "flame" is influenced by terminology used in "Mixing with your mind." Essentially, the heating pipe is causing the wall to vibrate. Not only is it vibrating it is singing a low frequency hum, like a drum resonance except that it is constant. By "flame" I mean this. If I was to walk across the room with the offending wall on my right, I would start with the resonant sound from the wall being barely perceptible, as I walk across the room the sound of the hum increases in volume until it reaches a maximum near the middle of the room, then it starts to subside as I reach the other end of the room. Basically there is a low frequency vibration that extends into the room in the shape of a bell curve.
Eric_Desart17 September 2007#5
Simon, At a first glance that sounds as if this resonance coincides with the first even order axial mode. But that's guessing. If you use wood on that wall, that's still not clear (you really should make a sketch with exact measures, cavities and so on from all this or this becomes endless), it can be a mass spring resonance. Basically you either need to decouple that wall (can go further than that wall, since that wall can be excited by other walls or floor) from that equipment (or the way around). I can't judge from here what's needed to do so. The only alternative is to isolate your studio from that brick structure, and taking into account that you're speaking LOW frequencies, that calls for care in function of MSM as well. The normal basic rule with structural born sound is decoupling, meaning preventing the phenomenon to establish (primary solution). Normally only when that proves impossible or impractical one goes to secondary solutions. I can't judge from here how that wall excites your other stuff and construction.
