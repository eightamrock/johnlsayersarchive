Hi, and welcome. There is an announcement at the top of the forum about what to do to assure getting as many responses as possible. The announcement leads to this post (click here). Actually, several people, who are experts on this forum, will not reply if you don't do what is written in that post. Many others who are very helpful, will not reply out of respect for the moderators' wishes. That said, I see several problems with your layout, and have some comments on your questions: 1) Symmetry: Your mix position must be located on the center line of the room, and the room must be symmetrical about that center line, at least for the area in front of the mix position (between the front wall and your head). Behind that it isn't so important, but in front of that it is absolutely necessary. If not, you will never be able to do a great mix that translates well. You did not do your room in SketchUp yet, and your diagrams are not to scale, so it really hard to say what you need to do to fix that, but most likely your mix position will have to face the "sliding window" in order to get ceiling symmetry. Model your room accurately in SketchUp and post it here. 2) Speakers: They should fire down the longest dimension of the room, not across the short dimension, nor diagonally. In your case, that is not going to be possible, since the only arrangement that makes sense symmetrically is to face the sliding window. But I'd say that symmetry is more important in this case, so you'll have to live with speakers firing down the short axis. 3) Geometry: The speakers should be angled such that the acoustic axes intersect at 60° about 15 cm behind your head, and your head should be about 38% of the distance from the front wall to the back wall. The speakers should be set at the same height above the floor as your ears are when you are sitting at the mix position, which is normally around 1.2 m above the floor. (That's the height of the acoustic axis of the speakers that I'm talking about, not the height of the speaker top or bottom). 4) First Reflection Points: You say:
"I understand that the best way to approach this is to sit in front of the DAW and have someone put a mirror against the walls. Then put the accoustic foam on all spots where you see your speakers."
True, but that is only for your first reflection points. You are going to need a LOT more treatment than just that! The treatment at the first reflection points needs to be about 2 inches deep at least (depending on what it is!), and needs to be a bit larger than the zones where you can see the speakers in the mirror. And don't forget the ceiling! There is a first reflection point up there too.
Because of the sloped ceiling I’m not sure if I should place accoustic material on there as well.
Yes. If you can see the speakers in the mirror, then that is a reflection point, regardless of the angle. Treat that point. That's why you use a mirror! It automatically takes into account angles. If light bounces off the mirror to your eyes at that angle, then high frequency sound will follow the same path. So if you can see it, treat it. 5) Bass traps:
Also not sure if I should look into getting bass traps and where should I place these then?
Yes, yes, yes and YES! It's a small room: therefore it WILL need bass trapping, and lots of it. Bass taps work best in corners. so use every corner that you can, and make each trap large and deep. The best bass traps are called "superhcunks" and you make them yourself by staking up mineral wool or semi-rigid fiberglass in the corners. That might not be so easy in your case, due to the angled walls, but you can build some kind of frame to keep it in place. Search the forum for "superchunk" to find how to build them. Second best is large sheets of thick (4" at least) semi-rigid fiberglass, such as OC 703, placed diagonally across the corners. All of the corners. There are 12 corners in a normal rectangular room, but you have more in your room. Treat as many as you possibly can. If you don't have good bass trapping in your room you will never get close to having even room response in the low end, so your bass will be muddy, blurry and highly variable around the room. You will not be able to do a decent mix that translates well. You might be able to do a mix that sounds good with your head held still at the mix position, but if you play that mix in any other room it will sound lousy. It won't work. If you find that all that bass trapping takes out too much of your high end, then you can wrap the bass trapping in plastic to keep the highs in the room. 6) The "fireplace tunnel". That is a large flat hard reflective surface right behind your head and speakers. It will need to be treated with lots of absorption. 7) The Window: That's another large flat hard reflective surface, and it will be right behind your speakers, where it will be creating all kinds of comb filtering and phasing artifacts. That will need treatment too, at the very least with some kind of absorptive panels placed up against the glass, right behind the speakers. 8) Floor: You didn't mention the floor, but since that looks like an attic space I'm guessing that the floor is just wood. If so, leave it like that: Don't bother with carpet or anything else until you have completed the rest of the room and tested it properly, to see where your remaining problems lie. If you still have mid - high issues then you can use some of the floor area to treat that, perhaps with throw rugs, carpet, or some such. 9) Also floor: If it is a typical wooden attic floor then it is basically a large drum head that will resonate at certain frequencies. You need to do your best to stop direct transmission of vibration into the floor, or it will sing along with you! So get or make some really heavy speaker stands, such as with concrete blocks stacked up, or hollow steel filled with dry sand, or some such, then isolate your speakers from those too, with foam, rubber, or some such. Auralex sells special pads for this exact purpose. Also isolate anything else that makes a noise, such as guitar amps, drums, etc. If you have acoustic drums, build an isolated riser to help minimize impact noise getting into the floor. 10) You didn't mention isolation, so I guess your family and neighbors really love listening to you playing and mixing? And also that you really don't mind outside sounds getting in to your recordings? I sure hope that is the case, because since that is an attic, isolating it effectively is not going to be easy or cheap. That's the obvious things that spring to mind. I'd suggest that you remodel your room accurately in SketchUp, take into account the above to re-do your layout, then post again to see of there are more issues that need t be taken care of. And don't forget to FIRST OF ALL read the post that I linked you to at the top. - Stuart -
