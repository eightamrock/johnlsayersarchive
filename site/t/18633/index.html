If it might eventually be a fully commercial studio with paying customers, you should probably take into account the legal requirements for people with disabilities, such as ramps instead of steps, wider corridors and doors, wheelchair accessible bathroom, and suchlike. My budget for materials for the whole project is about $5-6,000 USD, and I'm hiring a contractor to do most of the labor.
Sorry to sound harsh, but your budget is on the low side, and unrealistically so. You have something like 900 square feet there, so you are budgeting just US$ 6 per square foot for materials. I would guess you'd need about 4 or 5 times that amount for materials. Just the HVAC system alone is going to eat up half of your budget, more or less, and your doors and windows are going to eat up the other half. Figure the drywall cost: you have about 130 linear feet around your perimeter, meaning 33 full sheets of drywall, but you are doubling up on that for the outer leaf (correctly) and then doubling again for the inner leaf, so you need 132 sheets of 5/8 fire-rated drywall. Plus another 56 sheets for your ceilings, and allow 10% for wastage: total 206 sheets. At around 10 bucks per 4' x 8' sheet, there goes 2 grand, just in drywall. Then add acoustic insulation, studs, nails, screws, hundreds of tubes of acoustic caulk, electrical materials, roofing materials, trusses, the slab, flooring (800 sq.ft of laminate is at least another US$ 2,500), inspection fees, etc. And that doesn't even consider the labor costs, which can easily be as much as the materials, if you have to hire everyone from several disciplines framers, drywallers, electricians, HVAC installers, roofers, painters, etc. Then comes your acoustic treatment, which can easily cost another several thousand dollars. You are way under budgeting. To get a more realistic budget, call a few contractors in your area and ask for their flat rate per square foot for building a detached garage or home theater. Add about 25% to that, and that will give you a good idea of where your budget should be. I'm not trying to be a wet blanket here, but trying to do this without having the right budget in place is only going to lead to major disappointment. I would suggest re-thinking your budget, or planning to do this ins several stages, as more funds become available in the future. For example, just build the Control Room for now, then do the live room later when you can afford it, then the booth and other rooms further down the line. Etc.
Right! That's about the minimum volume for a "critical listening room", according ITU and EBU specs. It would be worthwhile sacrificing some of the live room to get a bit more space in the CR.
- Stuart -

My plan is to put laminate flooring in the live room and the isolation booth, and the brand that I will use is what I put in my house, which is about two dollars per square foot so I'm probably looking more at $1000.
Don't forget the underlay! That could cost a few hundred dollars more.
I haven't decided about the control room yet, I was thinking some cheap carpet but some people have frowned at that.
Add me tho the list of people who frowned on it! :) They are all correct. Carpet is not much use acoustically, and in fact it does the exact opposite of what you need. It absorbs high frequencies very well, mids somewhat, and lows not at all. You need to absorb massive amounts of low, some mids, and no highs. So installing carpet is fighting against what you should be doing. Forget that: Bare concrete is actually a great floor surface for a studio, and if you look around the forum you'll see some really cool things people have done with that, such as staining it artistically, polishing it, etc. It can look great if done well. If yours is in poor condition (cracked, chipped, uneven, rough, etc.) then that might not be an option, or also if you just don't like the look of concrete! In that case laminate flooring is good, and so are ceramic tiles, linoleum, and other similar hard, reflective surfaces.
One additional question: as I mentioned, the current exterior wall of the building is corrugated metal. Would it be worth putting a layer of sheet rock on the inside of that wall or would that violate the mass–air–mass system that I am using for the double stud interior wall?
With a tin outer wall you probably don't have much choice: you'll probably need to build a three-leaf system, which isn't optimal for low frequency isolation, but is your only real option. The tine will be your outer leaf. So just pretend the tin isn't there, and build the studio as a proper fully-decoupled two-leaf MSM system next to that. You can do your "middle leaf" on the same framing as the tin, if it can handle the weight, or on separate framing if the structural integrity is not good enough for that. Then each room consists of just its own "inner-leaf", also on separate framing. So there's no need to beef up the tin, although sealing it to prevent rattles would be a good way to start. - Stuart -
