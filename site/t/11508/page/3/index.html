Remember, you aren't framing simple interior partitions. All of your walls will be bearing the load of the ceiling structure. A single flat 2x4 above a door isn't going to cut it. At a minimum I think a header made of two 2x6s sandwiching some 1/2" plywood would be in order. It would stand upright like a joist. The window opening would be at least a 2x8 sandwich, probably 2x10s. Your jack and cripple studs are fine. Don't get discouraged, all of this can be fixed. The Southern Pine Span Tables reads that No. 2, 2X4 with a 12 inch layout would at least get you in the neighborhood of 12 feet worth of span, from outside to outside of walls. Drywall ceiling, no attic space, 10 psf live and >>> 5<<< psf dead load. 2X6 would be far better. Using the same 12 inch layout you would get a span of 15'-6" with an increased 10 psf deadload. A 16 inch layout would shorten your span to 13'-6" same load. Your headers will be OK as this isn't going to have to withstand any wind or loading due to natural elements. But if you had it to do again, a larger header adds mass and directly effects the lateral stability of the wall.

rod gervais13 March 2009#70
Dead loads are the loads of the materials that make up the structure and/or building components. Examples: Studs, ceiling joists - headers, sheathing (plywood - OSB - Drywall, etc.) Duckwork, fans, fan coil units, electrical within cavities or permanately mouted to wall/ceiling/floor, electrical lighting, plumbing pipe (waste and domestic hot water) sprinkler piping, etc., etc., etc. Live loads are things brought in after the room/space is complete. Examples: Furniture, gear, people walking around, etc., etc., etc. To calculate the ceiling you would take the entire weight of all of the ceiling components (including the ceiling joist) all plywod/drywall layers, any insulation, lighting, ductwork with registers/grilles, and divide those by the area of the ceiling for a total psf DL. Tis just that simple. Rod
