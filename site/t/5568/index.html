knightfly3 February 2006#2
1. Is there any benefit to spacing out the triangles, other than cost-savings? Wouldn't this provide more surface area? Say, 4 triangles per 4' panel? Or maybe leave 4" gaps between triangles (6 triangles per 4' panel)? I don't think anyone's ever done that and reported back; but for LF absorption, it would LESSEN the effect since only half the face would be absorbent to lows. 2. I've learned from this forum that denser material absorbs more bass, but may reflect highs, so what if I used 8 lb. for the triangles and 4 lb. for the face? 4 lbs is already kinda high for any but direct incidence - I'd stick with straight 4 lb if that's your two choices. 3. Is it o.k. to glue the face directly to the triangles? I read somewhere that it should not touch the insulation behind it, but that was involving a sealed slat/slot thing. Only problem with glue would be if it's a continuous (or even majority) layer when dry; then it would form a membrane and restrict mid/high absorption. 4. Since I don't wish to glue anything directly to the walls and ceiling, I plan on attaching 1" X 2" firing strips length-wise (perpendicular to the triangles), one strip for each of the equilateral sides, thereby providing one contact point on each side, and placing the triangles 1" away from the wall and ceiling. Is this 1" helpful? Unlikely to be noticeable - consider its magnitude compared to the rest of the absorber. 5. And lastly (for now), after all is said and done, is THIS better than any of the above? (Using just fabric instead of slats, and using 4 lb. on the face and 8 lb. behind that, leaving a 2" or 3" air gap?) Not that much difference - placement would be the same, you're only using straight absorption instead of some helmholtz and some absorption. The main difference would be that using slats will keep the room brighter. IF that's what you want, try wrapping your 4 lb stuff in light painters plastic dropcloths (about 1/2 mil or so) - this will absorb less highs. Not exactly the SAME result as slats, but similar... Steve

knightfly14 February 2006#7
1. So, does this 4" SAFB sound like a good choice? Yup. 2. Are traps only necessary where the back wall meets the ceiling, and the 2 rear vertical corners? Are traps really necessary where the side walls meet the ceiling? And how about where the front wall meets the ceiling, and the 2 front vertical corners? Smaller the room, the worse your modes will treat you. Yours has two nearly identical dimensions, so will need more treatment to get closer to flat response. A start for ALL smaller rooms is doing all vertical corners, front behind speakers, first reflection points on side walls and ceiling, and heavy absorption to the rear of the mix position. Wall-ceiling corners are next if this doesn't finish the job of flat response. 3. And one last alternate method: What about tightly rolling up a 2 X 4 sheet, wrapping in plastic, and hanging in the corner with straps, then covering with a face made of another 2 X 4 panel? Best is a complete fill, ala Superchunks; next best would be a second thickness right behind the diagonal outer layer of absorption. Last would be a single layer of 4" - less than that and you may or may not even hear the difference. 4. Or is filling the corners with cut triangles (no gaps) the best way to go? OR... is merely mounting a panel across the corner really good enough? Is filling in the corners just overkill? See above. 5. And considering the dimensions of the room, do you guys REALLY think it will make a big difference if I position myself lengthwise? Really??? After all, I'm going to put 4" panels on the rear wall as well. I'll do it if you guys really feel strongly about it. But I'd really rather not. Yes, I do; for one thing, the narrow dimension is likely to put you exactly centered between walls, in an UNNECESSARY null/peak area. Rotating 90 degrees solves that problem. It also creates another one, that of the window; you will probably need a movable absorber for the window during mixdowns. The only null/peak zone you have little choice in is the left/right one - anything else and stereo imaging goes funky. Other than that, you should NOT be in either peak or null positions for any harmonic of any room mode. I can see why you're hesitant, for one thing the window; for another, the couch would need to be forward enough to clear the door. The good news is, this will probably put the couch in a better spot for listening than against the wall anyway - less bass perhaps, but better overall balance of sound... Steve

knightfly7 March 2006#18
Got your PM - My question still stands. Is it OK to just face the traps with plastic? You can; or you can put the fibrex into garbage bags (not the expensive, thick ones) - idea is to contain the fibers AND brighten the room a bit by reducing high frequency absorption. If you stay THIN, like 1/2 mil or so (painter's plastic drop cloths, for example) you won't affect lows or mids while doing this. Bottom line - complete wrap with ONE layer of thin plastic won't hurt anything, and will better contain any loose fibers. Acoustically, once you've covered the front (and sides) with thin plastic, doing the back won't make any difference (acoustically, that is) And framing the traps with 3" pine won't cause a significant reflection problem, will it? Actually, YES. If you frame them so they look "Nice", you're covering up their edges - this replaces absorption with reflections/diffractions. If you do that (the frames) I'd recommend larger surface areas (maybe a foot more in either direction) to compensate for the loss of edge absorption, AND to move any reflections further away from the mix position - this should do two things - one, it will help direct any reflections elsewhere, and two, any acoustic disturbance caused by the frame will have a longer path back to the mix position so will DIFFUSE a bit more. Also, I will be putting a cloud over almost the entire ceiling. Is it really necessary at all to put traps in the wall/ceiling corners on the sides? Where your door is, and that SILL running along the one wall, I would think it'd be hard to do all four VERTICAL corners; so you probably WILL need to do at least the left and right sides' wall/ceiling corners. I'd do the stuff you KNOW you're gonna do first; then listen and TEST - you'll know then if you have enough absorption. My guess is NOT, but every space is just enough different to make me a liar... knightfly31 March 2006#20
Curving the pegboard makes it a polycylindrical absorber - NOT having insulation up against the curve makes it very narrow band (not good) - and the standard available types of pegboard are kind of limited as to frequency - one example calculation puts 1/8" pegboard, 1/8" holes on 1" centers, spaced off the wall by 16", at a frequency of around 135 hZ. Here's the formula - http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/download.php?id=6680 Note that this is for panels with SUBDIVIDED air space - this means that in order for this formula to be accurate, each hole must have a divider around it. Not fun to build. I've yet to see a formula for NON-divided air spaces, I'm inclined to think (lacking evidence to the contrary) that the reason the corner slat absorbers work without figuring this in is that they are a VARIABLE air gap behind. Keep in mind that I'm NOT even close to being an acoustician and have not built and tested bunches of these with different parameters and actually MEASURED the results - all I know is that John's been using them for years with good success... Bottom line here - for your small room, I would NOT get into tuned traps, with the possible exception of a couple panel traps tuned to 60 hZ and below, which are the only areas your room dimensions may show problems at. But the first thing to do is ALWAYS broadband absorption, and usually that's ALL you need unless the room has unusually bad dimensions... Steve

knightfly31 March 2006#22
Where they'd go depends on which room mode is causing the problem; panel traps work on sound PRESSURE, not velocity; so they need to be at pressure peaks - one basic axial mode calculator that gives a good visual is the one at harman's site, linked in this forum about 4th from the top of the page - it shows a quasi-sine wave of sound pressure for each of 4 harmonics of each dimension of your room, so you can tell where the axial modes peak and where they're nulled in pressure. Generally, if you have a mode that's caused by the length of a room, there are two approaches - since the two short walls will be at high pressure for ALL modes, you could place the traps on those walls (although modes for width may interfere, this gets trickier) - or you could place them along the LONG wall, at different percentages of the wall's length depending on which harmonic you're trying to treat. If you download the calculator and open it (requires Excel on your computer) you'll see that First harmonics ONLY peak at the ends; so traps for this should be in a corner, preferably on the short wall for long wall mode and vice versa. The second harmonic (black graph) could be placed the same, OR it could be placed at the center of the LONG wall (assuming it's the LONG wall's mode you're treating, and at the second harmonic frequency) - this is because the graph shows another PEAK at the center of the dimension. Third harmonics (shown in red) are seen in the graph to peak at 1/3 and 2/3 of each dimension, so a frequency that coincides with a third harmonic would need its panel trap (tuned to that frequency) placed on that wall's 1/3 and/or 2/3 points (IOW, where the wave will peak in pressure) Fourth harmonics (yellow) peak at every 1/4 of the dimension they're caused by, so could be placed at any 1/4 or 1/2 of their corresponding wall dimension. More, including the "MC Hammer" formula, here http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?p=41713 Hope I didn't just make things MORE confusing... Steve

knightfly1 April 2006#24
All good ideas; for one thing, even looking at the other two types of modes your room isn't too bad; just a couple of lower frequency things that MAY cause some lack of SPL at 60 hZ and below. For ANY room, the first thing to do is broadband absorption - in a large percentage of cases, this will be all that's necessary to smooth out the room response. When that's NOT the case, this is when tuned traps may be necessary. Where you locate them is decided by which harmonic is causing the problem; and if you tune a panel trap to 4th harmonic and place it where second and fourth harmonics peak, it's tuned to the 4th so won't affect the second harmonic AS MUCH as it does the frequency it's tuned to. The thing you FIRST need to do, before you can FIX something, is to MEASURE it; without that, you have no idea if anything's even BROKEN. For that, there's help too; check this out http://homepage.ntlworld.com/john.mulcahy/roomeq/ HTH... Steve
