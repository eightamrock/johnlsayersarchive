(The text is kind of ambiguous). So your thread can stay here. and we assume you WILL build it one day, so no problem! Anyway, about the questions:
sound bleed into the storage space and upstairs is not an issue
It doesn't matter: you have to do the same on all five sides of the room (4 walls, 1 ceiling). Sound is like cold air: It doesn't matter if you only leave one window open on your car when it is freezing cold outside, the entire car still gets cold inside. If you only do four sides of an MSM system, then you don't have ANY MSM system at all. Sound will simply leak around the other side. So it's not an option to leave one side incomplete, or only partially isolated, or isolated to a lower level. Once the sound gets through, it is through all over. You have to do all five sides to the same level.
would i need to double stud out EVERY wall in the basement to achieve a say STC 68 (according to auralex) from my live room (left side) to my control room?
Hang on a sec! You say that you already have an outer leaf, which is concrete, and that you have 2x6 stud frames next to that. Why do you want to "double-stud"? What does that mean? All that you need in order to isolate effective is a second leaf that is not coupled in any manner to the existing concrete walls, which is your outer leaf. If your existing 2x6 stud frame is NOT touching the existing walls or ceiling, then you are all set. Just put a couple of layers of 16mm drywall on that frame, for your second leaf, and you are done. However, if the 2x6 frame IS touching the walls and/or ceiling, then you need to take it down, move it over a bit, and re-build it so that it no longer touches anything.
would I be ok double studding with resilient channel
If you have a decoupled stud frame, then you do not need resilient channel (RC). The one and only purpose of RC is to decouple drywall from walls that are not already decoupled. Yours will be. You cannot "decouple" twice: once you are decoupled (by having a separate frame) then you are decoupled! No RC necessary. Save yourself some money!
,5/8" + 1/2" drywall
A myth. Mixing different thickness drywall buys you practically nothing in terms of isolation, and can actually cost you isolation. The theory is that the different surface densities help to flatten out the coincidence dip of the wall, but if you build the wall right the coincidence dip is so well isolated already that it doesn't matter, and it is in a part of the spectrum that isn't a problem anyway! On the other hand, by using then drywall (1/2") instead of thick (5/8") for the second layer, you have robbed yourself of mass, which you DO need for the low end, where the REAL problems lie. You have also added a thin flimsy rattling thing whose own resonant frequency is too high, to the detriment of overall isolation... In other words, forget the hype about needing to mix materials of thicknesses: it buys you nothing useful, and could cost you.
and sheetblock(auralex) sandwich
Sound waves don't care how much you pay for the mass in the wall. All they respond to is the mass itself. The cost is irrelevant. So if you have stacks of money to blow, then buy expensive mass. On the other hand, if you are like most of us here and don't have millions in the bank, then just buy the cheapest mass that will do the job: Plain old ordinary 5/8" drywall. Nothing better, for the price!
and then just resilient channel and the drywall sheetblock sandwich on all the other walls and ceiling?
How much isolation do you need? I mean, in real numbers: how many decibels of transmission loss are you aiming for? With that figure in mind, you can decide on the type of construction that will get you there, in your budget. You mentioned an STC of 67, which is pretty decent, but STC is actually not a good way of measuring isolation. It does not take into account low frequencies, such as drums, bass, keyboards, etc. It only deals with speech frequencies. So a wall rated STC 67 might be fantastic for blocking out the neighbors screaming at each other, but do nothing useful when they turn up Pink Floyd on the stereo... A better system for rating isolation is TL (transmission loss). So you need to come up with a number, and design your structure to meet that number. In any event, if you close of your south side with an outer leaf consisting of a stud frame with a couple of layers of 5/8 on it (thus completing your outer leaf envelope), then you can build an inner leaf within that (room in a room), which will consist of a stud frame around the entire room, including joists across the ceiling, and another two layers of 5/8 drywall on that frame (including the ceiling). That will get you in the ball park of 60 dB or more, depending on how carefully you build it (I'm assuming that the floor is concrete slab on grade: if not, then all bets are off).
p.s. im not splaying my walls in the live room A. to save space

Loud drums is around 115 dB. What NC curve are you planning for the control room? Assuming 30, you would theoretically need something like 85 dB of isolation to make the drums inaudible. That's a real tall order! If you can get 65 dB of isolation, that would put your drums at around 50 dB in the CR. That might or might not be acceptable, depending on your criteria. If you are monitoring at 85 dB in the control room, that should be fine, since you'd be around 35 dB above the bleed, but once again, it all depends on what is acceptable for YOU. OK so 65 dB is doable for a wall without too much hassle, but its tough to get a window up to that level You'll need pretty thick laminate glass and a very healthy air gap. Thick laminate glass is expensive, so you might want to think about reducing the size of your window, to save some money. Try quoting 3/4" laminate glass in your area, to find out how much you can afford, then base your window size on that. You might be able to get away with 3/4 in one leaf and 1/2 in the other, so quote both sizes. You might also be able to get away with other types of glass (not laminate). But look around and see what you can afford.
