Exactly. Your instinct is correct here. Adding additional panes with tiny air gaps can potentially reduce your isolation, due to the four-leaf effect. The most effective method is to have two thick panes, one in each leaf, with as large a cavity as you can get in between them. Simple two-leaf MSM isolation remains the best and least expensive method for isolating. If you had four leaves in there, then you'd need roughly double the total mass of glass that you would have needed in a two-leaf, and also a much larger cavity depth. Your first drawing (on the left) is the correct one. But I have my doubts about those dB numbers being correct! There's no way a single pane of glass 16.8mm thick is going to get you 42 dB of isolation by itself. Mass law predicts about half of that, at roughly 36 dB. 6.8mm ain't gonna get you 36 dB either: more like 30. I'd suggest you ask your supplier for the acoustic lab test reports that show their glass really does get that amount of isolation.
100mm cavity, acoustic roll insulation, 150mm stud work, 12mm OSB, 15mm soundbloc plasterboard, green glue, 15mm soundbloc plasterboard.
The general rule of thumb is that the surface density of your glass needs to be at least the same as the surface density of your wall. One layer of 12mm OSB plus 2 layers of 15mm drywall works out to about (7+10.8+10.8) = 28.6 kg/m2. The density of glass is somewhere around 2400 kg/m3, so you would need each pane to be at least 12mm thick, absolute minimum. Therefore, your choice of 12.8 + 16.8 is borderline, and 16.8 mm on both sides would be better, given that the air cavity between the panes is thinner than the air cavity between the wall leaves, and obviously you cannot put insulation between the panes! You do seem to need a lot of isolation! That's a pretty hefty wall you are building there. What is your isolation goal, in decibels? - Stuart -

Hey Blankstudios and Stuart, I've been going through similar discussions with a few window companies for my studio build! When they write 6.8mm what they mean is a laminated glass that is 6mm+acoustic foil+8mm thick. So its actually 14mm thick glass with an acoustic foil in between. Our studio is ran by 3 day to day producer/engineers. In our current project space that we'll be moving from, we're pretty much always booked up and on each others toes so we need a space where we can get more than one job working at a time (usually one person record while another mix). In the existing space we have a main control room, small live room, then a second mix room (with inadequate sound proofing). So in this build we wanted to create a space with two control rooms, both looking in to 2 recording spaces (one small, one large). The idea here is that one person can be mixing - in either space - while another is tracking (using one or both of the tracking spaces). Or, if our walls and windows are good enough, both end control rooms can be recording in their respective recording spaces (dependant on instrumentation i expect and subsequent levels). We've designed it so line of site through all rooms is present, tried to keep good room ratios, and also had to work with building control to maintain a fire strategy that they're happy with (the long corridor to the front door exiting at the bottom left rated to 60minutes and available to those back rooms). Its possibly a bit of a clunky design, i'm sure some of the more experienced folk here will have some thoughts toward it. But it's built around the ergonomics of our desired mode of operation, so I'm excited to get it up and into action!
