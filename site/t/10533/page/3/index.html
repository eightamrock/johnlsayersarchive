Well, basically its the fabric. I had not spec'ed fire rated fabric as a cost savings factor and also since this is really zoned a "home office" and not a commercial space. BUT, the reality is if I am using it in any commercial way, the liability is still mine regardless of zoning. Insurance is mine. If there were to be a fire and someone got hurt (let's hope not) I could get sued for having non-compliant fabrics in the space. Not worth it. By taking care of this now, my inspector won't shut me down during the final and make me take it all down and re-do it. That's the savings right there. BTW, my next door neighbor is also my inspector so I will not slip anything by him like this. Plus, I wouldn't want to. Code is there for a reason, a really good reason. Better to do it right the first time. Right, so here's the reality check for all us DIY'ers out there..... Burlap - No freekin' way. Goes up like a torch. Try it. Muslin - again, better used as a torch for exploring caves then above your head in upholstering the ceiling clouds. Velvet - gotta be fire retardant, treated in some way. Acoustic Foam - light it and see. :ahh: :asth: (I wasn't using any of this but thought it was worth mentioning) I'm going for Guilford of Maine stuff and some treated velveteen. There's a reason why all the studios are made out of Guilford. Yes, its acoustically transparent but its also Class A fire rated. The best rating. All of this is more expensive, but nothing compared to the expense of a lawsuit where someone got hurt in a fire. -ashley

Fuggetaboutit... :finger: However, Wes had something in that regard. Back in the rear dark corner of Lowe's, there lies a section of the store devoted to drop ceilings.... :mrgreen: Back there you can buy hooks that will go through two layers of 5/8" drywall in packs of 20 for around $2.50. Now that's more like it! 120 hooks and two spools of 1/8" hanging wire later, we are hanging the empty frames up to see how they'll look and get everything set before putting in the lighting, upholstering and stuffing them. Since we used metal framing, we had to pre-drill the holes to get the hooks through the 18 gauge metal. Problem...... the coarse threads of the hooks didn't feel quite secure enough just hanging in the metal. So we decided to add plastic anchors so as to snug those hooks and also fill the hole fully. This worked quite well. Just to be clear, the hooks are in the metal studs but also within the plastic anchors. This works well. I have piccies but just haven't got them out of the camera. bassman7 February 2009#57
I have the pics of the ceiling hanging process..... We set the panels on the floor and placed them, carefully lining them up with string lines and such. Then using a plumb bob we transferred marks up to the ceiling and strung lines using pushpins. Find a stud within the line and then mark out from there. Redundancy is the key to making sure these don't come crashing down years later. Re-plumb bob down to where the anchors go on the panels and put in the sister set of hooks. Wire and hang.... -ashley

Soundman202016 March 2009#73
Currently, there is a think (1/4") layer of plexiglass covering the stained glass. We would take that off and replace it with the 1/2" tempered glazing. I know laminate would be a couple dB better but the cost is more than double AFAIK.
1/4" Plexiglass? No wonder you have a problem! Plexiglass is less than half the density of normal glass. 1/2" of tempered glass would be roughly 4 times the mass of the existing plexiglass, so by mass law alone you should get around 12 dB extra isolation, just from replacing that 1/4" plexiglass with 1/2" tempered. If you could go to laminate, then you'd probably get even better, maybe 14 or 15 dB. Theoretically, of course. About the idea of also putting another layer of glass on the neighbor´s window: I'm wondering if that might create a four-leaf system? With only five feet of air space separating your window from his, and considering the sound levels that we are talking about, I'm wondering if that might already be 3-leaf system that you are about to turn into a four leaf system, which would probably be even WORSE isolation at very low frequencies? Maybe I'm barking up the wrong tree here, but to my limited understanding, it does seem like a possibility. Maybe you should ask the acoustic experts here to figure out if this really is a 3-leaf that you are about to make into a 4 leaf. In that case, perhaps a better solution would be to replace his existing window glass with 1/2" tempered (or laminate) glass? Even if it is already 3 leaf, that would still add another bunch of mass, which should gain yo another few dB. One more question: Are you sure that it is only the WINDOW that is the problem? Could it be that there is another issue too? I also agree with Brien that maybe drapes, shutters or a plug would be another way of adding just a little more to the total. After all, if you can't do one big step that adds 20 dB of isolation, maybe you can do 20 small steps that add one dB each! Just thinking out loud here. - Stuart -
