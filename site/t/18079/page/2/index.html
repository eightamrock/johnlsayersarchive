Yes, you're right but i never understood why!! Can you please explain the purpose of the hard back on clouds?
The idea is to help break up vertical room modes. Since a mode is just a standing wave, if you put something big enough and solid enough in its path, then you upset the path a bit, and force it to change slightly: The bigger, more massive and more rigid the object is, the more effect it has. So a large cloud with a hard back on it will do that, and if you angle it as well, then you get the extra benefit of possibly changing the frequency of the mode slightly, as you reflect some of the energy off in a different direction from where it was going, thus changing the path length. That's the general idea. I think it was Glenn who first suggested this, so he gets the credit. And if he's around, maybe he can comment on his experience with the, since I'm pretty sure he uses hard-backed clouds regularly. - Stuart -

Soundman202028 July 2013#32
but i have an already massive angled ceiling, i thought it was the same!
It's not the same, actually.... :) Similar, but not the same. Your ceiling is part of the inner leaf of your room, and defines the boundary where the modes terminate. A hard-backed cloud is not part of the room boundary: it is not sealed to the walls, so sound waves can still diffract around it... - Stuart -

Interesting, but strange. I'm also not sure why they added a curve with the desk eq cut -3! Was there something in the room similar to a desk? Even stranger is that they did NOT show the most important curve of all: with the Low Shelf filter set to -5 dB, to compensate for the infinite baffle effect. It would also be very useful to see a photo of how that speaker was mounted in the anechoic chamber: Unless they show how they did it, it isn't clear at all that they actually simulated a true soffit mount (flush mount in wall). It would be great if they could show a photo of what they did, and also repeat the test with the Low shelf set correctly. The interesting thing is that plugging the port makes very little difference at the very low end. The other concern is the rise in high frequency response in all of their graphs: 18kHz is about 3 or 4 dB higher than 5k, on-axis. Combined with the bass rise, I would guess that this speaker is not designed to be a studio reference monitor, but rather is meant to "sound good" as a bookshelf speaker for a typical home stereo system. The overall response curve has the typical "smiley face" signature of that type of speaker. You would probably have to set the High Shelf control to about -3 or so, to get a usefully flat curve for a studio speaker, in addition to the low shelf. So ask them if they can do that too: a test in the flush mount wall, no air gap around the speaker, with the reflex port plugged, the low shelf on -5 and the high shelf on -3, and the desk EQ on zero. That would be a realistic test of what you could expect in a typical studio. Oh, and also ask them how they plugged the bass port: what materials did they use to do that. - Stuart -
