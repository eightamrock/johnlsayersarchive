SonicClang26 March 2006#2
By "soundboard" what do you mean? There's no such thing is soundproof board you'd buy at the store. It's the combination of every day building materials that yields results in stopping sound. If you mean drywall, or gypsum board, then use that. Don't use actual plaster on the walls. You need multiple layers of drywall to stop the sound. Your joists sound ok, but what's above them? If they're already supporting a waterbed you might want to consider adding joists. But 12 inches apart is really close by today's standards, mine are 16, so you already have more than normal. Personally it sounds like you don't know enough about this stuff yet to have someone coming this week to do it. You'd probably benefit from holding off on having the guy come over until you know all this stuff. It takes a long time to really absorb this stuff and once you're done building isn't the time to learn it.
knightfly27 March 2006#3
Clang-man gave you the best advice - don't do this til you know more. If you've been reading this board very long, you should have encountered a few repeats of the "soundboard vs. gypboard" question - the answer is MASS. Soundboard doesn't have it; gypsum DOES. There may be a slight advantage to using soundboard IF, and ONLY IF, you ALSO have at least three layers of gypsum in the wall ON EACH SIDE. Otherwise, you want the most mass you can get in each leaf of your wall. "approximate" dimensions don't help - you need to actually MEASURE your joists - thickness, depth, spacing, and length, as well as type of wood if you can; otherwise no one can tell whether your ceiling will fall on your head or not. We also need to know EXACTLY what your upper floor is made of - material, thickness, etc - this figures into calculation of maximum load you can have. Some of the better grades of bathroom caulk might work, but they cost more for a 10 oz tube than the REAL stuff costs for a 29 oz tube. Another reason to slow down a bit, if you expect your place to STAY isolated after the caulk sets up - you can, if all else fails, order the right stuff online good stuff http://www.acousticalsolutions.com/prod ... /caulk.asp Again, I would slow down, read more, and become the expert; your builder will NOT be the expert here, I doubt that 1 in 100 contractors on average will have ANY clue to the physics involved in isolation construction. I personally have NEVER talked to a "normal" contractor who had the first inkling of what to do, or what NOT to do. Not kidding. If you rush, you WILL be disappointed in the results - that's one guarantee I feel really safe in making. So please, read/re-read the articles in the REFERENCE section near the top of the Construction forum, actually MEASURE your framing, etc, and let's take this slow enough so you're actually HAPPY afterwards... Steve

SonicClang27 March 2006#17
I've always used the terms "resilient channel" and "z channel" interchangably and I've seen them described both ways. Watever term you refer to it by, it's a piece of metal that decouples drywall from the studs and/or OSB so vibrations don't pass from the drywall to the framework. Honestly I don't remember where I first saw them referred to as being the same thing, that doesn't matter. And firring strips are not worthless, I beg to differ with you. They can be very practical if you're up against a brick wall that's backfilled with dirt on the other side and you want to save space. They're not intended to be like resilient channel. Their purpose is to act like a stud when you don't have the space to actually use studs. EDIT: Here's one example of the terms being used interchangably. I just did a google search for "z channel". http://www.djmart.com/ac101ba.html Resilient Channel (aka "Z" Channel) This is a piece of specially shaped metal to which drywall or other building materials can be mounted to isolate them from the framing members (studs) of a wall or ceiling. One leg of the resilient channel attaches to the stud, the other leg to the layer of building material being hung. This isolation helps improve the structure’s ability to achieve greater sound transmission loss

Wurlitzer27 March 2006#19
Lubaloo, I can certainly relate to your concern about space. I recently completed my home studio build which was intended as a band rehearsal room as well as for mixing and recording. As such, I was constantly concerned with how to save floor space (to accomodate more musicians) when considering the soundproofing techniques. Partly because of practical problems that came up and things that went wrong, I ended up with a larger airgap between the two wall leaves than most people use - about 8 to 9 inches most of the way around. The room will be OK for most rehearsal purposes but I'm slightly disappointed I din't manage to get it bigger. However on the upside, the soundproofing results are fantastic - I'm talking almost total isolation even at high listening levels. (I've recently posted about this here and at SOS). Which will be really handy for rehearsal purposes because I'll know we can play whatever we like whenever we like, and it needn't affect anyone. Early on in my build, when one of the things came up that meant the room looked like being smaller than I wanted, I did briefly enquire around about making it a single-leaf structure instead, and just beefing up the single wall with as much mass as possible. I was advised not to, so didn't. But whether I would have gotten results that were "good enough" that way, and saved a lot of valuable internal real estate, I don't know... I think to make a judgment like that you'd really need to look at detailed isolation specs on different types of materials and approach it more technically. The only way to be confident you're going to get decent isolation without doing this is to build a true mass-air-mass system. This means that the two leaves of mass have to be genuinely decoupled (ie, your plasterboard has to hang on studs which are not in direct contact anywhere with the outer wall). And this, unfortunately, takes up space. You might consider coming up with your maximum practical single, non-decoupled wall. ie what you would get with your current wall beefed up with render, a few layers of plasterboard etc. With no airgap at all. Then see if you can enlist some help here, at SOS etc, in calculating the isolation spec of such a wall. Then get some feedback on how such a spec would translate to the real world (eg, "bass drum above 90db would be just audible a few metres away, above 100db would be quite noticeable", etc etc.) And if this seems enough to you, do it and save the hassle and space required for a more complicated build. That's what I might have done had I been prepared to spend the time researching it more fully. But I wanted to get on with things and just sacfificed the space instead. Don't forget too that factors such as doors, windows and ventilation will have a huge effect on soundproofing, and need to be consider in any such calculations.

lubaloo, you have to be careful when adding gypsum directly to brick, you really need to do it without ANY air gaps between the gypsum and the brick (or render). at least, this is true if you want to isolate low frequencies see this chart below. this data is from IR-586 and shows the effect of drywall (gypsum) directly applied to block wall WITHOUT any furring (just directly screwed on). even the tiny gaps that occur do to irregular surface give worse iso from ca. 200 hz to 400 hz by around 5db. of course above that everything's great, +8 db or so. see this post for complete comment. http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... 0374#30374 what would happen with a 1/2 inch gap i can't say for sure, but it would almost surely hurt performance, most likely at a lower frequency than this example. isn't it amazing that you can add 2 layers of gypsum to a block wall and HURT performance over a range of frequencies? watch out!! dan
