Soundman202024 February 2011#2
can anyone pleeeaaasssee tell me if it is worth the extra cost of the RCIS or better yet is it even necessary in a M+A+M wall assembly?
Simple answer? No. Not necessary. It will not gain you anything at all. RSIC clips are designed only to decouple the drywall on one leaf. If you have a separate frame (which is what a properly built "MSM" wall implies), then the leaf is already decoupled. decoupling it again won't help. Forget the RSIC and save yourself some money and space. However: I also noticed that you are splaying the inner leaf walls in your CR, but you are splaying them the wrong way! The room should be WIDER at the back, not narrower. You might want to fix that. Your floor plan and your SketchUp model also don't match: one of them is not to scale, and not correct! The floor plan shows a square room with the listening position in the exact geometric center. It's hard to think of a worse layout than that! But things look better on your Sketchup model, so I'm hoping that's the accurate one... :) - Stuart -

Soundman202025 February 2011#4
One thing I am unsure of though is my plans called for (2) layers drywall outside leaf and (2) layers inside leaf. He is suggesting (1) 5/8 Drywall + (1) Sound Board Insul Sheet + (1) Layer OSB 1/2" Inch on the outside and then (1) layer 5/8 Drywall + (1) layer sound board inside. He seems to be right as the differenet materials that the sound will encounter is better but I am waying the cost as well. What are your thoughts?
Your original plans are just fine: 2 x 5/8 drywall on each leaf is excellent. Yeah, by combing materials of different density you can theoretically help to flatten out your coincidence dip and do other exotic things that might minimally improve isolation if Venus happens to align with Mars at sunset on Christmas eve (:)), but frankly the slight advantage of mixing dissimilar materials just isn't worth the hassle and extra cost. And for that kind of wall the coincidence dip is so far up the spectrum anyway that it hardly matters how deep it is. Your real issues are down at the other end of the spectrum, in the very low frequencies, where using different materials has no useful effect at all. The general consensus here is that it isn't worth worrying about. Lots of extra hassle/cost for barely measurable effects in the wrong part of the spectrum. Just go with the cheapest most dense mass you can lay your hands on, and 9 times out of ten that is going to be plain old 5/8 drywall. Sound waves don't care how much you paid for the mass! Simply adding an extra layer of 5/8 will way outperform that sandwich of strange stuff, and probably cheaper, too! Also, I would NOT consider using 1/2" of anything in an MSM wall, and certainly not 1/2" OSB! Too thin, to flexible, too high a resonant frequency, too low a mass. Bad idea. Thin stuff is not good (unless it is really, REALLY dense, as in steel plate or lead sheeting, which are great, but the cost is just a tad higher.... :) ) So I'd forget the 1/2" OSB thing, right away! No use. Get mass, get density, and get it thick. So just go with your original plan of two layers of 5/8 on each leaf, and you'll be fine. Now, if you REALLY want to make a difference with an exotic sandwich, then add a layer of Green Glue between your two layers of drywall: that stuff actually will make a worthwhile difference, much better than mixing types of materials, and it will be in the right area of the spectrum. - Stuart -

gullfo27 February 2011#6
if you are concerned with the cost (and plastic will not work for your glass) - you might consider reducing the real glass $150K windows to something smaller - say 5' w and 6' tall maximum. also the angles are not symmetrical or steep enough on the control room. you need those side walls at 15 degrees if you're going with a massive plate glass wall like that. the spread between the monitors also have to be wide enough to accommodate your console in the right position, support your monitors, and provide enough baffle to extend the speaker into the room smoothly. each on a 30 degree angle (forming a 60 degree isosceles triangle). if you are going to mount the video monitor on the front wall, you'll want a big one there as well. minimum 55". while you're at it, you might consider 5.1 capability for the room. also, you said in your original post you have 23x90 but you're only using maybe 1/3 of the space for the studio?
Soundman202027 February 2011#7
Like Glenn said: you have a huge amount of glass in that design. That is going to cost you a huge amount of money, and will most likely cause acoustic issues with the room. Glenn is absolutely right (like usual! :): You might want to give some serious thought to reducing the amount of glass in that design, (unless you have a bottomless bank account!). Regarding your questions on the Soffits: You are right about the angles on teh front wall: it does not look right to me, but it's hard to tell with only the picture. Also, I'm not really understanding the design concepts you have there for the soffits: What is the gray colored stuff above the soffit panels, going diagonally? Is that carpet? Cloth over insulation? Something solid? Something soft? From the picture is also looks like the speakers are poking out of the soffits, and are tilted down at some angle while the soffit is vertical. I wouldn't do that. The purpose of the soffit is to act like an extension of the front panel of your speaker. It is supposed to be co-planar with the speaker front panel, and therefore must be at the same angle. If you need to tilt the speakers down to get correct geometry, then you you have to tilt the entire soffit panel to the same angle. You can't just angle the speaker and leave the panel flat. That leads to another question: Why do you have the speakers so high up the wall? The general rule is to mount you speakers at the same height as your ears, which is roughly 1.2 m above the floor for most people (that's the height of the acoustic axis of the speaker, not the top or bottom of the speaker cabinet). Your picture shows the speakers to be mounted much higher than that, and there don't seem to be any obstructions in the room that make you put them that high. What is the reason for that? Can you change that, and put them at the recommended height? There is also the issue of width: the soffits aren't wide enough. They should extend quite a distance to each side of the speaker cabinet, at least 50cm or so, and more if you have the space (or if the speakers are already large). It looks to me (Glenn already said this, too: I'm just repeating it) that your geometry is off for the front end of that room, particularly the speaker / walls / listening position. You might want to look into that some more, and re-design it to follow the normal recommendations better. Finally, there's the issue of your subs: Do you really need two subs in that room? I can't see that there will be a need for that, especially if you are soffit mounting the mains. Also, with two subs spread apart you tend to create a null "trough" down the center of the room. I think it would be better t go with just one sub in the middle, or if you really have to have both of them, then I'd put them right next to each other in the middle of the front corner. They should also be on the floor, not part way up the wall. - Stuart -
