Deluks18 September 2008#4
The plasterboard onto the block walls will create a 'coupled' 2 leaf system. Not great. :( It will either be fixed to battens, or dot n dabbed to the wall (effectively glued) It would be better to render straight onto the blocks (hardwall, not costly finish coat) and then build seperate timber stud frames and fix the plasterboard to this. However if you've already shelled out loads of dosh and the work is complete then you'll just have to work with it. Is the wall made from 2 layers of solid blocks, or the hollowcore ones? With any luck it won't be standard UK cavity construction :( Patio doors are used for light, and visual communication, but as you seem to already have a window between control/live, then fire door will be better. You don't seem to have any seperation between these 2 rooms though, which means 2 walls 'not touching' and 2 seperate panes of thick glass (not any old double glazed unit) that includes using 2 fire doors back to back instead of just one. Ultimately though, soundproofing between rooms may or may not be as important as soundproofing to the outside world. Depends on your requirements/neighbours.

DarkStorm wrote:
I'll look through the stickies to understand decoupling more as well.
In the brickwork example given above there is a clear example of coupling. Looking at the inside of the outer most brick wall you can see what looks like "tabs" spaced every 5th row at 3 or 4 brick intervals. This brick tie is common in a structure that has no other framework but the brick. It looks like mass air mass, but if any vibration comes along that can move the one side, it in turn by this coupling will move the other side. Just as another example.

Soundman20202 November 2008#24
The one thing I'm unsure of (which I can't find in rods book) is how big should the air gap be between the two walls of the two leaf system?? We'd like to save on space so what's the smallest the air gap can be while still being effective?
According to a rather old (but very valid) technical paper on acoustics, when using drywall for your mass, the optimum total thickness for the entire wall is 11.5 inches, with the mass being equally divided between the two leaves. However, even a couple of inches of airspace is good, and from about 4 or 5 inches upwards you are not really gaining an awful lot more. If you have the space, then shoot for 11.5 inches, but if not, then just go for as much separation as you can. The air gap is important, but much more important is the total amount of mass, and also the amount of insulation in the air gap. If you have plenty of time and really want to understand the technical issues in optimum wall design, here is a link to the paper: http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... ight=paper
