Hi Adam, and welcome! :) It would be good if you could measure the actual sound level of your drum playing. Get hold of a sound level meter, and put some real numbers to what you do. A drum kit played hard is in the region of 115 dB, and you say that you don't play that loud, but that still doesn't tell us of we are talking about 110 dB or 90 dB. That's a factor of one hundred times difference in sound power, so it's kind of important! In other words, we need to know how loud you are, in objective, real-world decibels, not subjective opinions. You also say you want to play at night, and that you have neighbors 1.5m away, so you need to answer the other question: How quiet do you have to be? Your local municipal noise ordinance will tell you that. Pay a visit to their web site, or in person, and get a copy of that. It will tell you what the maximum legal level is for noise at midnight, also in terms of decibels, and it will tell you how and where that level is to be measured. So, with those two numbers in hand, you can answer the REAL question: How much isolation do you need? Simply subtract your "How quiet" number from your "how loud" number, and you have your answer. With that number in hand, now you can look at the various possible types of construction that have been tested and are known to give you the right amount of isolation. You can look at those, and pick the one that best fits your existing structure, and your pocket.

Soundman202031 March 2012#4
The double glazed window is the typical design(two panes of glass in same frame with an air gap)...
No use for what you want! Forget that. That will work fine for speech, but NOT for drums. Let me explain: When you have two sheets of "something" separated by an air gap, you have a tuned system. Technically, it is called an "MSM" system, for "Mass-Spring-Mass". In the case of double glazing, the panes of glass are the "Mass" on each side, and the air trapped in the middle is the "Spring". That's a resonant system, and it will resonate whenever it "hears" the tone it is tuned to, or tones nearby. For those frequencies (around its own resonant frequency), it is transparent to sound: it lets the sound straight through as though it wasn't even there. At about 1.4 times the resonant frequency, it starts isolating. At twice the resonant frequency, it isolates reasonably, and at three times the resonant frequency and higher it isolates quite well. The "tuning" is set by the amount of mass on each side, and the thickness of the air gap. As the mass goes up, the frequency goes down, which is GOOD! You want the resonant frequency to be as low as possible, ideally no more than half of the lowest frequency that you want to isolate. And as the depth of the air gap increases, the frequency also goes down. So here's the problem: The type of typical double-glazing that you mention has thin glass (low mass) and a thin air gap. So guess what? It is tuned to a high frequency! Low enough that it still isolates speech and similar sounds quite well, but way too high to isolate drums. If you really want to play drums at midnight, then forget typical commercial double-glazing. You need a much larger air gap, and much more mass, to isolate drums. Studio windows for accomplishing that normally have air gaps upwards of 4 inches (10cm), and commonly 6 or even 8 inches (15 to 20 cm), while the air gap in a typical commercial double-glazed window is just a cm or two. Also, the glass used in commercial double glazing is just a few mm thick, while the glass used in studio windows for isolating drums is at least a cm think, and commonly much more. As you can see, isolating drums is not easy! It can be done, but not with commercial double glazing. You need heavier laminate glass, and you need deep air gap. How heavy and how deep are things that you'll get into as you advance with your design, and figure out how much isolation you need. There's a process here, a sequence of things to be done. There are equations you can use to calculate things, and stacks of research that you can refer to, to better understand what you can achieve and how to achieve it.
I've heard that you can create a window plug or cover if the window is a weak link.....
Yes, you can do that, but once again you have to design it to meet YOUR needs, to give you the level of isolation that YOU need. The plug that worked for the next guy might be totally inadequate for you, or it might be extreme overkill. In order to find out, you need to take those measurements with a sound level meter, and do the math on different options of plug, to see which one will do what you need. - Stuart -

Soundman202010 April 2012#10
I know it's difficult without any numbers/figures of sound levels of my playing etc........but how effective would this design be?
Unless you decouple your inner leaf from your outer leaf, it won't be very effective. The single biggest issue with walls that are not decoupled, is that sound hitting the inner surface is transmitted directly to the outer surface via the studs. If you want isolation, you have to break that direct path somehow. If you don't have space for a proper second stud frame, then there is an alternative, called "resilient channel". It is a special type of metal channel designed to do exactly that: decouple the drywall from the studs, acoustically, so that the sound is not transmitted through that path. Take off the drywall on the inside of your room, put resilient channel across the studs, then put up your plywood and drywall on the resilient channel, taking great care to make sure that the screws do NOT touch the studs anywhere: the screws must ONLY go into the resilient channel. If you do that correctly, and also fill the entire wall cavity with good insulation, then that will help a lot to isolate your room. Do the same with the ceiling: Take of the existing ceiling, put resilient channel across the joists, and put two layers of 5/8" drywall up there, with plenty of insulation in the space above.
then on interior walls and ceiling, using 1 layer of 12mm thick ply followed by 1 layer of 16mm thick plasterboard.
That's fine, but put the plywood on FIRST, and then the second layer is the drywall. So the plywood is screwed directly to the resilient channel, then the drywall is screwed to both the plywood and into the resilient channel. Also, 12mm is too thin: use only 16mm (or 15mm) drywall.
with the floor, laying 25mm thick plywood, then neoprene mat followed by underlay and carpet.....the floor will also have 25mm ply under the joists and packed with insulation......
I'm not too sure how well that will work: it sounds like it will be sort-of decoupled, but not actually floated... I think I'd put an extra layer or two of that plywood on there, for extra mass, specially seeing that you are going to be playing drums in there. That implies impact noise, so more mass will help a bit with that. But forget the underlay and carpet! Carpet is a terrible choice for studio flooring. It has the exact opposite characteristics of what you want: It absorbs sound selectively, absorbing a lot of highs, only some mids, and no lows at all. You need to do the opposite in a small room: You need to absorb lots of lows, and not touch the highs! Think of it this way: when you see photos of world-class studios, how many of them have carpet on the floor? :) Zero. I'd suggest something like a ceramic tile floor for that room. If the structure is tough enough to take all this weight, then you could put down a layer of tile backer (the thicker the better) then lay thick ceramic tile on that. That would give you a great floor, acoustically, that would also be good for isolation. Of course, you'd have to treat the rest of the room accordingly, but you'd have to do that anyway.

temperatures don't changed that radically here.....I just need to get the air in and the air out.......will vents alone with silencers do this?
You might be surprised how fast the temperature will rise inside your room! It is going to be double-sealed, totally airtight, and really well insulated, from both the isolation system and also the treatment. You'll be in there (human bodies generate heat), playing hard (more heat) with lights and equipment turned on. Maybe more people with you in there too. The heat has no place to go! The ventilation system will only move some of that heat out, and it can get rather warm in there, pretty fast. There's also another major issue: humidity. Instruments, microphones and some equipment is sensitive to humidity, and very sensitive to sudden changes in humidity. People emit moisture, bot from breathing and from sweating: the harder you play, the more moisture you are putting out into the air. If you don't get rid of that moisture, the humidity rises really fast, affecting the sound of your instruments, and your mics, and your equipment. Air conditioners are good at getting rid of humidity, as well as being good at cooling. A small mini-split system only costs a few hundred dollars, and will take care of both the humidity and the cooling, while your fan-and-silencer system takes care of the ventilation. - Stuart -
