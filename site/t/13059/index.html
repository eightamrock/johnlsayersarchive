gn87berner20 May 2009#3
Use Fixall. It's similar to bondo, but for construction. comes in a powder, dries very hard like cement. it will swell if filling large gap, come back just before setup and "shave" it down flush when using in finished areas. If you don't shave it down while semi hard forget trying to get it flat again. Mix just enough to use before it sets, about 20 min. Like the name says, you can fix a lot with the stuff.

i got this idea from rod gervais' book home recording build it like the pros. he is talking about adding mass to a ceiling but its the same principle. i think i followed his concept but not his exact instructions. get the book if you don't have it already. money well spent.
Did you simply use drywall screws to hold it up and then use acoustical sealant around the edges?
no. if you start from the bottom and build up there is no need to actually screw through the drywall imo. there might be for a 30 ft wall but not for my walls with a max height of 11ft. i used ledger board (i think this is a generic term for a board that holds something in place) nailed to the framing. there are places where i added 2 layers of drywall and others where i added only one. the process was a bit different for each. how many layers are you adding? on the single layer i cut the drywall as tight as i could. the old framing is irregular so nothing ever fit perfect. then i cut 2" chunks of ledger board, pressed them tight to the drywall, and used a finishing nail gun to attach them. i can tell you the specs on the nails if you like. so the drywall was held firm against the wall. i figured it would be better to couple them then to have them semi loose. after that i used backer rod where the gaps where bigger then say 1/4". then i sealed up with acoustic caulk. i smeared it in with my finger to make sure there was a good seal. with 2 layers. i ran ledger board all around the drywall. caulked..usually no backer rod needed here. cut the second layer inserted this inside the ledger board, used 4d finsh nails to hold it in place, then backer rod and caulk.

Do you mean that you are going to attach the drywall to the existing (outer-leaf) studs? Or that you are going to build another (separate) stud frame to attach your inner leaf drywall to? If you don't do a separate frame and just put the drywall on the existing studs without RC, then you will not get good isolation.
I'm still not entirely sure if the main point is sealing the wall air tight, or to mechanically separate the wall from the ceiling, floor, etc.
Both! :) "Airtight" is a big part of it. If you have a really great 2-leaf MSM wall, with excellent isolation, and you drill a small hole aal the way through it, then you now have a lousy wall. The hole acts as a miniature loudspeaker. So hermetic sealing is important. And decoupling is important. If you have the same wonderful 2-leaf MSM wall as above (without the hole in it!), but you then drive a long nail through from one side so that it makes contact with a stud on the other side, then you now have a lousy wall. The nail "short-circuits" the two leaves, allowing vibration in either leaf to transmit to the other. in other words, it acts as a flanking path between leaves.
If it's to mechanically separate the leaf, what about the fact that the wall is screwed into the frame, which is connected to the floor AND ceiling! I dunno, still till learning.
Yeah, this stuff sure ain't intuitive! It take a while to get your head around it. As you point out, the floor is the common point, and does mechanically connect the inner-leaf and outer-leaf. That's why you want something really massive and well damped for your floor, such as thick concrete poured directly on grade. Thick concrete is very rigid, which governs for isolation at the lowest frequencies,it is massive, which governs isolation at higher frequencies, and it is damped across 100% of its surface by Planet Earth, which is very good! And since it is also coupled to Planet Earth, that kind of adds just a little bit extra mass.... :) So yeah, your floor is common to both leaves (unless you float an inner-leaf floor, and have a really large enough bank account and enough knowledge to do that correctly), but if it is thick concrete on grade then that isn't normally a problem. - Stuart -

Ro2 June 2009#19
Caulking takes patience and experience. The good thing about building a studio is, you get a LOT of experience :) You can finish sealant with the 'wet finger' technique. Take a cup o' water, add a little detergent (try to use detergent WITHOUT lemon). caulk the joint, dip your finger in the cup and whipe along the sealant to finish it of. Remember, a finished sealant like this can't be painted over (it can, but won't hold that good). And only works with acrylic seals. another trick is to cut of a piece of plastic tube (beveled) and "scoop" the sealant (I'll look up a video on that) Clean the joints before caulking, keep dust away. Kit flows better when it's not too cold (room temp.). Don't use too much sealant, make it in one go.
