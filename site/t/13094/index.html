Soundman202029 May 2009#12
... how can the side walls make another leaf?
What you said is this:
2 layers of either 15mm soundbloc or 2 layers of 12mm plasterboard on the front of the control room side (front of leaf 1), pack with rockwool slabs, 1 layer plasterboard on the back of leaf 1
OK, so if you put plasterboard on one side if a frame, then plasterboard on the other side of the same frame that is TWO leaves, not one. So the existing wall would be the third leaf. You are counting your "frame with plasterboard on both sides" as one leaf, when in reality it is two. A leaf is not the frame. A leaf is one or more layers of mass with air next to it, regardless of how it is held in place. If you put a couple of layers of plasterboard on a frame, that is one leaf. If you then put a couple layers on the other side of the same frame, that is a second leaf, since there is an air gap between the two sets of plasterboard. Apart from that, 12mm plasterboard is too thin. The resonant frequency is too high, and the mass is too low. Use at least 15 mm, and thicker if you can get it.
If the timber frames are sitting on neoprene and isolated from the structure, how can the side walls make another leaf?
There is no logic to that: a leaf is still a leaf no matter if it is coupled or decoupled from the structure. But apart that, if the frames are sitting on neoprene and isolated from the structure, then you have a dangerous situation! You cannot leave the wall free-standing like that. (At least, where I live you cannot do that. It is against code, since the wall can move if it is not firmly attached to the floor.) Vibration, thermal expansion/contraction, tremors, earthquakes, doors opening and closing.... There are many things that can move a free-floating wall. You need to attach your frames to the floor somehow, such as with nails or bolts though the sole plate into the subfloor. As soon as you do that, your frame is no longer isolated from the structure, since the bolts or nails create a bridge: they couple it. They create a flanking path through the neoprene. Neoprene is good for sealing the wall to the floor, but it cannot fully isolate and decouple the wall, since you need to bolt through it. But anyway, regardless of whether your wall floats on neoprene or not, that has zero effect on whether it counts as one leaf or two. A leaf is a leaf regardless of whether or not it is coupled or decoupled, sealed or not sealed. A leaf is a leaf simply due to being a large massive surface with air behind it. And since the way you describe your frame has plasterboard on both sides of it, that makes two leaves. You then describe another wall, which you refer to as the second leaf, but it is actually the third leaf, since you already have two... "... then pack the remaining space and leaf 2 with rockwool slabs then 3 layers of plasterboard on the live room side (back of leaf 2)...." That is leaf three, not leaf two. - Stuart -

Actually, on the contrary: ATTACHING it to the structure all around would be idiotic (with the exception of the floor). The dividing wall should be attached to the floor ONLY, and to the other inner-leaf walls, and to the inner leaf ceiling. No inner-leaf member should EVER be attached to any outer-leaf member, except at the floor. There must be no mechanical connection at all between inner-leaf and outer-leaf. I absolutely agree with what Brien said:
What you are trying to achieve you have not completely grasped the basics of, as best as I can tell.
That's the way it looks to me too. It seems there are some concepts here, or maybe its the terminology, that you haven't got the hang of yet. All of your inner leaf walls, and the inner leaf ceiling, must be decoupled form the outer leaf, and neoprene pads will not accomplish that. All of your inner leaf walls must have ,ass on only ONE side of the frame, NEVER on both sides of the frame, since that would create a three-leaf wall. In the case of your dividing wall, you described a three-leaf wall very clearly: put drywall on one side of one frame, then put drywall on the other side of that frame, then put drywall on one side of another frame. That makes three leaves. For your other inner-leaf walls (the ones that run parallel to the existing concrete walls) the same holds true. The existing concrete is already your first leaf: You only need one more leaf to complete the MSM wall. That leaf is drywall, and it can go on either side of the frame, but NOT on both sides. Only one side. The same with the ceiling. You already have an outer-leaf ceiling. So you only need to run joists across the tops of your new inner leaf walls WITHOUT attaching them to the outer leaf at an point, then hang your drywall from those joists, either below them or on top of them, but NOT both, and that is your inner-leaf ceiling. Once again, the existing structure IS your first leaf (except for the dividing wall, where you need to build both leaves). You only need ONE additional leaf (one again, except for the dividing wall where you need to build both leaves). There must be ZERO mechanical connections between leaves (except for the floor, which is common to both leaves). And neoprene will NOT decouple anything if you then directly bolt the two structures together, bypassing the neoprene. - Stuart -

Yep. And it must be attached to the other new walls, and the new ceiling, for stability, rigidity, etc. A single straight wall can't stand on it's own, but it can stand if it has other walls at each end, perpendicular to it, and a ceiling on top.
The neoprene underneath the wall along the floor is not a good idea.
Well, it will help to SEAL the gap underneath, especially if you have a very uneven floor, but it won't help much to FLOAT (decouple) the floor.

Not really. I wouldn't bother using neoprene there AT ALL. There is no reason to, and you run the risk of jamming it in too tight, compressing it too much, which would cause it to flank. Just leave a decent air gap there. You don't need to attach that dividing wall to the existing walls, since it will be attached to your new side walls. Those joints (new-divider-wall to new-side-walls) must be sealed, of course, but those side walls should be at least a few inches away from the existing (concrete) side walls, so you don't need any neoprene in there: just an air gap.
The wall I am building is a dividing wall. There is no existing dividing wall in place.
Right, but you are ALSO building new SIDE walls, which will be parallel to the existing concrete side walls (or slightly angled, if you splay them). It is those side walls we are talking about: they only need one additional leaf, since the concrete walls provide the other leaf. You need to build both leaves of the divider wall, sure, but the side walls only need one additional leaf, the "inner-leaf", which will be attached to (and support) one leaf of the divider wall.
The external walls of the structure are already in place. Does this mean that before I even start with the dividing wall, I already have one leaf which is the outside wall of the structure?
Not with reference to the divider wall, no. Only with reference to the new side walls. The divider wall needs two leaves in it, but the side walls only need one leaf, since the other leaf is already there. - Stuart -
