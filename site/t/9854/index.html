rod gervais6 December 2007#2
it should be concrete - the aggregate is a part of the structural composition - a sand mix does not get the job done. BUT - this shouldn't be a run away train here - you need to sit down with someone from your concrete supplier to come up with a mix that will work structurally - and you don't mention what you are installing for reinforcing throughout the slab - you are planning something for that aren't you? Your best possible bet would be to pay a few bucks to a structural engineer who can dovetail everything for you - you really do not want to screw this up even a little bit. Sincerely, Rod

rod gervais6 December 2007#4
Nikodemos wrote:
Most, if not every, floating floors (in studios) around here is actually screed....and that's what all engineers who have done something simmilar told me...
Nikodemos, That doesn't make sense to me - but then again I don't build in Greece. But - unless Greecian screed has some special properties I am not aware of- after pouring - it I were to take a cection of this deck - and make a score line the width of it (using a simple stone chisel) I could knock off a nice square edge piece of it without a problem. What that means (to me) is that if any sort of shrink crack were to develope in this slab over time - that vibration (under dead load) could be enough to complete the fracture without difficulty. On the other hand - with aggregate as a part of the mix - If I were to make a score line in a 5" slab a full 2" deep - and tried to clean that edge - it would break off very jagged due to the strength of the aggregate and it's inability to shear cleanly. In over 35 year experience in concrete - ranging from industrial to residential projects - I have never even specified screed for a slab on grade application - never mind an elevated slab. In fact - the only time I ever use screed in an application is if I first have a structural layer below it - and I am then (basicaly) using the screed as either a leveling operation to finished floor elevation - or as a base for an applied flooring finish (what we refer to in the States as a mud-job tile finish for example).
on the other hand a friend of mine (engineer) who's been helping me and he is building major constructions (industrial buildings etc) told me that concrete is the best way but cement/sand screed would probably work...... :?
Well it sounds to me as if he has more experience than the others - although I don't agree that non-structural cement is ever adequate. I would sugest that you spend some time and read through this: http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.p ... &start=280 Some more details you should check out : http://www.earsc.com/HOME/engineering/T ... asp?SID=61
I'm planning to use a steel reinforcment mesh or "web" (don't know if it's the right word) about 1-1,5cm thick. The slab would be poured on hardwood that is laid on the elastic pads...does this make any difference? I'll post a drawing later...
I wouldn't _ I would use 10mm deformed rebar grade 60 (US grade - I don't know the EU equivilent - but it should be hardened steel) on 12" centers - placed at the center of the slab. The problem with the mesh is that if it is stepped on during the pour (almost impossible to not step on it) it get's pushed to the bottom of the pour and stays there - then your contractor has to try to pull it up wjhere it belongs as they work through the pour. With rebar =- should you step on it by mistake - it will spring back into place - you have no guarantee where the mat will wind up height wise in the pour. Ands the bar being in the center of the pour is really pretty critical seeing as you have forces working in both directions on these slabs -
