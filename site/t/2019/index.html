knightfly12 October 2004#9
Sorry I lost this one, don't always have time to check this forum; Innovations was as close to correct on this as I'm aware - the bottom line is, ideally you would always have mass-air-mass between you and any space you need isolation from. The reality is, this just isn't possible in many cases in a situation that's more complex than one room in the middle of nowhere. I've talked to a couple other professionals in this field in the last few months, and all agree that in cases of airlocks, for example, you still need m-a-m isolation between outside and inside when one of the airlock doors is open, so you need to maintain m-a-m construction in EACH wall of the airlock. Even though this weakens iso compared to eliminating all inside masses in the airlock, it's still enough to keep good iso; and the airlock is a necessary part of the whole when considering actual use. The British have used multi-leaf wall construction with good success in their studios, the BBC site has papers available with constructions that work; these are NOT the most efficient from a cost/construction standpoint, but they show that m-a-m isn't necessarily the ONLY game in town, just the most efficient. In cases where more than two leaves between sound isolation requirements are necessary, my take is that you just need to realize there will be compromise and build these parts heavier to compensate; In most cases this won't involve the ceiling, because it's better to do each ceiling section separately and float them inside their respective rooms when you're going for max isolation between rooms - this would allow a bit of "bleed" into the common ceiling cavity from each room's walls at the tops, but this would be minimal and would be absorbed by heavy insulation in the ceiling cavity anyway; and m-a-m would be maintained between each room and the outside world, as long as there was only the individual room's ceiling mass leaf and the outer, containment mass leaf involved. In the case of a parallel walled outer containment with splayed walls inside for acoustics, it's best to make a complete double shell outer containment with overkill, then build inner walls lighter and at needed angles, doing whatever treatment each section needs; any solid, lighter walls inside the containment will lessen the isolation given by the outer, double wall; this is why the outer shell needs to be heavier than it would if there were no trapping/acoustic control inside. Bottom line - make all walls/floors/ceilings m-a-m where it's possible, then wherever it's NOT possible increase mass in the walls that will be weakened by multi-leaf construction, and get on with it :? ...
