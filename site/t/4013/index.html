knightfly19 July 2005#4
These panels you can get for 10 euro; the pic isn't quite clear enough, is each panel 32mm compressed fiberglass bonded to 10mm gypsum? And, are they about 1200mm x 2400mm in size? If so, the first thing I'd do is make it plain to your neighbor (if you haven't already) that you want to solve this problem, but that you'll need his cooperation to do it; hopefully doing this will help keep the whole thing from turning into a "war" - generally people want to at least be acknowledged when they have a problem, and that's what you're doing at this stage. Next, if you have access to any kind of sound level meter this will help - first, it will let you actually MEASURE any differences you make in the sound levels at your neighbor's place, and second, it will (assuming you practice using the meter BEFORE you let your neighbor see you doing it :=) add an air of professionalism to the proceedings. Next, be sure you let your neighbor know what it is you're doing - you don't want him thinking you're making all that noise just to irritate him - Have one of your bandmates stand in whatever room of your neighbor's place is the loudest, and you play a solid beat on your drums - if you have access to the sound meter, set it on "C" weighting, slow response, and have your bandmate hold it in a place in the neighbor's room that can be duplicated later, EXACTLY - get readings of the levels in the neighbor's room while you're playing the drums AS THINGS ARE RIGHT NOW, with no changes whatever. Then, use two of those panels you pictured; lay them on the floor in the SAME PLACE your drums are now, then place two MORE panels of PLYWOOD or MDF on top of the other panels, with the seams running at right angles (so they don't wobble so much) - set up your drums ON TOP of this platform (be sure nothing touches the walls) and repeat the experiment, trying very hard to play at the SAME VOLUME (and the same pattern) you did before. Have your SAME bandmate (consistency is important here) check the sound meter at the SAME POSITION it was before, and make notes of the sound levels. Make sure the settings on the meter are the same as before- some meters, such as the Radio Shack Digital Sound level meter, default to specific settings on power-up. Mine sets itself to fast, "C" weighting. For this experiment you want "SLOW" response to get more of an average. Be sure to have your neighbor present at BOTH tests, so he can (a) see that you really care, and (b) see for himself whatever improvement was made. Once this test is done, we can tell more about where your sound leakage is coming from. One thing I would NOT, repeat NOT do, is to put a bunch of these fiberglass/gypsum panels up around your room just yet; chances are, unless you were to put them up with the fiberglass facing OUT into the room, you will create another leaf in your wall/ceiling structure, which would make low frequency isolation WORSE. It could, however, make the mid/high frequency isolation a bit BETTER, but this is probably NOT where the problem is. The next step in the room, as you mention, is making the sound more intelligible so you don't play your amps as loud. This will involve several absorbent panels placed across corners and on the ceiling. If you can find either some panels like the $10 ones WITHOUT the gypsum, or some mineral wool batts 75-100mm thick, these will work. We can get into that part after the "drum riser" tests... Steve

knightfly19 July 2005#6
The drum tests were just an example; of course use whatever is the most irritating. And yes, you would put two of the "yellow stuff" panels down to make a square (fiberglass side DOWN) and then the plywood panels make a square on TOP of the other panels, but rotated 90 degrees so they "tie in" to make this "drum riser" more stable without having to nail anything. All you're doing here is making a temporary floated floor for your drums/amps, so you can tell whether your suspicions are correct about the continuous floor being one of your main problems... Steve Another thing; you might try turning UP the TREBLE and DOWN on the BASS tone controls on your bass amps, at least for practice; this generally will improve the note "definition", making it easier for others to hear without all the low frequency volume. This might let you turn the overall volume DOWN a bit and still hear what the bass is doing, and at the same time make less low frequency to travel to the neighbor...

Good job, now you know about how much of your transmission is due to flanking - another test you can do (if you're into it) is to play a fairly loud sine wave thru your speakers, and adjust the frequency down while holding your hand on the riser's OSB layers; note the frequency of the sine wave when it vibrates your hand the most, and you may be able to improve things even more by adding another layer of OSB to lower the resonant frequency.
