OK, I understand the polystyrene concrete slab. This is a "green" product. Supposedly (depending on how yours is and what it actually is ) with good thermal and sound properties, I haven't seen anything that rates the sound capabilities. That said, there are standards in place for sound exchange between rooms/apartments, etc. so it has to meet at least the minimum in your country. The very idea that polystyrene beads is used in the concrete in replace of the more dense aggregate suggests that the properties of this concrete will be different from what the old school might call "real" concrete. It is not as dense, it will not have the same weight and by virtue of this, will have a reduced ability to isolate sound in the same frequency spectrum as standard concrete would. You could view the brick in the same manner, not as dense with many air holes from the making of the brick process and much lighter (less mass) than standard brick. In effect your contractor was incorrect in his evaluation of this product as having the better sound attenuation properties of the two. As an aside, in your geographical area, the reduction of mass in the building actually helps the building to remain, or have a better chance of staying erect, in the times of violent flexing and radical movement due to earthquakes.

I agree totally with Brien: There are several issues with that plan. It won't work very well. In fact, it won't work at all! I see at least 7 problems there. Summary: 1) The plan says "Perfíl metálica cada 40mm". :shock: Every 40 mm???? I think you mean every 40 cm, not 40 mm!!!! 2) The plan calls for "lana mineral de vidrio". Ok, so which is it? Is it "lana mineral", or is it "lana de fibra de vidrio"? It can't be both at once! Fiberglass is not mineral wool, and mineral wool is not fiberglass. 3) The plan calls for a density of "144 kg/m3". That is WAY too dense :!: . About 400% too dense. If it is fiberglass, then the density should be around 30-40 kg/m3. If it is mineral wool then more like 40-50 kg/m3. If you use insulation that is denser than that, then you will compromise your low frequency isolation. In other words, the ceiling will not isolate well for low frequencies. You should reduce the density to the recommended range. 4) The plan says that the "lana" should be 11mm thick. :shock: That's a little bit less than half an inch. You need at least 4 inches of wool, which is roughly 10 cm. 11 mm is useless. 5) The plan calls for two layers of drywall ("yeso cartón"), one of 12.7mm the other of 10 mm. That is too thin! You should only use 16mm drywall, or if you can't get that in Lima, then 15 mm. (But Sodimac has 16mm, I think.) So put two layers of 16mm drywall up there, instead of the two thin layers of different sizes. Thin drywall is lousy for isolation. It has to be thick and heavy. 6) Next, the plan mentions an "estructura metálica", which seems to be a simple metal rod or metal channel that supports the 2x2 joists. But that metal thing is attached directly to the concrete above! It is not decoupled! So you have a direct flanking path right through all of the "isolation" in between, making it all worthless. 7) Finally, the last layer is show as being "Kankio micro-perforated panel". That won't work. You are trying to use that for something that it simply is not designed to do. I looked at the Kankio web site, and their perf panels are not meant for that. Sorry, but that entire plan is hopeless. It will not do anything useful. It will cost you a lot of money, for no return. So what should you do? Instead of the "estructura metálica", use proper acoustic isolation hangers and wood joists. They need to be long enough that you can get your HVAC duct in, and still leave a small gap above and below. Your joists need to be much bigger than 2x2 (50mm x 50mm): they have to support a lot of weight. Go for at least 2x4s. Attach two layers of 16mm drywall to the joists. Fill the air cavity above it with at least 6 inches (15 cm, = 150mm) of 48 kg/m3 mineral wool, or 32 kg/m3 fiberglass. That will work. It will isolate very well, and give you plenty of space to install your HVAC. But you will need to use the correct type of duct for your HVAC, and you will need to use silencers in the duct. (Forget the Kankio stuff: you don't need it, and it doesn't work the way you think it does.) - Stuart -

So what´s the aquarium analogy?
Sound is like water, and building your room is like building an aquarium. Isolating your room ("soundproffing" it) is analogous to waterproofing an aquarium. If you try to build an aquarium by putting glass on the front, cardboard on the back, newspaper on one side, a thin piece of wood on the other side, an old piece of carpet for the bottom, and nothing at all in the back.... you can imagine how well that will work! :shock: Obviously, it wont work at all. The water will leak out all over. To build an aquarium properly, you have to build it with glass on ALL sides, and it has to be perfectly sealed. The exact same thing happens with your room. If you do not "soundproof" it properly, the sound will leak out all over. If you want to isolate it properly, you cannot accomplish that by doing one thing on the ceiling, another thing on the front wall, and not doing anything at all on one side. That simply will not work. sound will leak out through the weakest part, totally ignoring the good part. You have to do the exact same thing on all sides. Back to the aquarium: If you put cardboard on all the sides, it STILL won't work! The cardboard will leak. You have to put something that is watertight on all sides. Same analogy with your room: you have to build a "soundtight" structure on all sides, and you have to do it the same way on all sides. The plan that you were given for the roof is roughly equivalent to putting cardboard on the front of your aquarium... It will not hold water. No matter what you do to the walls, floor, doors and windows, that "cardboard" is your weak point, and that is where the sound will leak out, just like the water will leak out of the aquarium. That's why you'll hear a lot on this forum about "room-in-a-room" construction. That is the most economical way to isolate a room successfully. It means that you build another room just a few cm inside your existing room, but not touching the existing room at any point (except the floor). You build four new walls that do not touch the existing walls or the existing ceiling, and then you build a new ceiling across the top of those new walls, and that new ceiling also does not touch the existing ceiling, or the existing walls. It only touches the new walls. The original walls, floor and ceiling are called your "outer leaf", and the new new walls and ceiling are called your "inner leaf". The method is also known as MSM construction, meaning Mass-Spring-Mass. The two "masses" are the two "leaves" of your wall, and the "spring" is the air in between those leaves. That's how you isolate a room. And what is shown on that plan they gave you fails on may points: It does not separate the two masses! They are joined by both the metal bar, AND by the metal profiles! So there is no isolation! sound will travel directly along both of those, directly coupling the inner leaf to the outer leaf. Also, the mass of the drywall is way too low, as compared with the mass of the concrete, and that drywall is also too thin and too flimsy to be any use. Next, the fiberglass WAY too dense to be any use. The purpose of that is to damp resonance in the cavity, to push down the MSM resonant frequency, to stop flutter echo in the cavity, and a couple of other things. It fails to do all of those efficiently, since it is way too dense. Even if the rest of the ceiling were done right, this would greatly reduce the effectiveness of the isolation. And the micro perf panel simply isn't doing anything useful for isolation there! It seems like whoever designed that does not understand the purpose of perforated panels, and even less the theory behind isolating a studio. Sorry, but that design is hopeless. It will not work. The only isolation you'll get from that is what you are already getting from the concrete slab ceiling above you. It might even make things worse, since it adds more things that can vibrate at high frequencies! - Stuart -
