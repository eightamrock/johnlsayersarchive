Cram wrote:
I had not thought of a dremel or like tool yet.. Less vibration with more precision..
Marc, a rigid foam is actually the best idea, the one I always recommend in fact. You take a chance when flush cutting the nails of creating a situation where (over tie of course) that whatever they're holding down will come loose. The length of nails fastening 2 pieces of material together should always be greater than the thickness of the 2 pieces in order to create a proper bond between the 2. rule of thumb is three times the thickness of the material you're fastening for a proper long term connection (or) if installing decking or underlayment - not less than the nailing schedule in the building code. So if you're installing 1/2" underlayment over sub-floor -you should be using a minimum 6d nail (2") in accordance with table 2304.9.1 of the IBC (note that the BOCA and the CABO One and Two Family Dwelling Codes read exacxtly the same). Cutting off those nails creates noncompliant construction from a code perspective. Now - that having been said - the nails I'mm looking at in that picture actually look like the bottom 3/4 of framing nails - which would suggest to me that there is actually a wall above that section of deck - and that those nails are part of what creates the shear strength of the building under wind load........ Those I definately would not want to cut - Use the foam - just buy the cheap white foam and push it hard to the deck before installing the drywall - that way you don't create a seperate leaf in that area - the foam just becomes a part of the whole...... Sincerely, Rod

sharward14 March 2007#9
I'm now inclined to agree with Rod that nails should not be cut, in part because of Luftweg's question, since it now makes sense to me. An object with a thickness of, for example, 3/4" attached with, for example, a 2" nail, has 1 1/4" penetration. Even if the surface to which it is attached is, for example, only 1" thick, leaving 1/4" protruding on the opposite side of the surface, it still takes 1 1/4" of pull-out in order for the object to become disengaged from the surface. In other words, the length of the protrusion is, I would think, of some value. The solution of applying closed cell foam that is at least as thick as the longest protrusion seems to be the way to go. Bear in mind that there is a cost (besides supplies and materials) to doing this: the air gap shrinks by the thickness of the foam, so if you can afford the clearance, you'll want to make up for it by reducing the height of the ceiling by the same amount. --Keith :mrgreen:

Guys - another option would be to just bend them over and flush them up with the plywood - That way they still have their entire body length - the shear remains the same(but would change if they were cut shorter) and then the need for foam goes away..... I would consider this acceptable for anythig EXCEPT when the nail is a ring-nail (a polished nail with rings running around the body). Ring nails are typically used when particle board underlayment was run over a sub floor - and ring nails do not bend but rather break because they are a case-hardened fastener........ In that case just use the foam - and I would do it for the length of the bay, that's a lot less work than trying to transition from some foam to no foam. Rod
