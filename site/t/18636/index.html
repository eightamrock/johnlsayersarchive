1) The resonant box is actually a floating floor.
You probably should read this thread before you go any further with your floating floor! : viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8173 What is the original floor in the room made of? And what is under that original floor? How is it supported from below?
2) The "fluffy" thingie is actually the backside of BITUVER FONAS 31 bitumen deadsheet... thin enough and I though it might do the job.
According to the data sheet for that product ( http://bituver.it/en/wp-content/uploads ... 1-rev4.pdf ), it is designed for reducing impact noise, not absorbing resonance in HVAC ducting. There is no impact noise going on inside an HVAC silencer. Also, those fine fibers will slowly erode from the air flow over time, and they will end up all over your room, and inside your equipment and instruments. What you need is proper duct liner, which is suitable THICK (not thin), and has a specially bonded surface to prevent the fibers from coming loose: http://www.armacell.com/www/armacell/AC ... .12.09.pdf http://www.industrialnoisecontrol.com/m ... -liner.htm http://www2.owenscorning.com/around/sou ... BERGLA.PDF http://www.hamiltongv.com/DataSheets/AeroflexPlus.pdf http://www.specjm.com/files/pdf/AHS-370.pdf

Soundman202012 November 2013#7
The construction is rigid enough to hold the new load if that is one of you concerns.
My concern is that, with a concrete slab under you, you don't need to float your floor at all! A simple "drum riser" type of floor would be all you need if you plan to have something in there that vibrates the floor or creates impact noise (bass cab, percussion, drums, etc.), or if that is just a vocal booth, then you don't even need that: just simple laminate flooring on a good underlay is all you need for vocals. What you have done there is to create a resonant box with two cavities that is NOT a floating floor, since it does not have enough mass to be one, and is not decoupled on resilient supports. It is some type of 3-leaf system, so it will not be good at isolating low frequencies, and you have lost 28 cm of ceiling height in addition (since the floor is 28 cm thick). So you have reduced the volume inside your booth, and made the ceiling much lower than it needs to be, thus harming the acoustics of the room. But what concerns me even more is that you see to be building your walls on top of that "floor"! :shock: That's plain dangerous. There is NO structural support for those walls. You cannot do that safely. You cannot build walls on insulation batts! That needs to be fixed. Walls MUST be attached to the floor, either directly or with proper resilient supports that are specifically designed for the purpose. What you are doing is dangerous, and should be taken down and re-built correctly, before someone gets hurt.
Will be changed with some 20mm glaswool slabs I have. Actually i'll use an Ecophon ceiling plate with glass tissue on one side to avoid particles spreading...
I would not recommend that. Duct liner is the only type of material you should be using: that's what it is designed for: to be used inside ducts that supply the air that goes into your lungs. I would not use anything else, since lungs are rather valuable! I would not risk unknown products with unknown properties when dealing with the air that I breath.... Healthy lungs are worth a lot more than the cost of a few square meters of duct liner... :) - Stuart -

OK, last input from my side. All points mentioned by Stuart taken in consideration and construction changed. Thank you for the time and the advises ! I would allow myself to disagree on one point though. Such a (relatively) light construction might be and is completely safe to build on a heavy mineral wool base. I was using a stone wool slab with a density of 160kg./m3. Generally speaking, dedicated floating floor slabs (glass- or stonewool) are able to withstand a load of 500 up to 1.000kg./m2, without any deformation. My construction, as built till now has a combined mass of approximately 300kg./m2. The slab that I used will not deform by a load on top less than 1.000kg/m2 (10 kPA). I do not want to get into an argument, just giving a side opinion. Best regards to all!
