Agree with Glenn. That layout also gives you much better symmetry for your control room, without having to change anything about the existing structure.
1. In the Live Room and Control Room, should i tear off the existing drywall (even the ceiling and start with the bare studs? Im not afraid of a little demo work.
I would, yes. Otherwise you'll end up with three-leaf walls that don't have enough mass on them, and you won't be able to seal your outer leaf properly. What is on the other side of each of those walls?
The Control Room has carpet (which can be pulled)
Yep! You definitely want to do that. What is under the carpet?
2. When i frame out within the rooms themselves, should there be something in between the concrete floor and the 2x4 frame, some sort of rubber or acoustical caulk/sealant?
Recommended practice is to run some beads of caulk under the bottom plate, then to run additional beads under the edge of each layer of drywall. If your floor is very uneven (such that caulk might not create a good seal), then you could run a strip of neoprene or some such under it, to get a better seal.
4. Should the ceilings in both rooms be angled off in the final framings?
You don't have enough height to slope your ceilings. Just build them flat. You might want to consider inside-out construction for those.
5. Am i out of my mind?
Absolutely! But you are in good company: the rest of us are too. Certifiable insanity is actually a pre-requisite for building a studio in your home. Studies show that it is impossible to even think about doing this unless you are totally nuts, so welcome to the club! :) - Stuart -

Soundman202011 November 2010#6
The other side of the wall is concrete slab...
Excellent!
...the 2x4 frame is built against the wall...is that a problem?
It might be, yes. Is that framing attached directly to the concrete walls and/or the floor above you? If so, then you might as well take that framing down too, but do it carefully so you can re-use the wood! On the other hand, if the framing is NOT attached to the concrete walls or the ceiling (floor above), and does not touch them at any point, then that's a good thing, and you can re-use it as is.
Would i want to acoustic caulk between the concrete wall and the existing 2x4 frame?
If it is tight up against the wall, then it is no use to you. Like I said above, you can take it down and re-use it, or if that's a big hassle you can just leave the framing there and build your new wall next to it.
And there is slab underneath the carpet.
Again, excellent! That will make a great floor for your room, acoustically.
When you say inside out construction on the ceilings do you mean whisper clip-hat channel-drywall-greenglue-drywall and then maybe a 1x1 frame?
Inside-out construction means that the drywall goes on the "wrong" side of the framing. In other words, if you build your new inner-leaf walls inside-out then after they are finished the drywall would be facing the wall cavity, and the studs would be facing the room: Standing in the finished room, you would see the studs, with the drywall on the other side. There are several advantages to building your wall this way, among them: space savings, easy wiring, and easy installation of acoustic treatment. The disadvantage is that it is harder to build like that, as you have to build the walls lying down on the floor, with the drywall and everything, then lift them into place: they are HEAVY! Or you can build them in smaller sections, then lift those one at a time. - Stuart -

Soundman202013 November 2010#9
In reference to britune's Virginia basement build. John Sayers posted this pick of his stud wall next to the concrete block. How exaggerated is the space between the block wall and the stud wall? 2"? 4"? Do you leave that space open or do you insulate that space as well?
You want that space as big as you can afford to make it, but not less than 4". The reason is because your wall is a resonant system: it is tuned to a frequency at the bottom end of the audible spectrum, and hopefully way below the bottom end. In order to keep that frequency low, you need a large gap and lots of mass on both leaves. If you reduce the mass or the gap, then the frequency goes up and that implies that you lose isolation. So if you can afford to leave an 8" air gap there, that that's fantastic and it will make a big difference to your isolation. And yes, you do need to fill that gap with insulation. If code allows in your area, then fill it completely. If not, then fill to whatever depth code allows. The purpose of the insulation is mainly to act as a damper on the MSM resonance, and it also has some other positive effects. - Stuart -
