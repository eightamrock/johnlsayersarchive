If you're building a room witihn a room structure then you only want two leaves. At the moment you're existing structure has two leaves. Adding your internal walls and framing to this would be adding a third leaf which is what you don't want, especially for bottem end isolation. The external wooden cladding would be fine as a weatherprroof outer leaf, but id guess that it won't be sealed airtight. In order to make this a two leaf construction, you'd have to remove all the plasterboard from your existing walls, build your new walls and ceiling which are not mechanically connected to the existing walls in any way (no nails, bolts, screws glue etc etc) and then plaster the new wall framing. Two leaves. Problem is, that your external leaf of timber cladding isn't giving you much mass. Mass (and an airtight seal) is what will give you isolation. So when you take the plaster off your existing walls, you'll need to beef up that external leaf in some way. Thick ply, cement sheet or something along those lines. This in turn leads you to how much isolation you need, which appears to be the main issue. You need a real world figure in dB of how much isolation you require. Figure out how loud you are going to be when you're jamming. If you've got a drums, bass and elec guitars, this figure is going to be over 100dB and then figure out how quiet you'll need it to be outside so the neighbours aren't pissed off. Subtract the small number from the large number and you get the figure for transmission loss that you need to build for. That's where you can start to figure out how many layers, and what material you need to make this construction work.

yep, keeping your audio and power away from each other is the way to go. never run them close and paralell to each other and if they have to cross, cross them at 90 degree angles. If you run your power from the top of the walls and your audio at the bottem, that should eliminate any interference. Soundman202011 November 2014#7
You are on the right track with #4, for sure! It's the least expensive and should give you the most isolation. Least expensive, as RSIC clips and hat channel are not cheap, and most isolation because it has the largest air gap between the leaves, and therefore the lowest resonant frequency. So, all other factors being equal, it should give you the best deal. For the "beefing up" of the outer leaf, the easiest (and least expensive) way to do that is to cut strips of 5/8 drywall to fit snugly between the joists, caulk all along the edges very carefully, then hold them in place with small cleats nailed sideways into the studs. Green Glue is probably not worth it here: but do use it on the inner-leaf, between your two layers of drywall there. That's what I'd do, if that were my place. - Stuart -

Regarding the neoprene under the new internal walls. For the neoprene to be effective it has to be loaded properly, which means a whole heap of maths regarding the thickness of the neoprene and the weight that is being loaded on it. Not enough weight and it won't compress enough, to much and it over compresses. So i'd say not to use it and make sure you do your sealing of the new internal wall very thoroughly using acoustic caulk. Regarding fixing your new internal walls to your existing ceiling. don't do this :!: You need to build a room within a room. this means the new walls, with another ceiling built on the new walls, does not touch your outer leaf/walls in any way. no nails, no screws, no glue, no timber....nothing. A couple of things to consider. MDF is not interested in being on the exterior of an environment, in any manner. So you should use whatever is the local sheathing applied to an exterior, be it chipboard or OSB or plywood. Not MDF. Second, if you choose to use RC, if this winds up being your choice you are not held hostage by the existing airspace. You can install rips from a 2X4 nailed directly to the existing framing which will increase the air cavity. Granted if you use RC on the wall framing you should use it on the ceiling framing as well.
