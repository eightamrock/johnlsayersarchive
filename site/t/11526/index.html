Yup, definitely the weak link. Yup definitely you want them as good as you can get. For all of your walls and ceilings (not just outside walls), think "Mass-Air-Mass" or more correctly, think "Mass-Spring-Mass". What that means is that you want a massive panel as one "leaf" of your wall, probably built up from several layers of sheetrock attached to studs (or brick, or concrete) then an air gap of a few inches filled with fiberglass or rock wool insulation, then another massive panel built in a similar manner. And you do not want anything connecting the two panels, so they should not be on the same set of studs. Or at least, one side needs to be decoupled using something like iso-clips and hat channel, or resilient channel, but even better is just to build another set of studs. This is called a "two-leaf wall", and is the best possible isolation you can get at a reasonable cost. There are no short-cuts or magic building materials, despite what you might read on the internet. The only "almost-magic" product worth considering is something called "Green Glue", which isn't glue at all, but even then it isn't magic, and is based on solid acoustic principles. Search this forum for "MAM" and "MSM" and "mass-air-mass" and "two leaf" and "three leaf" and suchlike. Then read it very carefully, until you understand the concept. Don't start building before you do. Also, check out the Green Glue web site: they have lots of laboratory test results that help you grasp the concepts. You do NOT want to build a wall that has three leaves, or four, etc. you ONLY want to have two leaves in each wall. not more, not less. Bad things happen to sound isolation at low frequencies, with multi-leaf walls.
Inside Walls. Is a 2x6 wall with 5/8” sheetrock on either side with insulation sufficient or do I need more? I have considered an extra 1 /2” sheetrock on top of the 5/ 8” sheetrock.
For best isolation, go with two independent 2x4 frames, each with a couple of layers of sheetrock on only ONE side of the frame, for a total of two sets of sheetrock: 2 leaves Mass-Air-Mass. Rip the sheetrock off one side of the existing frame, carefully, use the pieces that you rip off to beef up the other side, from the inside of the wall, between the studs, up tight against the sheetorck on the other side. Then build a second frame, put a couple of layers of 5/8 sheetrock on that with copious Green Glue in between, fill the air gap with insulation, make sure everything is sealed air-tight, and you are done!

Research "room ratios" on this forum. Also "room modes". You need to make sure that the dimensions of each room are optimal. There are good ratios, and there are bad ratios. For example, avoid all multiples, so if your room is six feet wide, do not make it 12 feet long. If it is 8 feet high, do not make it 8 feet wide, or 16 feet long. research tis forum to find out why, and to find ratios that will work for you. Final points: your control room must be totally symmetrical. Put the speakers such that they fire down the longest dimension of the room, not across it. If the ceiling slopes, it must slope up and away from the speakers (speakers under the lowest point, roughly). Don't plan to put anything in the front and rear corners, such as doors or windows or lights or electrical power sockets or furniture or equipment, as you will need that space for bass trapping. Best advice I can give you: Buy Rod Gervais's book, and buy the Master Handbook of Acoustics: Read them both until you understand everything. Then start designing. Only then think about building. Don't make the mistake that so many visitors here make: start building before you have a clear total grasp of the principles, and a design that has been thorougly "sanity checked" by the experts here. An older home like yours, elevated wood framed on piers, presents many obstacles. Flanking not being the least of them...it's practically living with you! The potential to overload this type of house is a real and present consideration, code compliance or not. If this is in fact something from the 30s then the chances of pier footings versus a complete linear perimeter footing, are increased. This "pier pad" does well to support itself and the distributed load it receives. Any change in the weather, e.g. high sustained winds, snow loads or seismic activity delude this piers ability to continue to support what it was designed for with added weather elements plus the added amount of weight. This will be a high priority concern for your engineer. You have options, but since I am not inside that build I cannot say with any authority. One would be this. The floor gets stripped all the way down to the existing framing, you can see the ground now:) Once the interior perimeter walls are established, additional pier pads can be installed to support the new walls. The floor joists should be up-sized to attain the height you are looking for, this eliminates the "drum head" design you suggested by building a new floor on top of the existing wood framed floor. Another would be to ask the locals. The weight of the entire house AND the build will rest on this floor/pier area and it is a simple task to topple piers or create unwanted settling due to compression.
