In a word, yes. You're figuring that you can simply not volunteer to pay the permit fee tax and pay the inspection fee tax and also not pay the additional property value tax, until such time that the city discovers the sin, and then the worst that would happen is that you'd have to pay those taxes then, and then be proud of yourself for postponing the payment of those taxes and maybe even save a few bucks in the interim. That's not how it works. If they find out, they will punish you not only by getting you to pay the fees that you didn't pay to begin with, but there's likely a multiplier that will be applied as a fine and then they will have the right to order you to tear it all down because they'll be unable to determine that it was built to code. Now I suppose it's possible for you to stay under the radar all the while... That's assuming that code enforcement is never contacted because all of your neighbors love you -- even the new neighbors who haven't even moved in yet -- and have zero concerns or issues about all that noisemaking going on, all the people coming and going and loading gear. Someday you'll want to sell the house. Then you'll be in the unenviable position of having to disclose the fact that you have unpermitted construction on the property. (I know someone who once passed on buying a house because of an unpermitted garage conversion -- that person is me.) Of course, I suppose it's possible that you'll live in the house until the day you die, so maybe you don't care about that. Let's talk about hazard insurance you have on the house. Suppose there's a fire. Suppose the cause of the fire is attributed to the wiring in your unpermitted construction. The insurance adjuster will be looking for ways not to pay all or part of the claim. Even if the fire isn't your fault, your illegal work may be a culprit in not retarding its spread. The story gets worse if anyone is injured or killed. Yeah, I'm a worry wart. None of that is likely to happen. (Hopefully not.) Now, what's the plus side of getting a permit?
  • You'll sleep easier.
  • Your project will be legitimate.
  • The inspector(s) won't let you do crappy (unsafe) work.
  • Your home will increase in value with the additional square footage and won't have an ugly disclosure come resale time.
  • You'll be forced to plan everything before building, which may seem like a pain at first but you'll come to appreciate it when you actually do start building. (We'll ignore the fact that you already have started building.)

I'm glad I didn't scare you off, Johntron. More than once today I thought about this thread and wondered how, or if, you'd respond to all of that. Building codes vary from city to city -- each adopts its own "model code" (often UBC, Univerasal Building Code), and then some cities amend the codes in some ways, usually to be more restrictive in one way or another. That being said, regarding ventilation -- I'm quite confident that whatever codes apply in your home town are similar to those in Boise, Idaho. Why do I pick Boise? Just because they've published this lovely sheet that explains ventilation requirements in habitable spaces. In my case, the 0.35 ACH rate is actually 0.50 (i.e., more restrictive). The point is, a habitable room requires a window of a certain size or mechanical ventilation -- either of these can allow the occupant of the room to "get some fresh air in the room." Don't try to wiggle out of this requirement by saying the room won't be habitable -- if it's air conditioned, you're attempting to make it habitable, period. I can state with certainty that you can't "keep the studio open to the rest of the house" and use that tack to skirt the ventilation requirement. Even if you removed the door fto the rest of the house from the frame altogether, that's still a separate habitable room you'll be making. There are rules for what spaces can be "considered" part of an adjacent space/room and they take such things into account as intended use, amount of wall space separating the two, half-walls and how high up they go, etc. It's all very convoluted, but the point is that I'm confident none of those exceptions apply in your case. Bottom line, you need ventilation. How you achieve that is up to you to research (what is possible and what is allowed). When you run into a brick wall, just holler, and we'll help however we can. The ventilation system I'm going to use in my project is really cool (no pun intended). It has four ports on the fan unit -- one sucks fresh air from outside; another pushes that air into the room; still another sucks stale air out of the room; and the last one pushes the stale air to the outside world. What's kind of cool about the system is that the incoming and outgoing air pass each other inside the unit (although they do not mix), in an effort to recover heat for efficiency purposes. In cool climate, heat from the stale air being sucked out of the room warms the fresh air coming into the room, whereas in hot climate, heat from the fresh air being sucked in from outside is transferred to the stale air being pushed to the outside world. At least that's the theory -- it will be interesting to see how well it works in practice. It also has an an electrostatic filter, so dust and other crud is minimized. It's a nice system with powerful fans (hopefully much more than I will need to meet code) and the 6" ducting should be relatively easy to work with. Some air conditioning systems are capable of feeding fresh air into the room. However, that may or may not be sufficient to satisfy the building inspector. So, where do you go from here? I would suggest stopping the building altogether, studying here to learn as much as you can about what does and doesn't work from a soundproofing perspective (i.e., become very well versed in the mass-air-mass principle), develop your plans (layout, construction detail, electrical, ventilation, the whole works), and then when you're pretty confident and comfortable with your plans, take them down to your friendly neighborhood building department to get their feedback. In my case I was able to show some of my earlier illustrations and I got some very helpful (albeit disappointing at the time) advice. Hopefully they will appreciate that you're trying to do the right thing and can help you achieve your goals. Take your time, be methodical, learn as much as you can about what works, what doesn't work, what's allowed, what's safe, what's dangerous, what's wise, what's stuipd, what's best, what's worst, what's cheap, what's a rip-off, what's right, what's wrong. Some of us are teachers, but we're all learners. If you have a few spre hours and a plentiful supply of aspirin, read my project thread from start to finish, and see how confused and frustrated I was at times. :shock: It's all part of "coming of age" in this stuff. I can personally relate to your balancing act -- I too decided I was going to do this thing. And now I am -- finally!! 8) --Keith :mrgreen:
