How Big Should A Vocal Booth Be?My understanding of small vocal booth/voice-over treatments tend to lean toward the "dead" side. During the insulating phase of my front soffits, I noticed how nice and quiet it got with each 4' x 2' x 3" section of rockwool that was installed. Bryan Giles cautioned however, against building a square booth saying; "Be careful, a dead square booth will have low end issues (you'll hear the issues on your mic.) Steve (Knightfly) mentioned that "4' x 5' x 7' inside dim's is about the best modal compromise for a small booth." Modally, with a 6'4" ceiling you'd do well with wall dimensions (inside) of 5 and 7 feet. That combination shows no modal problems at all. This means that vocals in such a room shouldn't be "colored" by the room. You would want to keep the upper half, including the ceiling, absorbed with such as rigid fiberglas insulation boards, covered with a light plastic film (they shed) and fire proofed cloth (they're ugly) - you'd likely get better vocal sounds with the singer seated rather than standing, since otherwise they will be nearly at the ceiling. Being that close to a boundary (especially if it isn't absorbed) will cause comb filtering due to early reflections. Mode-wise, a 5 x 6 x 7 (inside) booth looks very good. It will also fit into an 8 foot ceiling area if necessary, and is big enough for 3 (very) friendly vocalists or 1-2 with a guitar, so that's what I'm going to start with. Once I finish (don't stop breathing while you wait, I have 'way too many projects still open) you could change dimensions to suit, just do NOT make any two dimensions the same, or more than one dimension in even # of feet.

Are control rooms usually the same size as the tracking room?
From the SAE website:
So what size should a control room be? These days people are building bigger and bigger control rooms because so much happens in the control room now. Often the bass player and keyboard player actually sit in the control room. I often have the vocalist singing in the control room while the bed tracks go down, so a good size control room is a good idea. So many people think that you need a big studio and end up putting a poky little room at the end and call it a control room. I suggest that a control room ideally should be at least 6m x 5m with a minimum ceiling height of 2.4m. This size room means a good sized working area with space for the musos, friends and hangers on. Also because you don't want rear reflections to interfere it is better to start with a longer front to back dimension than the side to side dimension. i.e. 6m x 5m. The studio, on the other hand, requires a different set of considerations. The first to consider is do you want just one room or more? The trend nowadays is for more than one studio so the you can get isolation between players and different acoustics for each room. The acoustics you want for a live drum sound is totally different than you want for a vocal for example. I recommend at least 2 rooms. You can put drums in one, guitars in the other, keys , vocals and bass (DI) in the control room or maybe the band in one and the vocalist in the other. Getting separation between guitars and drums is usually OK in a good sized room but keeping them out of the vocal track is hard if they are all in the same room. Just a note here - the kitchen is an important room to consider. Gallons of coffee and tea are drunk in studios. An engineer was once asked - how do you normally have your coffee? "Cold with a fly in it !! "- was the reply. Also a kitchen allows for a fridge for the beer and a microwave for the pizza, vital ingredients in every album recording session. Toilets (bathrooms for you in the US) are also a vital service a studio should offer. The coffee and beer has to go somewhere. http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html

Aloha Jason, I'm glad you found that "collection" of ramblings helpful. Keep what you find useful, file the rest away, but be sure to examine and verify to see if what was said is true, especially before building. :wink:
a) Are these the best modal dimension for a live drum room or dead drum room?
My understanding is that this is a principle for rooms in general. Every room, be it small or large, application (drum room, vocal booth or control room) has its own set of modes and axial standing waves. The smaller the room, the more acoustic treatment (as in "dead") the room needs to be (correct me here if I'm wrong, please) in order deal with the modal and standing waves. This is NOT to say that larger rooms do not need treatment, just that they have their own areas to deal with.
b) How do you use Bob Gold's calculator to figure out the best dimensions of a room and where can find this calculator?
You can find it here: http://www.bobgolds.com/Mode/RoomModes.htm and it's basically a "fill in the blanks" and hit "compute." You can also find a room mode calculator at: http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html under the acoustics/shape & size tabs. The dimensions are taken from one of several articles in the area of the so called "golden ratios." http://images.google.com/search?hl=en&q=golden+ratios (see also attached) Another example: Golden Ratio Height Width Length 1.00 1.14 1.39 1.00 1.28 1.54 1.00 1.60 2.33
c) I've been think about making the height of the entire structure 12 ft plus a scissor truss roof (recommended by John Sayers) for the extra height. What would be considered the height of the room....12 ft or the height of the highest point of the roof?
I have the understanding that the "12ft height" is for the inside dimensions - floor to framed ceiling rather than roof, which is a different term/structure altogether.
And does the slanted roof affect the acoustics of the room for better or worse?
On one hand it breaks up the parallel surfaces (better) between floor/ceiling, but on the other hand, depending on what angles/height/acoustic treatment it could acts as a parabolic surface (worse) which could cause havoc. I've seen several live rooms where they treat the ceiling with a series of "clouds" in order to break up reflections and provide absorption.
d) I've only recorded at a couple different studios and in both places the rooms were rather large but totally carpeted, sounding somewhere in between live and dead. Are there any advantages of having a room that sounds in-between live and dead?
From the SAE site/acoustics/absorption tabs
For an instrument to sound good you must hear a balance of its high and low frequencies. If your room is too dead in the high frequencies the instrument sounds lifeless and tubby, whereas if it's too live in the high frequencies the instrument sounds distant and has no presence. To balance a room you must have a true balance of the high frequencies and the low frequencies with the right amount of deadness to liveness. http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html
e) And what is an appropriate reverberation time for a live drum?
I don't know of a formula or prescribed RT specifically for drum rooms, but if you find one, I'd be interested in adding that to the "collection of ramblings." RT60 Calculators: http://www.trinitysoundcompany.com/rt60.html http://www.csgnetwork.com/acousticreverbdelaycalc.html http://www.sengpielaudio.com/calculator-RT60.htm
