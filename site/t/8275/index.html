bcarr25 February 2007#3
Ok, here is some more information. The the basement ducts are all done with flex ducts and each send and return go all the way to the furnace instead of connecting to the rest of the houses trunk lines. The only exception is the two supply vents in studio 1. These two ducts are so far away from the furnace that I had to tie into the main trunk after a 25ft flex duct run on one and a 50 foot flex duct run on the other. A couple of the return ducts tie into the first couple of feet of the main return trunk line as well. My hope is that this would reduce the noise from the studio going to the rest of the house as well as reduce the noise from one of the studio rooms to any of the other studio rooms. I know flex duct is not always recommended due to the fact that it gets crimped in places and that speeds up the air flow. I wanted to use a separate trunk line for the basement that was made out of insulated duct board but none of the contractors would use it. One of the contractors told me that our city does not allow duct board. I guess this is my compromise. The only benefit is that since this is the basement I can keep the dampers (that are located at the beginning of the run) partially closed because it should not need as much heating or cooling as the rest of the house. Maybe that will slow down the air anyway. I have also included some construction details that I did not include in my other posts.

Question: Do you think I need to add more mass under the 1st floor's plywood? I would really like to avoid it if possible because there are many ducts up there and I have the staples sticking through the plywood from the oak flooring that was put in on the first floor. If I need to add more mass will my floor joists hold it?
If you want to reduce the lower frequencies from passing through to the upstairs the answer is a SOLID YES. You mentioned you wouldn't be playing your music very loud but how loud is that? Steve rcommends not listening to music above 85db. How close do you come to that? Might not be a bad investment in a Sound Level Meter (Radio Shack makes an inexpensive one. It starts at 50db though. You'll probably need one later anyway) Here's a link to the meter: http://www.radioshack.com/product/index ... age=search) You could get an approximate idea how loud you listen to music and post a question on here as to if you would need a "beefed up ceiling" based on the level you're comfortable listening at. If you don't already know, a "beefed up ceiling" is simply adding drywall to your existing subfloor. BEFORE you do that you definately need to get a structural engineer to approve the added weight on your subfloor. I "feel your pain" about having to add "beef" to your ceiling, but for me it wasn't a second thought. It HAD to be done because when you start crankin' the ol' speakers up, that bass WILL travel! Another thing is, if you do decide to beef your ceiling and get approval from a structural engineer, be SURE to tell them EXACTLY how you plan to do it, (how you will secure it to your subfloor, how many sheets of drywall etc) and if your ceiling JOIST can handle the weight of Resilient Channel and (however many sheets) of drywall you will put on it. These are 2 DIFFERENT evaluations they will have to make. (One for the subfloor additional weight and one for the ceiling joist weight) Also, be SURE to get a copy from their firm with a letterhead stating their evaluation and their findings (legal proof for you...just in case... :roll: ) Here's some additional help below...(One thing about posts on here...be sure to look at the DATES of the posts. Some posts listed in 2003 have additional/updated info AFTER 2003, even up to 2007) http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=3726 http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=2322 http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... &start=195 http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... ght=#43861 http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... &start=405
