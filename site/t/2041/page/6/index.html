knightfly16 October 2005#129
Drywall first, then use long enough screws to penetrate the framing by at least an inch. http://www.stanleyfasteners.com/catalog ... h36-5.html I'd use something similar to the PS 604, fastened into the drywall/framing with 5/16" lag bolts - drill into the framing thru the gypsum (a good studfinder will help if you're not exactly sure where center is) using a 3/16" bit, then drill a relief in the same hole that's a bit less deep than the smooth part of the lag (if there is a smooth part), put a good-sized daub of caulk in/around the hole, put the lag screw thru the bracket and tighten. You want a lag that's about 1-1/2" longer than the thickness of your wallboard. I'd use chain for hanging; you may need some "quick links" to connect to the angle. If this frame is part of your OUTER leaf of containment, you'll get some flanking. If it's part of you INNER leaf, probably not much at all... Steve

knightfly17 October 2005#132
please explain to this fool what the above sentence means? what's a relief? Why, it's Alka Seltzer, of course :wink: All seriousness aside, if you look at most lag screws you see a smooth part just below the head that's full thickness (for a 5/16" lag screw, this portion is actually 5/16" thick) - this part has no threads, and other than sideways (shear) force it has no holding power. IF the hole isn't large enough for this part of the screw, you have a really hard time driving the screw in all the way without either a lot of force or splitting the wood. To get around this, you should drill a pilot hole that's almost as deep as the screw is long - this hole should be the same diameter as the SMALLER part of the screw, NOT counting the threads that stick up. This gives good holding power without splitting the wood. Then, unless the lag screw is really short, there will be the full size part I mentioned in the previous paragraph - for best fit and tightening ease, you should use a larger diameter bit and drill just the depth of the SMOOTH part of the lag screw, so this part doesn't bind when you drive the screw. I normally set up two drills for this; one with the smaller, pilot bit and one with the larger bit (and a depth stop, either an adjustable rod or a set-screwed collar around the bit. Otherwise, the larger bit will grab and before you know it your entire hole is too big for the threads to grab. NOt good. Smaller wood screws can be done with one "everything" bit, that drills pilot hole, relief, and countersink area (for flat head screws) all in one pass - but as far as I know there's not such a beast for lag bolts, the pilots I have stop at #12 screws. Just a quick blurb on cabling for all interested parties, then I really have to give up and grab some sack time (It's 2 am here) - For audio cables, there are several choices in each part of the cable; which you choose should depend on what you intend to do with the cable, and how viciously :? - There are pluses and minuses for each choice, I'll try to run them down from memory - Sheath - (the outer covering that protects and insulates the cable) this can be EPDM or Neoprene or other rubber, or any of several synthetics including PVC (the typical grey plastic) and probably a dozen other types of plastic - EPDM and neoprene are very tough, fairly flexible and an absolute BITCH to strip without damaging the internal wires - but the toughness part helps them hold up to hard stage use. Shield - this is there to prevent hum and interference from getting into the signal wires, so ideally should be 100% coverage - meaning no gaps or holes in the shield. A very small gap can allow RF interference into the signal wires; lower frequencies require a larger hole before the shield doesn't work. There are at least three main types of shield used in audio cables - the worst performing, shield-wise, is a spiral wrapped stranded wire shield - this type opens and closes lots of gaps in shielding when the wire is flexed, and typically only gives around maybe 85-90% coverage. The only good thing about this type shield is its ease of termination when attaching connectors. Next best from a shielding standpoint, and BEST from a stage use standpoint, is braided shield - this is the same stranded wires, but the shield is actually braided around the core of the cable, and then the outer sheath is applied around the whole thing. A braided shield is pretty flexible, gives maybe 95% shield coverage, and is a real PITA to hook up in connectors. I, being an old fart, have a set of specially made "extractors" that make using braided shield only a MINOR PITA - but I did a google search a few weeks ago on these, and apparently they've dropped off the face of the earth. If anyone wants to look, they're called Little Joe Lead Extractors - my set consists of 4 different sizes, and they work on either twisted pair or coax cable. They are basically just a modified hypodermic needle, with a LARGE gauge "needle" and a plunger that pushes anything OUT of that needle - you strip the outer sheath, slide the "needle" over the center insulator (or wires), squeeze the shield tight against the outer barrel of the "needle", and push the plunger - this pushes the wire out through the braided shield right where you finished stripping the insulation, so now the shield is a third (or second) wire, which you pull tight and form into a braided wire and connect it to your ground terminal. The third type of shield is best for wires in studios which won't be moved around; it's a foil shield, and it's wrapped around the conductors in a spiral wrap, but the spiral overlaps itself so there is no loss of coverage; Foil shielding has typically 100% coverage, and you attach it to the connector with a bare wire that is run between the foil and the outer sheath - since this wire is in contact with the foil for the full length of the cable, you get a good contact and the entire cable stays shielded. The downside of foil shield is that it doesn't like to be flexed a lot, and it's normally used inside a PVC sheath so this type cable isn't good for stage use. It is, however, the easiest cable to work with. Other things to watch for in audio cable - capacitance per foot. This is especially true of "semi-pro" gear which typically has unbalanced connections, or if they're balanced they're still not true LOW impedance transmission lines, because the INPUTS don't load the source to a true 600 ohm impedance like a transformer input does. What that means is that, with a loop impedance that's several thousand ohms, a small amount of capacitance can make a noticeable difference in high frequency response by creating an RC (Resistance/Capacitance) filter that "rolls off" the highs just like the tone control in a guitar. Some audio cable doesn't even LIST a capacitance value; those that do are all over the map - really good audio cable may have as little as 15-20 picofarads per foot of capacitance, 110 ohm AES digital audio cable has typically only 10-15 pF per foot, and lots of high end mic cable has up to 60-80 pF per foot. The longer your cables, the more this matters - enough capacitance over a fairly long cable run can make you turn up the treble control by maybe 10-12 dB; when you do that, you also "turn up" any high frequency noise, such as hiss, so your sound suffers from bad signal-to-noise. Do this with ALL the channels in a 24 input board, and it gets unbelievably bad. This is why more people are using the AES digital cable to wire semi-pro studios - when you have 1000 feet or more of cable, all that extra capacitance can be very noticeable, and not in a GOOD way. OK, I lied; this isn't quick or short at all... :cry: Just some quick things - solder. I try ONLY to use Eutectic solder - it has a slightly lower melting point but most importantly, Eutectic solder's components (tin and lead) both melt at the exact same temperature so the solder has a very narrow "pasty" range, which means that your joints will have much less likelihood of failing due to physical movement during the phase change from liquid to solid. The following page explains this better - http://www.tpub.com/content/constructio ... 50_118.htm Soldering iron - for almost all cable work, a 40 watt iron is plenty - if you can afford a temperature controlled soldering station, your cables will be more consistent and reliable. I find that a 700 degree chisel tip works best for almost everything. Cleaning the tip before every joint makes things go smoother and more reliable.
