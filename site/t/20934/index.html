Hi there "logjamparty", and Welcome! :)
(I don't have a dB meter, . . .
You really should get one! Without that, how can you determine what your isolation needs are? And without knowing what your isolation needs are, how can you determine what type of wall, floor, ceiling, window, door, or HVAC system you need to build? What dimensions / thickness / density would you need? No way to know, if you don't first know what your isolation needs are. And without a sound level meter, how will you be able to calibrate your system? How will you set your levels correctly? You can't do it by ear, since no human ear has that capability. And how will you calibrate REW, in order to analyze your empty room acoustics, so that you can determine what treatment is needed? If you don't calibrate REW, you won't have accurate results on which your treatment can be designed... etc. You need a meter. They are not expensive. A decent one will cost you around US$ 100 on eBay or Amazon. Make sure it has both A and C weighting, and also both slow and fast response. Stay away form the cheap Chinese junk toys that go for under US$ 50.

Nope. You can't. I often use the analogy that isolating a room is very similar to building an aquarium. With a studio, you don't want the sound to leak out. With an aquarium, you don't want the water to leak out. Building a studio where most sides are really good but one side is very weak isolation, is the same as building an aquarium with three glass walls and one made of carpet... Isolation is all or nothing. If you build a room where the walls are isolated to 50 dB but hte ceiling is only isolated to 30 dB, then the entire studio is only isolated to 30 dB, and you wasted a lot of time, money and effort on those walls: they make no difference at all.
-One window and one door. Not sure if I'll frame over the window. It would be cheaper than building an acoustic window, or ordering one, but I think I may miss the sunlight.
An window that isolates is fairly easy to build: All you need is one pane of glass in each leaf, supported in a suitable frame. The glass should ideally be laminated glass, but at the very least it must have the same surface density as the leaf that it is in.
-Heating and cooling from a Mitsubishi mini split
And your ventilation system?...? Having a mini-split is a good start to HVAC, as it takes care of three letters, but you are still missing the "V"... which is sort of an important letter, because it is what keeps you alive... no ventilation = no oxygen, and even more important, no removal of the deadly CO2 buildup...
I'm gonna need fresh air, clearly.
I'm looking for design ideas that will let me exchange air,
Do it the normal way: Calculate what air flow rate you need (volume), what air flow speed you need (velocity), and that will give you the duct size. Then design a silencer box for each point where a duct goes through a leaf, and make sure the internal cross section of each silencer box changes suddenly by a factor of at least 2 at the entrance and exit, and that the final run to the registers in the room is long enough to ensure that there is minimal turbulence in the airflow at the registers. Then calculate your total static pressure, and find a fan that can move that volume of air at that speed when facing that static pressure. And make sure that the surface density of the silencer box walls is sufficient to provide the isolation level that you need, which you can calculate onces you have your sound level meter and have taken the necessary readings... :)
but I'll be designing and building the fresh air.
That covers the basics, above. It all starts out with a sound level meter...
-Should the fan be on the interior, or do they make all weather fans so it can be on the exterior?
No, and Yes. Put your fan outside, as far away from the studio as possible, and get one that is designed to be used outside. Fantech has some decent ones.
-Can I add some kind of filtration on the supply side?
Not only CAN you, but you MUST! You will need a grill that prevents leaves, feathers, papers and large things from being sucked in, plus a mesh of some type that prevents bugs from getting in, plus a filter that stops dust, pollen, and suchlike from getting in. Inspect / clean / replace regularly.
-Where is the smartest place to vent from? I'm guessing the ceiling to eliminate hot air.
I normally bring in fresh air at the rear of the room, in the ceiling, facing straight down, then put the exhaust registers at the front of the room, roughly above the speakers. That produces air flow throughout the entire room, ensuring good mixing and good replacement rate.
Plus, it seems like an ERV would just run and be really loud (from what I've read they need to be installed in a conditioned space, which eliminated my ideas of building an enclosure for it outside and running ducts inside.)
Not if you design the system carefully. You might want to consider an HRV instead, which is cheaper, simpler, less complicated, and the only moving part is the fans. Not quite as efficient, but still does a very useful job. ERV's are only useful if you have the right humidity conditions.
