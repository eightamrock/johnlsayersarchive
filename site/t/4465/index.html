sharward4 October 2005#2
benny wrote:
I'm making the walls using a layer of gyprock, insulating with rockwool and then a layer of MDF.
I think you mean leaf instead of layer. See the definitions.
Should I try and cram as much rockwool in as possible, or should I put in an amount that fits in easily.
I believe you want slight compression so that you dampen each side, and you don't compress so tightly that the assembly acts as one leaf, and you don't want less than the thickness of the cavity because there may be a "partial third leaf effect."
I read in the reference section that "if insulation is used it must be open-celled or it will act as an additional leaf" I don't know what open-celled means?
Open celled means air can pass through it. A sponge is open celled. In the mass-air-mass equation, insulation that air can flow through is considered "air." The "definitions" link above will clarify this. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward4 October 2005#5
Yeah, what he said. ;) Basically, ideally, you want enough insulation to be lightly compressed on each side of the wall. That's enough to dampen each side without it being so tight that the wall acts as one. If you do too little insulation -- meaning less than the depth of the air cavity, depending on the type of insulation you are using, you could end up with the insulation itself acting a bit like a third leaf. At least that's how I have come to understand it. If anyone is smarter than I am about this, please -- I am extending my toes and gritting my teeth! ;) --Keith :mrgreen:
knightfly4 October 2005#6
Pretty much correct - for the insides of walls, using the standard "fluffy stuff" is the least expensive and (hardest to screw up) way to go, and gives as good results as the expensive rigid fiberglass, rockwool or mineral wools. For an inside construction like a vocal booth, it's best to use NON-faced batts. The "fluffy stuff", or "pink", can protrude beyond studs on one side by up to an inch or so before it will be too compressed. For ceilings, 4-6" is enough REGARDLESS of the air gap - gravity is your friend here because it will keep the insulation in contact with the inner ceiling panels for best damping. Some good general points - http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.p ... 280e1d399c HTH... Steve

Keith: Doing furhter research I dug up [url=http://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/publications/en/rh-pr/tech/02-108.html]Technical Series 02-108 [/url] AKA Noise Isolation Provided by Gypsum Board Partitions My statements were wrong. The relevant bullets from the referenced document are
Generally, at low frequencies, the increase in the transmission loss of a partition obtained by adding a sound absorptive material inside its cavity is equivalent regardless of the material used. Above 250 Hz, mineral fiber and blown cellulose give the best results; mineral fiber insulation provides slightly better transmission losses than glass fiber, especially around the critical frequency. Also, in the case of glass fiber, a greater transmission loss can be achieved by using denser batts
and
With the exception of sprayed cellulose, the best transmission losses were obtained when the entire cavity of the partitions were filled with a sound absorptive material. When the entire cavity is filled, caution must be taken not to use a material that is too dense, otherwise a mechanical coupling could occur between the two sides of the partition which could result in a degradation of the sound isolating performance of the partition, as was observed in the case of a partition whose cavity was filled with sprayedon cellulose.
We now have referenced test data for filling cavities completely. Andre
