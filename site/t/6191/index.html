sharward11 May 2006#2
Very interesting and (other than the craw-digging you're going to have to do) encouraging news, 14K! I will say, however, that the "extreme" example your inspector cited (" unless I used concrete & rebar on the 2nd floor") is actually not out of the realm of what would have been the possibilities. There is no shortage of discussion on this forum about the pros and cons of floating a concrete slab for isolation. Floating a concrete slab generally requires two concrete slabs of up to 6" in thickness for a single floor. :shock: So, OK, we know you don't have that option. (Although, truth be told, if you had known ahead of time that it would be the best option for your situation, you very well may have chosen to go that route, and thus you would have engineered your building differently.) So, the ultimate (and difficult and expensive) solution is not possible now. But what is possible exactly? Also, although you won't necessarily be adding "roof" load bearing walls to your design, you very well may have load bearing inner walls in your space, if you mount heavy ceilings on those walls. With your (presumably) vented attic, you would need to have two ceilings in order have two dense, air-tight leaves overhead. The roof would act as a third leaf, with the air inside the act acting the spring between your upper ceiling and the roof, but I would think and hope that the vents in the attic, along with its large volume of air, would mitigate the dreaded third leaf effect. At any point did you tell your inspector that these extra walls you're contemplating (and the ceilings they'll probably support) have multiple layers of drywall on them (like, three or four on each side)? 5/8" drywall weighs about 2.3 pounds per square foot. A small wall that is 9' x 10' (90 square feet) with four layers of drywall (9.2 pounds per square foot) weighs about 828 pounds for the drywall alone (i.e., not counting framing lumber, or any ceilings)! (Obvioiusly, using two layers of drywall with Green Glue would cut the weight in half.) Now these walls may not seem very heavy when you calculate the total amount of square footage of room space they'll cover, but when you consider the fact that their footprints will measure over 150 pounds per square foot, you cannot afford to assume that the trusses and the rest of the framing will hold. You have to prove it, even if to nobody but yourself (if the inspector doesn't care or doesn't think he has to care). So, in short, while I'm glad your inspector is giving you the thumbs up, it seems to me that he may have given you his approval without all the facts necessary to do so. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward11 May 2006#4
Your goal should not necessarily be to increase the strength of the building to some kind of maximum state and thus be assured that whatever you build on/in it will hold. It should be to measure and calculate everything so that you know how much it will weigh, and then verify that the structure will hold. If that verification fails (it probably won't -- but just in case it does), then you'll need to either lighten the load or increase the strength. Only cross the unlikely "it won't hold" bridge when and if you actually get to it (on paper, of course). Analogy: You're going to drive from point A to point B and there is noplace to fill your gas tank along the way. Bad plan: Fill the tank before you leave and assume you'll make it all the way across. Good plan: Determine actual engine efficiency in MPG. Determine capacity of tank. Multiple capacity by efficiency for estimated maximum range. Determine distance between points A and B. Verify that distance is less than range. If yes, fill up and drive. If not, address challenge (carry extra gas outside of tank, use more efficient car for trip, etc.). Makes sense? --Keith :mrgreen:

14K, Hey remember above and beyond anything your asking BI, which he approves or doesn’t, or anything we on the forum say, THE MOST IMPORTANT NUMBERS YOU NEED ARE: PSF (pounds per square foot) dead (structure weight) and live (people and gear) load that these truss/joists will have. This is why Sharward is repetitively and rightfully posting about weight. :D With the PSF dead and live load info (I believe you were waiting for this info from engineer?) you can then add up your structure weight for dead load (how much structure mass you can use for isolation and acoustics) and your live load (you are going to need to get a good estimate weight of your gear, albums, & amount of people that will be on 2nd floor at once) and then proceed to how you are going to isolate Moving from 24” OC to 12” OC may not actually double the load.
would I have been able to tell the difference if I had not applied all the treatment
YES you will, and as you build each layer, you can do “by ear” tests by cranking a boom box inside the room and go outside to listen, take a note of what you here maybe even playback the same track at each test. Also you can get a DB meter from Radio Shack to aid your testing. And could I ask that maybe you try and keep posting your questions, pics, info and explanations of how you’re coming to make your decisions to a single thread. I don’t think this forum has seen a 2nd story sound isolated room done successfully built on wood frame. How ever successfully you accomplish sound isolation in your situation will serve as a learning tool for future forum users, just like you and I both have learned from previous threads of forum users.
