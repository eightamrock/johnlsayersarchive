I like Lou's layout a lot, but I would modify it slightly in your case, to make better use of space. I would aim for a proper RFZ design, so the front part of the side walls should be angled more steeply than at present to attain that, but then to avoid wasting space and having a hugely wide rear end, I would straighten out the rear section of the side walls, to have the parallel or near parallel, also eliminating the diagonal cutoffs in the rear corners. The rest of the layout looks fine, and both your live room and vocal booth will end up with more floor area like that. That would be my suggestion. Others might have different opinions. A possible alternative is a corner-control room, placed across the diagonal at one end, but I'm not sure if you have enough space for that. One other point here: You did mention that the roof will slope over the entire studio, but if your control room ceiling slopes, then it should only ever slope upwards from front to back (lower over the speakers, higher over the sofa). The CR ceiling should never slop sideways, nor the other way: worst case, it should be flat. So you should take that into account when you position your layout within the available space. The other rooms don't really matter, and their ceilings can slope any way that makes sense, but the control room is critical.
I am planning a wall within a wall type construction but my first big question is that since sound isolation in the rear is not a big deal because no one is back there, can I forgo the double wall facing the back which is directed down a long valley, because I intend to have 2 big 60” windows on that wall (Live Room) for the view and would hate to have to double them up.
OK, there are two issues here: 1) Unfortunately, isolation is an "all or nothing" proposition: you need to isolate the entire room to the same level, including all four walls, the ceiling, the floor, the doors, the windows, the HVAC system, and the electrical system. Your overall isolation is only as good as the weakest link. An analogy that I often use is that of an aquarium: If you want to build an aquarium that holds water, you need glass on all sides, and good seals. If, instead of using glass, you try to put paper on one side, cardboard on another, and a piece of carpet on the bottom, then obviously the aquarium will not hold water. Sound is like water in many ways, and just as water will "spill out" of the weakest side of your aquarium, so to sound will "spill out" of the weakest side of your room. And just like the water splashing around everywhere once it gets out, sound does the same. If it gets out on one side if your room, then it will be heard around all the other sides too, especially in low frequencies. High frequencies are more directional, radiating out in straight lines, but low frequencies will simple "wrap around" objects that are smaller than the wavelength of the sound. And since low frequency sounds have wavelengths measured in dozens of feet, your garage is actually "small" as far low frequencies are concerned. So building a "room-in-a-room" studio with one wall missing is somewhat like building an aquarium with the back wall made of cardboard... 2) However, it all depends on how much isolation you need, in terms of decibels. Since you don't seem to need a lot of isolation, perhaps a single well-designed window would provide enough isolation, especially if it is in the control room, where levels will be lower than in the live room. That MIGHT be a possibility. For example, you could isolate the live room very well, but not isolate the control room so well. However, you did say that you want the window in the live room, so this might not be feasible. The issue is this: even though you are not worried about disturbing neighbors, there might still be serious problems going the other way: There are numerous things that could wreck your best take, such as wind, rain, hail, thunder, aircraft / helicopters flying overhead, cars arriving / leaving, phones ringing, radios / TV's playing, people talking outside, dogs barking, other animal sounds, lawnmower, washing machine, vacuum cleaner, toilet flushing, water in pipes, etc. the more you think, the more possible noise sources you can come up with, and any one of those could trash a great tracking session, if you don't have good isolation. So those are the two issues that you should be considering, in deciding if you should isolate the live room or not. - Stuart -

Soundman20208 March 2013#6
What would be the correct way to determine the proper angle for the monitors? I've read different approaches.
The approach that matters most is the one recommended by the real experts, such as the ITU, the EBU, the AES, Dolby, and others. They all agree on this, so it's pretty much the standard and accepted "best practice". Based on their published specifications, and a few other tidbits of useful acoustic theory. this is what I suggest: Set your listening position on the center line (splitting left from right) of the room, about 38% of the distance from front wall to back wall. (NOTE! 38% is not a magic number that you must hit! It is a guideline, just a starting point). So set your chair such that your ears will be at that 38% point, and will also be 1.2 me above the floor. Now set up your speakers, angled inwards at 30°, so that the imaginary acoustic axis is also located 1.2 m above the floor, and aimed just past your ear. In other words, the left speaker will be pointing just a bit to the left of your left ear, and the right speaker will be pointing just a bit to the right of your right ear. Done like that, the imaginary acoustic axes will intersect about 20 cm behind your head, at an angle of 60°. NOTE: This is the acoustic axes that we are talking about here! Not the top or bottom of the speaker, and not the center of the tweeter. The manufacturer of your speakers should be able to tell you exactly where that axis is on your speaker. If not, there are methods for "guesstimating" it. That is the perfect geometry, in theory, but real-world rooms are not perfect, so you should try "tweaking" these a bit. Do that by listening to music that you know very well using this setup, and try moving things around a little, to see if you can find a better spot. So move the speakers a bit further apart / closer together, adjust their angle a few degrees either way, and slide your chair back and forth a few cm, listening carefully to see if there is a better spot.
Also, would you recommend them to be mounted higher and tilted down and in or at ear level and just tilted inward?
You can, but there are limits. They should never be tilted down more than 7° normally, or 10° absolute maximum. More than that, and you start getting strange psycho-acoustic effects. It also makes it a lot more complicated to build the soffits, of the speakers are tilted down...
when people come in to the control room to listen, if the monitors are tilted in for the mix engineer, won't that affect what the guests are hearing in the back, or does it just not really matter.
Yes it will affect what they hear, since they will be off-axis to the speakers, and yes it does matter, if the guy back there is the one paying the bills! He needs to hear things just the way you do, not different. If you have really good speakers with excellent off-axis response, then things might not be so bad, but "not-so-good" speakers might sound great where the engineer sits at the console, and not so great on the sofa at the back...
Are the monitors ever mounted so that they can swivel into a position thats better for the back of the room?
I've never seen that done, no, and I would not recommend it. But what you COULD do, is to mount a second pair of speakers up higher, in the soffit, and aim those at the sofa. - Stuart -

Soundman20205 April 2013#8
Anyway, for running cables from live room to the isolation booths, can I run them in the walls (away from power lines of course).
You sure can! The general rule is to run power wiring high up, at the top of the walls, and just drop down vertical feeds to sockets and switches, while running all low voltage down low, at the bottom of the walls, and shooting up feeds to the connection points.
I don't know if I want to try to run them through conduit in the floor or not
. Regardless of whether or not you do it embedded in the floor, I would still do all the cabling in conduit: it just make it so much easier to change things later!
Also, should the monitor soffits be angled at 30 degrees as you mentioned for my particular room, or is this dependent on room size, etc.
30° is the "standard" recommendation, but if you need to change that to make things fit, then there's no problem. As long as you don't go beyond 45°, max, or less than about 20°, then you should be OK. Of course, changing the angle also changes the size and shape of the sweet spot, so do be aware of that.
Do the windows seem OK and should the window for the vocal booth be smaller?
I love big windows! Sight lines are important for studios. However, you need to check that the glass is not at a first reflection point, because if it is then you'll be covering it up with absorption... - Stuart -
Gregdx724 April 2013#9
Stuart, Quick question. Getting close to beginning construction and the question came up, would it matter if we do standard stick build construction, or went with a metal building type construction? Does it matter for isolation, etc.?
Soundman202024 April 2013#10
Either will work, if designed and built correctly. The key issues are mass, damping, and air gap: metal has mass, so does drywall. Drywall is self-damping, to a certain extent, whereas metal "rings". But you can use either, as long as the construction takes into account the difference between materials, and as long as you have enough mass on each leaf, with a big enough gap between them. I would think that metal would turn out to be more expensive, considering the thickness you'd need to get the mass. - Stuart -
