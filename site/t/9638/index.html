I still need to start a construction thread but already I’ve got my first goof up. I’ve never noticed or paid much attention myself but are some brands of drywall more prone to dusting than others? How is everyone prepping the edges? Thanks, Chris

Dan Fitzpatrick24 October 2007#12
i've had this same thing happen, here and there, but i haven't worried too much about it. i just add some more caulk into the crack, seems to fix the problem. you should wait a few days if you can between layers and inspect for this if you can. if you can't wait, then you should just rely on the redundancy of multiple layers to take care of you. unless you have an awful lot of this, it is unlikely to have two failures right on top of each other. finally, i think this is related to not having enough caulk in the joint. i always try to run my finger into the joint to push the caulk in ... also this pushes it against the rough edges of the board giving a better seal. of course with your nice 1/4 inch gaps this isn't realistic. but i would overfill a bit anyway and try to push the caulk in as much as you can. also the caulk will stick good to the paper which will help keep the seal closed, so if you have a littel caulk over flow onto the paper that is all good.
TomVan24 October 2007#13
Hey guys if I can add a little to this thread. Caulking, glazing and joint installation with any elastomeric compound you have a rule of thumb that applies. Not that in the field it is a perfect situation every time but to understand the material and how it is supposed to function will help. Shrinkage will occur in almost any material ( ie; wood, sheet rock mud)that is wet or damp when installed. The acoustic caulks range of solid content can be 50-100%. What this means is if the material is 50% solids that when it is in its cured/dry state, 50% of your application has dehyrated or left the compound during its curing process. And most materials will tell you maximun thickness and maximm width to install. Because there is an elongation property of the material. It can only stretch so far until something gives.....the Bond line or the material iself.... In most cases and mine too. The wood itself was damp during construction. It shrunk ever so slightly that something had to give. I had to recaulk a few joints just for that reason. And I did cut a few out that were slight and the larger ones I just recaulked between the gap. Rule of thumb is half the depth of the width for proper elongation to happen. I tried an illustration but it was hard to draw and explain. But here is a way to understand elongation better. Take a piece of rubber that is 1" by 1/2" and stretch it. Now take a piece of rubber 1" by 1" and try and stretch that. Then you can understand that the depth is important to the width for movement and shrinkage Now having said all that .....if your materials..wood, sheetrock are all dryed in you can caulk as deep as you like as long as you have non to little movement. Peace Tom
