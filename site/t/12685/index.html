Steele Audio3 June 2009#11
BAKroom3, Actually, those glass doors are not going to reflect back to the main mix position. If you look at the angle the speaker has to go to hit the glass, if I think about it like a pool shot, that first reflection is going to go back behind the head of the mixer. This is because of the way the walls are splayed outward. I like billiards, and that's one good way to look at the reflections on glass. Second and third reflections will be less of a problem, particularly if you have proper absorbtion, and especially since this design has so much bass trapping in the rear. The glass may affect the listening position in the back, but the average person sitting there won't know the difference, and the person in the mix seat is where you want that sweet spot. I look forward to seeing what you do with the space, I'm a bit jealous that you can actually make it out of a seperate buildling. Peace, Bryan
Soundman20203 June 2009#12
if I think about it like a pool shot, that first reflection is going to go back behind the head of the mixer.
Well, that's partly true for high frequencies, where the wavelengths are small: Those are indeed highly directional, and can be represented as rays. But its a bit more complex for mids and lows, and even highs tend to spread out across a spherical or cylindrical wave front. In addition, the soffits on that sketch appear to be at the wrong angle: They look like they are at 45°, but it should be 30° to get the correct speaker / mix position geometry, which means the speakers will be pointing much closer to the center of the rear wall, or roughly at the middle of the glass. That seems to send the reflections pretty much right at the mix position. I reckon there will be bad stuff coming of that glass, right back at the engineer's ears, messing up the stereo imaging, making the sound muddy, and perhaps even causing comb filtering. From what I've learned, you don't want a large hard reflective surface behind you. It should either be absorbent (in a small room) or you should have diffusers of some type back there (in a larger room), or maybe a combination of diffusers and slot resonators, absorption and slots, etc.. The bass traps don't seem to be very large either: If those glass doors weren't there, they could by much larger. I don't recall ever seeing a successful pro studio with such a large hard reflective surface directly behind the mix position, without any treatment on it. That's not to say that there aren't any studios like that: There might well be some, but I don't think I've ever seen one. And since they are so scarce, there must be a good reason for that! In any event, all of those walls need to be doubled up: The sketch shows single-leaf walls, but in reality they will have to be two-leaf, of course, which means twice the number of doors, twice the price. If that were my room, I think I'd go with smaller swinging doors (two of them, back to back, one for each leaf), much like Rod suggests in his book, and with treatment of some kind on the door itself, as well as on the entire back wall, to control reflections Or turn the entire room around, and do it like Jon suggested.... :) Anyway, that's just my $ 0.02. - Stuart -

And that way i could put proper bass trappings and diffusors on the back wall then as far as the windows from the control room into the live room would it be possible to maybe move them forward or rearward a little to prevent the stereo imaging from being compromised?
Yup! :) John's design is the best way to do it. Flip the room like he has it, so that your mis position is facing the door, and the rear of the room is against the wall, where you can treat it properly. Also note that John did pocket doors on that wall, which I imagine he did in order to get enough room to to the soffits and trapping in those front corners. Another option might be to do back-to-back swinging doors with large glass windows, if you really do need to see into your foyer / rest area. Of just plan solid doors if you don't really need to see in there. - Stuart -
