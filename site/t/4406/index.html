sharward23 September 2005#7
Nice try. :roll: No, this is not a California-specific code. According to UBC 310.6.1, minimum ceiling height is 7’6” in habitable rooms and 7’0” in kitchens, halls, bathrooms, and toilet compartments. Sloped ceiling must be 7’6” for a minimum of 50% of room areas. As soon as a room gets air conditioning/heating, it becomes habitable. There might be exceptions for basements and/or at the local level. A friend of mine once converted a small hose into office space in Reno, Nevada (i.e., not California) -- he finished a basement with pretty low clearance. He had to move a lot of plumbing and wiring in order to keep his ceiling heights in compliance with code and to get a final sign-off from the building department. If the bottom of the ceiling joists are 7 feet off the floor, then technically he may not be able to even add one layer of gypsum to the bottom of the joists without RC. :shock: It's up to Big3rd to check with his local building department offiicials to determine what he is permitted to do legally and safely. His town may not use UBC -- he has yet to tell us where he lives.

sharward19 November 2005#15
Please, please, please do more research on floating floors. It is so often assumed to be "a given" that they are required. Yes, there are benefits if done correctly. However, it is very difficult to do it right, even if you know exactly what you're doing. Unfortunately, there are many opportunities for error, and in those cases your results will be worse than if you hadn't floated a floor at all! :shock: I'm not anti-floating... But I am anti-floating when it's clear that the floater doesn't have the necessary grasp to be successful... Which is a virtual guarantee of failure. Oh, and in case it isn't obvious, the "carpet padding" as a hard wood floor elastomer is a terrible idea. :( I would go into more detail about what is involved in properly floating a floor/room, but I'm short on time, and it's been covered here (and on StudioTips) ad infitinitum. I hope this doesn't come across as too harsh or unsupportive. I really do want to see you succeed in your project. :) It's just that sometimes it takes a little slap upside the head to get a point across. ;-) --Keith :mrgreen:
sharward19 November 2005#16
A bit of follow-up... Here's a quote from Paul Woodlock over at StudioTips:
Paul wrote:
After designing a room within a room with high mass walls, the weak link then becomes the floor. So for those ( Like me ) who need a LOT of soundproofing the floating floor is desirable. The cons of a floating floor is that it's a] expensive, b] Heavy, which usually means ground floor ( or basements ) only, and c] must be calculated properly. . . . A lot of the methods don't really work properly. Sadly floating floors are the victim of misinformation, and there's been many floating floors built in DIY studios ( and even some pro studios ) that a] are pointless or b] don't work. or c] both. (Source)
If you don't already know who Paul is, spend a few days reading his studio build diary, and you'll have a much better appreciation of what it takes to float a floor properly. 8)
