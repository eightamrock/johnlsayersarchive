knightfly28 March 2006#2
If you can find a prefab that will GUARANTEE you 60 dB @ 50 hZ and STC 80 or better, I'd buy it. When the prefab people you talk to get finished swallowing their tongues at that request, you should probably build. You're talking Sylomer-floated 6" concrete slab, 6" minimum concrete outer shell, about 18" air gap minimum, and probably 3-4 layers of 5/8 gypsum wallboard for inner leaf with Green Glue on at least one or two layers, MINIMUM - double Overly door sound lock, (about $14,000 just for that) de-coupled conduits, and a mass-air-mass ceiling/roof that can match those specs. That's just off the top of my head, but should be close to what you'll need for what you want to do. Cost should run between $50-$65K for a 270 sf CR... Steve

len-morgan28 March 2006#4
This may sound like a stupid question but WHY do you need 120 dB of isolation? Are you going to be doing a final mix WHILE the concert is in progress? I have been going through this very dilema with my studio (which Steve has been most gracious in helping me sort out!). My speaker baffle walls were going to be too small and in order to make them big enough, we'd have to cut the window down to about 1 foot wide. But then we got to thinking that I only need see out the window when I'm tracking, and I only need the accurate reproduction when I'm mixing (i.e., AFTER the tracking is done). The solution is a fold out wall on either side of the window that is only pulled out for the mixing process. So what I'm getting out is think about not only what you want to do in the control room, but WHEN. You'll have different requirements. Knightfly's suggestions are right on if you HAVE to do both jobs at the same time. Your other alternative is to do something like you see while waiting in line at Disneyland: A visible control room with lots of blinky lights and maybe a maniquin (sp?) on a motor that LOOKS like an active control room, and then put the real one somewhere where isolation is a little easier to achieve. len

Personally, I'd be willing to pay my money and have you mail it to me the next day. Have you considered the legal ramifications of reproducing copyrighted materials without the proper licensing? A BMI or ASCAP license only covers the PERFORMANCE of the music. Reproduction (a "mechanical" license) is another entirely separate set of licenses (currently about .085/song/copy depending on the length of the song). If you have 10 songs on a CD (.80/CD) and distribute say 1000 of them, you're looking at $800 in licensing fees. Hopefully, you've thought this all out already.

That is actually an insane statement........... you apparently have no idea what you're asking here. One of the quietest facilities on the planet is Galaxy, and it doesn't have that level of isolation. To acheive what you're talking about - you would need a floating concrete bunker - elevated concrete slab on top of massive springs - with concrete walls and ceiling - and then inside that you would have to construct your finished room. There isn't a door manufacturer on the planet that makes a door anywhere near those levels of isolation (not even with double door assemblies) so you're talking contracting one of them to design special doors for your use. If you started today with design - you couldn't complete design in 3 months - never mind complete design, construct and open next month.
So my questions are. . . 1) Generally, any thoughts one way or the other?
First and formost - hire a studio architect - someone who only does that for a living.
2) Price difference. (I know the uber-booths are about 60k)
Seeing as an ubber-booth can't acheive your goals (assuming your goals are what you think they are) - their cost is irrevelant............
3) Amount of time it takes to build a room from ground up? (Including electrical, venting, ect) 4 weeks? 4 months? 1 year?
It all depends on the final design - with a floating bunker (if you could get the design done in 4 months) - then 2 weeks of bidding and contract award - two more weeks for the contractor to mobolize and provide shop drawings - one week for shop approvals - one more week to get the reinforcing onsite and form - then pour. Now you wait at least 28 days of curing before you can begin the wall sections.......... 56 if the engineer of record doesn't like the break results. And believe me when I tell you that this timeline is more than perfect execution of the work - you'll have to pay big bucks for a fantastic construction manager to pull this off that smoothly......... So we are 6 months and a week and you now have your isolated slab to work on. You'll be another 3 weeks for walls - and then 2 more weeks to pour the roof - lots of shoring inside the room to carry the formwork - and then at least another 28 days before you can remove the formwork and any shoring...... That now places your schedule at 8 months one week to get to the point where you have a room you can begin framing in. From there you can probably complete the work within 3 to 4 months depending on final design - one week for testing and final treatments and you're ready to open. So let's say 13 months to a finished room. AND - this is all assuming that your existing slab can handle the load (which is possible - but not highly probable) - removing the existing and reinforcing it to get the job done will add another 2 to 2 1/2 months to the job. So possibly 15 to 16 months. As far as cost goes - your bet is as good as mine -

Best advice I can give is slow down - first find the architect who specializes in these rooms - let him/her guide you through what it is you really REALLY need for isolation, pay him to oversee the construction - pay him to sit in on pre-bid meetings so that the contractors bidding understand exactly what is required of them in the performance of their work - and then pay him to inspect that work every step of the way to verify that the owner is getting exactly what is in the design package. At least this will bring some reality into the process. Sincerely, Rod
