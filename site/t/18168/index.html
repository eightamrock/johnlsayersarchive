Our plan is to lay 2 seperate foundations (1 for live room, one for control room), build a timber frame structure around the entire size, then build sub rooms inside this on the seperate foundations
That is one way to do it, if you need extreme levels of isolation. But you will need three sets of foundations, not two. You need one foundation for each room, plus one for the outer-leaf that encloses them. The foundations need to be dimensioned correctly to support the amount of mass that will be needed for each room, and also laid correctly to ensure maximum damping and maximum isolation. We're planning on running piping between the two rooms for cabling and electrical lines etc..
Fine, but your conduit (the correct name for electrical "pipe") also has to be isolated between the rooms. If not, it becomes a flanking path, and reduces isolation.
Ive been giving a good contact for some high density 50mm rockwool that we were going to use to soundproof the rooms. I've been told by a friend who has built studio and used this stuff that the 50mm high density rockwool amount to apprx 100-150mm standard rockwool, so we are probably going to double up on this.
Well, there you are getting into hype and hearsay. The truth is that "high density" rockwool is wrong for good bass absorption. The optimal density for rockwooll (and other mineral wool products) is 48 kg/m3, which is about 3 PSF. However, if you choose to use fiberglass insulation, instead of mineral wool, then the optimum density is about 30 kg/m3, which is about 2 PSF. For details on why this is the optimum density, take a look at the Wyle 73 paper, or also take a look at IR-761. The evidence is conclusive. Those documents also show that higher density is worse for bass absorption and better for high frequency absorption, while lower density is the reverse: better for bass absorption and worse for high frequency absorption. The best balance is at the densities I mentioned. Your friend is also not correct about thickness.Density and thickness are not interchangeable, and 50mm of one density is not the same as 100 mm of another density. In fact, it is not even the density itself that matters: It is a property known as "gas flow resistivity". That is the property of the insulation that describes how it impedes the flow of sound waves, and that is the principle property that matters. But since most manufacturers never bother measuring this parameter, it is hard to find for commonly available products. Fortunately, it turns out that, for each type of insulation, there is a rough relationship between density and gas flow resistivity. Thickness of the insulation is important, but only in relation to the total size of the air gap inside the wall. 50mm of insulation inside a 50mm air gap is fantastic. 50mm insulation inside a 500mm air gap is practically worthless. The purpose of insulation is to damp the resonance inside the cavity, and coverage is an important part of that. Thin cavities only have high frequency resonance, which can be handled by 50mm of insulation. Larger cavities have much lower frequency resonance (which is good!) but also need thicker insulation. But in both cases, the density of the insulation is what matters. Putting higher density insulation in a cavity just to save space or money will NOT improve the overall isolation. In fact, it could make it worse in low frequency isolation, and low frequencies is exactly where you need the most isolation anyway.
Also in the live room we're considering cladding the inner wall in stone,
That does work, yes, and you'll find several examples on the forum of people who have done that. Stone has good diffusion properties, and also looks good. But once again, it has to be done correctly, as part of the overall acoustic design of the room. There is a range of optimum frequency response and time-domain response for recording studios, and having either too much reflection or too much absorption is not good. They have to be balanced. So stone is fine, provided that you balance that with the treatment in the rest of the room.
Sound isolation is key to our project. We have neighbors on each side of us, but luckily none directly behind where we plan to build.
That explains why you are thinking of isolating the foundations. But the big question is: How much, exactly? In terms of decibels. That's the key to everything. If you don't know that, then you can't know what type of materials and techniques to use. Isolating to 50 or 60 dB TL is one thing, while isolation to 90 or 100 dB is major, big-time different other thing. Your first order of business is to define that number.
Im wondering if anyone could recommend some basic things we should be doing as regards the thickness of the timber framing, the gap between the outer and inner sub walls and also any other information regarding soundproofing that would relate to our project.
The basic concept is that the wall is a tuned system. It has two "leaves" to it, the "outer-leaf" which faces the outside world, then the "inner-leaf" of each room, which faces the room itself. (A "leaf" in the context of acoustic isolation is just any large, massive rigid surface with an air-gap next to it, followed by another leaf.) So the wall is a tuned two-leaf system. Technically, it is a "fully decoupled 2-leaf MSM system". "MSM" stands for "Mass-Spring-Mass", since that is the principle of physics that makes it work. The two leaves are the two "masses", and the air between them is the "spring". So it is a resonant system, and that's why it isolates: it will ONLY resonate at its natural resonant frequency (and the harmonics to a lesser extent). So the trick is to tune that resonance to a low enough frequency such that the wall isolates all higher frequencies at the necessary level. You "tune" the wall by changing the mass and the air gap. That's why you need to set your goals in terms of how many decibels of isolation you need, and what is the lowest frequency you need to isolate. Know those two factors allows you to play around with the equations that predict the resonant characteristics of the wall, and the isolation that can be expected.
We also considered building in concrete but are leaning more towards a timber frame design. If anyone has any thoughts on this that would be great!
Concrete will provide much higher isolation, but also costs much more. The key to isolation is mass, and concrete has LOTS of mass. The more mass you have in a wall, the better it will isolate. Concrete is roughly 3 to 4 times more dense that drywall, and is usually much thicker than drywall, so the surface density is many, many times that of drywall, and the isolation is a lot higher. But so is the cost.
