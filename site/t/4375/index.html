Now that we have some context, I'll take a stab at your questions:
1. best recommendations and/or link on floating ceilings and hvac options. Don't quite have all this under grasp yet.
Your best bet will be "room within a room" with an inner ceiling that sits atop your inner wall frames. If you do this, you don't need any resilient channel (a.k.a. "RC") so you don't have to "float" the ceiling -- it's already decoupled if done this way.
2. plan to sheetrock floated walls and ceilings.
You may see varying opinions on this, but I don't think you need to float if you are in a standalone building.
3. Best electronic recommendations. Basically, not a recording studio, just amatuer recording and rock/blues jamming and lots of drumming since I'm a drummer. Not stage volumes but like what you might find in a commercial rehearsal studio, maybe a little less. Plan to have an HVAC.
I'm not qualified to answer this because I know zilch about recording. All I'll say is, I really hope your budget doesn't include this aspect of your project! :shock:
4. How thick should my slab be? Is there any benefit from having a 1' wall extend up from the slab all around the slab? Does this stop low frequencies? I saw one post where a fellow had a slab like that and I couldn't tell if it was by design or just what he had to build on already.
A flat slab with deeper footings should be fine... But seek some advice from foundation experts in your neck of the woods. Make sure it's rebar reinforced and I'd say 6" thick.
5. SHould I use a layer of vinyl sheathing between sheetrock layers?
Absolutely not. Read this.
6. Does the "acoustic glue" product advertised by the folks who make quietwood and quiet rock work? is it worth it over regular caulk?
Comparing a glue to a caulk is apples:oranges. That being said... That company's products have been slammed here over and over again. Here's an example. If you're asking if acoustic sealant is better than regular caulk(s), the answer is an unequivocal "yes." However, shop carefully, as many sellers assume you are a chump -- you can usually get a good deal if you shop around. I bought 60 commercial-sized 28-ounce tubes for just over $4 each here in town. People here have purchased it for less than that.
Finally, should the layers of sheetrock be glued together? BTW, I don't plan on using quietrock or quietwood, I just saw the glue product advertised.
OK, good -- glad you're not going to try to buy those ridiculously overpriced panels. :) As far as the glue goes, I don't think you need to worry about that. You need mass-air-mass. Keep reading and studying here and you'll come to the conclusions that there really aren't any "magic" products. You just need lots of mass, some air, and lots more mass, in each direction (except maybe down in your case), and you need it to be air tight of course.
7. Building a floated wall as I've seen pics of, with an inner and outer leaf of 2"x4"'s with a 2" gap between, is it better to have an air gap or to use insulation for max sound stoppage?
Insulation doesn't really stop sound. What it does do is dampen the wall -- think of an object vibrating that you press your hand against to minimize the vibration. In the mass-air-mass equation, insulation counts as air -- it doesn't make the air any more effective, it just helps the mass out a little.

Dan Fitzpatrick17 September 2005#7
nice post keith. groovemaster, you definitely do NOT want to glue your sheetrock panels together, because this will lower the resonant frequency (aka coincidence frequency) of the assembly. the coincidence frequency is where the panel itself performs most poorly (deviates from normal straight-slope mass law). unglued panels = several, high-frequency coincidence frequencies glued panels = one, lower-frequency coincidence frequency higher frequency is better because in general any wall performs better at high frequencies.
sharward wrote:

keith, as far as i know, this isn't quite right. although i'm no expert either! :) what keith says is true about how insulation helps the wall. BUT, insulation DOES stop sound too. By having the sound waves travel through insulation, friction is created which converts sound to heat. This is how insulation works to absorb sound on the outside of walls and it works the same way inside walls. one of the canadian research reports i have read, i'll try to provide a link later, shows that 100% fill is better than partially filled. some people worry that the insulation will couple the leaves so they leave an inch of space. this probably isn't necessary IMO unless you are REALLY packing the insulation in and makes the wall less fire-resistant. Brian Ravnaas has indicated that, as a rule of thumb, every inch of insulation added to the inside of a wall is like adding a virtual inch of extra space between leaves. so if you fill the cavity you have in effect doubled the space between your leaves, which lowers the mass-air-mass resonance (which is VERY good). Hopefully i'm not misquoting Brian here ... i really should look up the post on studiotips where he told me that. but anyway, that is how Brian's resonance calculator works. actually as i recall the basic equation for calclulating mass-air-mass resonance uses a different constant depending on whether insulation is used or not.
