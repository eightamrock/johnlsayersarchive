Soundman202010 February 2009#4
Not sure if I can help much, but here are some "non-expert" comments that might point you in the right direction for further research:
The biggest wall is next to earth (not electrical i really mean earth, like soil, it's an underground floor) and the ceiling is also under earth.
I understand that you mean the room is actually underground? That there is nothing on the other side of that long wall, except many meters of dirt? And how far underground is the roof? I'm guessing that it is also a couple of meters of dirt on top of that roof, and nothing else? If so, then you won't need to isolate those walls at all, I think. Concrete damped by dirt is a great isolator! So you will only need to worry about isolation for the other two walls.
the walls are brick and cement, and the floors and ceiling are reinforced concrete.
How thick? The concrete is probably pretty thick, I'd guess, since it is obviously a large building, but how thick are those bricks? Are they rendered (plastered) on one side= Both sides? Painted? How good is the brickwork? I mean, is the mortar in the joints neat, consistent, even, etc, or is it poorly done with gaps, holes, "bubbles"?
The other 20% of the time it will be used for recording purposes.
When you say "recording purposes", do you mean tracking only, or also mixing? In other words, will you need to use it as a control room for critical listening / mixing?
for reference, on an iphone decibel meter app, we hit an averge 100dBs on the loudest parts of our rehearsals.
Either you have a very quiet drummer, or the iphone SPL meter isn't very accurate! I normally get around 110 dB just from the drums alone, played normally, and up to 115 or more played loud. Can you get a more accurate meter and measure again? Also, measure both inside the room and outside the room with the door closed, with everyone playing as loudly as they can. That will tell you how much isolation you already have, and how much more you need to get. Also measure the average inside and outside levels when nobody is playing: just ambient noise.
When recording (separate instruments, one track at a time) I can bring back the some reflection by hanging some mdf or drywall or whatever hard surface to make the room "more live"
You can probably do that by building some gobo's on wheels, or even hinged to the walls.
- I know the proportions for rectangular rooms. But in a triangular room, how high should my ceiling be?
More volume is generally better, within reason. 4m is a pretty good height! You have plenty of room for treatment up there.
- Is there any interest in building a ceiling that is not flat?
Yes. No. :) Seriously, it depends on what you want and what you have. Since you want a dead space, and you have "meters of earth above concrete" for your roof, I'd say that you probably don't even need a ceiling, and can just that space for treatment. Keep the floor as concrete, or maybe lay laminate floor, but don't carpet it. You can always use rugs if you need to deaden the floor a bit.
- am i right in my assumption that i should have a dead room?
I would not want my room totally dead: it sounds awful, and is fatiguing to the ears. If you want to track in there, then I think I'd try to keep it a little bit live. Maybe treat the drum area heavily, but less treatment for the rest.
- how much would all this cost me, if i were to build it myself with the help of my bandmates? - how long would it take to build it (it would be an evenings-and-weekends thing)?
Those are the tough questions that nobody can answer for you! You could spend many tens of thousands of dollars and years to get it right, if you really want to, or you could do it in months for just a couple of thousand. Are you going to buy read-made acoustic treatment from a manufacturer, or are you going to build it yourself? How perfect do you need to get it? etc. etc. I'd suggest that you first isolate those two walls, build some decent size bass traps in the corners, do some kind of large thick cloud on the ceiling, a bit of absorption on the walls, and then test the room to see how it sounds and figure out what to do next. But do consider that that's just what I would do, if it were my room! I'm no expert on treatment, and maybe others can give you better advice. Hope that helps! - Stuart -
