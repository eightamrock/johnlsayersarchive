Hi... I might be a little late on seeing this, but I'm wondering how you have decided to approach the drainage issues? Have you decided to definitely go with the interior perimeter drain system (the quasi- 'french' drain)? Have you never even considered an exterior drain tile system? I've been looking into these sorts of things myself now for a few months, and at present it seems as though the exterior system is superior... But, the question of whether the intervention is warranted is unanswered. In your case, if you are cutting the whole slab out to make it deeper, you might wanna look into a 'true' interior perimeter drain tile, placed just inside the footing, rather than the 'channel' stuff... The lower you can get the piping, the lower you can keep the water away from the slab (plus avoid any mold and humidity problems inside the basement, from water in the channel -- remember, the channel is open to the air of the basement, no? With the drain tile at the level of inside the footing, there is no opening in the basement (except for clean-out sweep 'T's', and of course the sump pit)). I know one pitch of the channel is that it can't clog, but, if you have several sweep 'T's' in line with drain tile pipes, there would be plenty of access for roto-routing, if ever needed in the future. Have you ever got any prices on how this compares to the channel method? By the way, I'm also dealing with a fairly low ceiling height; the finished height will be like 7'1" in the studio room(s), and a bit higher in the rest of the basement... IF you could dig down and gain, say, another foot in height, it might be more worth it -- but for just 3 or 4 inches, I'm not so sure. What do you think, and what have you decided or considered? sharward wrote:
For ventilation, I think you basically have two choices: having operable windows (which is obviously not a choice in a studio context), or having mechanical ventilation. Mechanical ventilation is the opposite of "passive" ventilation.
Keith, when I reference "passive" I mean passive in the sense that the fresh air system does not contain a fan dedicated for the purpose of providing fresh air. Rather - the fresh air inlet is located immediately adjacent to the return air side of the air handler unit. The static pressure of the return air will carry the fresh air into the room - you then need a relief outlet mounted in the return air duct (at least 10' from the inlet) and through the use of a barometric pressure relief damper you can maintain constant flow. This method does require the constant use of the air handler fan unit during occupied hours. Providing the required amount of air is a simple as sizing the duct for the fresh air supply. Sincerely, Rod

Keith: The thing I see right off is that -- I believe -- the drain tile should at least be below the level of the floor... If the water level gets to the height of a drain that's above the floor level, hydrostatic pressure might force water through the floors, walls, or where the floors and walls meet... Even if the drain tile can empty completely (to the sump, which would pump it off), there could still be a groundwater level that -- while below the drain -- is higher than the floor. Essentially, the drain tile wouldn't even come into play for water levels that are below it. K

I live east of Albany, 'bout 90 miles south of you - allergy-wise, if you're not already planning it I'd add a dehumidifier to the list. In the summer, I put it in my editing room and put the single 5" dia. hose out the window with the (supplied) sliding baffle/screen attachment - in winter, I close the window and just let it exhaust DRY WARM air into the room (and empty the bucket every couple of days (about 5 gallons !!?!) Prediction properly predicted 8) Santa Cruz Guitar co. is likely using that high a humidity recommendation because (I used to live there) most luthiers tend to recommend keeping their instruments at the humidity they were MADE in - that's most likely the reason for the high number. Most recommend closer to 42-43, but in any case the NW can't get there without help :( I'm not sure why your RH upstairs is that low, unless your instrumentation is wrong - gas heat actually tends to ADD humidity in most cases. I have four of these http://www.natlallergy.com/prod/1526/ac ... meter.html which are the cheapest ACCURATE RH meters I've found - at one time I placed all 4 of them side by side AWAY from outside walls and let them stabilize - they all read within 1% of each other, so either they are as accurate as claimed, or they all can tell the SAME LIES :lol: Back to my rube goldberg methods - RH is just that, RELATIVE humidity - this means that near a colder wall (assuming typical insulation) the RH will be higher than in the center of a heated room - this, because cooler air can hold LESS moisture before condensation takes place, so the RELATIVE humidity (as the same air cools) will rise. For this reason, I try to keep fans moving air (on lowest settings if I'm forced to be in the room with them) and keep RH well below 50 for health reasons - as far as instruments, most piano manufacturers ask for around 40-42%, and guitar manufacturers (not necessarily Harmony :wink: ) ask for a bit higher. Here's a bit more http://www.pjguitar.com/article2.htm I think a STABLE set of conditions is at least as important as WHAT those conditions are; if you allow your instruments to experience changes in temperature and humidity all the time, they will "work" glue joints, etc, more - the constant shifting will weaken construction and damage the wood (for musical purposes, as well as structural integrity).
