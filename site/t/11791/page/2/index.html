Wes Lachot26 February 2010#31
Maybe I can clear a couple of things up for you guys. First of all, my name is really not that hard to spell. Check out my signature. John, the wood pattern is not a slat resonator, but neither is it merely eye candy, in case you're interested in knowing more about it. What it is is a precise mathematical pattern of alternating reflective and absorptive regions developed by RPG Diffusor Systems, called a "diffsorber" pattern. It's purpose on the side walls is to widen the sweet spot and provide more realistic imaging at the rear of the control room. With regard to the ceiling coupling, sometimes we do allow this, depending upon the larger situation. In this case, noise from above was the far greater threat, and it was determined that we could not allow any breaks in the membrane for any reason whatsoever. The isolation between the tracking room and the control room is excellent, due to the combination of all the perninent factors. I cranked up "Voodoo Lounge" to an ear-splitting level in the control room and walked into the tracking room, closed the sound lock doors, and could barely hear a thing. Good soffit implimentation is the limiting factor for this to work properly, in my experience. But it is true that we make every effort to remove flanking paths if possible. Anyone who does this for a living as I do knows that the ideal is just not always possible. Cadesigner is absolutely correct that there are no absolutes. RPG makes some incredibly useful products, but to be fair, not everything they make is on the same level. I don't use their plastic products, for instance. But also to be fair, their leadership in the industry, extensive testing, willingness to do custom designs, and thousands of happy customers put them in a league of their own. The products of theirs that I use, I do so because they sound good to me - it's that simple. Creating great sounding rooms is what pays our bills. I don't have the luxury of monkeying around with stuff just because it looks cool. This business is way too competitive for that sort of shenanigans. We invite you to hear one of our rooms and see for yourself. Hope this is helpful. Carry on. -Wes

jbassino wrote:
Thanks for joining the forum Wes! Do you mind sharing what material you used for the walls? Just drywall? And how many layers? Also, could you talk more about those "diffsorbers"? Any pictures on how they are built? Thanks :D
Those walls use 3 layers of 5/8" drywall per side. One side is mounted directly on the studs, and the other is mounted on hat channel with resilient clips. This was the best solution for this particular project, but we use a variety of methods--dual wall, staggered stud, green glue, various numbers of layers, etc., depending upon the particular situation. It's not a one-size-fits-all sort of thing, and the real art of it, of course, is squeezing just the right amount of sound isolation into the available budget. The budget always seems to be just a bit smaller than it should be--know what I mean? The diffsorbers use the "Chinese remainder theorem" math developed by Trevor Cox and Peter D'Antonio. They published a paper on it a little over a decade ago. I have been using it for years, when it seems appropriate, and it does sound good. That's the acid test. The diffusion is mostly midrange diffusion, but that's the frequency range where diffusion can really help. The roughly 50% relective surface is a plus at high frequencies, and depending on what you do behind the scenes, you can get some very nice targeted bass trapping going. That's the part that's proprietary--I can't give away all of my secrets! The main point is that the side walls are a great place for diffsorbers if you're trying to extend the sweet spot toward the rear of the room. It's not always so wise to put full range diffusors there without absorption, yet too much absorption can make the room too dead. So diffsorption is just the ticket. -Wes

Most of our fabric work is done with a professional stretch fabric system. This allows us to do just about anything we want to do with the fabric. But sometimes we do build frames as well, especially if it can save our clients money. Our installers have gotten really good at both methods, even to the point where a room can be done partially in stretch fabric and partially with panels, and you really can't tell where one leaves off and the other begins. To answer your question about where bass traps are located: 1) The rear wall usually contains fairly deep broadband trapping, as well as tuned membrane or Helmholtz traps behind the scenes. These tuned traps are really what makes our rooms what they are. Over the years, building lots of rooms, we've learned which boundary issues are most important to deal with to create smooth frequency response and decay times, and we've figured out ways of treating these with tuned traps. I can only spell it out for you so much here, but if you search my posts on other forums, I've laid out the principles several times in the past. It all comes down to doing the math on which boundaries are causing nulls at which frequencies, then dealing with those specific frequencies. 2) The ceiling cloud is a big bass trap--always broadband, sometimes more. 3) The soffits, always in the rear, but often on the sides as well, contain broadband, and sometimes tuned, bass traps. 4) If the main speakers are not soffited, we'll uses front-of-room bass trapping as well. But I prefer soffit-mounting whenever possible. 5) Sometimes we work bass traps into the side walls as well. --Wes

jbassino wrote:
Bump! Wes, I had one question regarding your soffit design. I see that the soffit wall, actually is the inner leaf. Any reasons for that other than building an square front end and then build an attached framing for the soffits? (like most people do here) or am I wrong and the pictures dont really show whats going on Thanks Javier
Javier, There are a couple of reasons for this. As a starting point I usually, if space allows, like to adhere to a true RFZ design theory, utilizing very steeply angled side wall and not relying on absoption in the front end for reflection control. All of the reflection control from the engineer's position forward is accomplished with the wall (and ceiling) angles themselves, so I am not tied to a soft front end unless it's appropriate for a given client. I try to make the liveness/deadness of the front end client-specific, because different people really do have different tastes in that regard. Another reason is that I prefer to stick to a mostly purist "infinite baffle" technique. I feel the bass works best when the wall and the speakers are truly one--de-coupled, of course. -Wes
