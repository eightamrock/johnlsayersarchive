I understand that perhaps in other countries its either unusual or an offence to carpet a space - this is commonplace in the UK in rehearsal studios
It's also common place in cheap, dangerous rehearsal spaces in many other parts of the world. But "common practice" does not make it right. Number one, it is a fire hazard (as Brien pointed out), and number two it makes the places sound really bad, acoustically. Carpet has pretty lousy acoustic properties, which is why you never see it used in well designed professional studios (except maybe to hide the real acoustic treatment, in which case it is proper fire-rated acoustical carpet). It absorbs sound selectively, to different levels at different frequencies. It absorbs highs pretty well, mids not very well, and lows not at all, which is the exact opposite of what you want with acoustics treatment. So your room ends up sounding dull, honky, boomy, and rather unpleasant. In your case, since you want to build a "room in a room", it will be inside the MSM wall cavity, where it wont matter that much from the acoustic point of view, but it will still be a fire hazard.
Fire caulking could be one way to begin,
It is the ONLY way to begin! Both leaves must be sealed airtight. That inlcudes the existing outer leaf.
By the way, the walls are wooden partition on three sides, the back wall is 2 ft thick brick and plaster. These walls cannot be altered.
So you are going to need a LOT of mass on your inner-leaf walls, and large air spaces between. Since you have no choice but to end up with three-leaf walls, that is the only way to get good isolation. You have to compensate for the three-leaf losses with the extra mass and extra air gap.

Soundman20209 November 2010#10
Is it just done through lack of knowledge about what carpet does to a room? As I said, its so commonplace.
Most likely. It seems to be second only to egg crates on the list of acoustic myths. Probably folks go to modern movie houses, see what they think is carpet on the walls, not how good the acoustics are, and figure that the carpet must be responsible for the good sound. what they don't realize is that the carpet in a movie theater is NOT the treatment: the carpet is most likely designed to be acoustically transparent, and its only purpose in life is to hide the real treatment that is on the other side of the carpet, but looks pretty ugly. The carpet is pure aesthetics: the real magic goes on behind it. But folks don't know that, so they figure "If movie houses get such great sound with carpet, then I can do that too"! So they grab any old chunk of carpet, glue it to the walls, and think the did something wonderful, when in fact they just took a huge step backwards, acoustically. The issue with most kinds of carpet you commonly find in homes and offices is that the acoustic properties are unknown: it just was not designed with any acoustics in mind, so the properties end up being totally random. In most cases, typical home and office carpeting absorbs high frequencies quite well, to varying degrees, absorbs a little bit of mids, and absorbs absolutely zero lows. In reality, most studios, rehearsal rooms, home theaters, and other small listening rooms need exactly the opposite in treatment! They need huge amounts of low frequency absorption ("bass trapping"), a moderate amount of mid absorption, and only a little high frequency absorption, if any. So 99 times out of a hundred, the carpet does the exact opposite of what is needed. Why do people still use it, then? #1 reason: Ignorance. #2 reason: "I saw it on the Internet, so it must be right!". The exact same reasons why so many cheap rehearsal rooms and home studios have use egg crates and mattresses in them, with the drum kit in the corner, the singer in the exact center of the room, and everything turned up to 11.... Even a basic understanding of small room acoustics would convince them to do none of the above, and the sound would just be so much better. But ignorance always wins out against knowledge for those who aren't prepared to learn... - Stuart -
