I've been renting space in the basement of a commercial building for my studio for the past 6 years or so. The space available has been less than ideal. With a modest increase in rent, I'll have roughly 3 times as much space available with 12' ceilings. I've been waiting a long time to have a proper space to work with. They expect to close on the building in the next 1-2 months, and I'll need to move quickly on the interior construction and wiring. I'll have 2 classrooms to use for the space, accessible via the typical middle-school hallway. I've included a picture of an adjacent classroom that shows a moveable divider, but mine will be separated by cinder-block. I'm told that it's a supporting wall, but I'm not sure that's accurate. In either case, I won't be able to completely remove the wall as shown in the picture, but I can put in a large window and possibly a door if needed. I'll be doing all the work myself with help from friends and family -- some of who are knowledgeable with interior construction. I'll be buying the supplies myself on a somewhat tight budget. I do have a friend who owns a window shop and will build windows for me at cost. In short, I'll spend what I need to build a respectable space, but I can't afford to go overboard, and I'll need to be very resourceful. I record a variety of artists and groups ..Jazz, Rock, World music, strings, horns, etc... Given the location and cinder-block construction, I'm not at all concerned about noise levels, internal or external. I am a bit concerned about RFI from the AM broadcast towers nearby, but I'm hoping that's not going to be an issue. If it is, I'll have to invest in a ton of metal screening for a makeshift Faraday cage.... I know very little about room geometry and acoustics, and after doing quite a bit of research, I've concluded that it's over my head and I need help. The depth of knowledge involved with room acoustics is staggering and daunting to me. If you could take a look at my first attempt at planning and give me some suggestions, I'd be extremely grateful. Thanks! Sean

For a first attempt, that's not bad at all! I would suggest maybe moving the two iso booths to the front of the room, on the sides, so you can have good sight lines into then, and also from them into the live room. It is important for musicians to have visual contact among themselves, and with the iso booths at the far end of the control room, that would be difficult. It would also make space for the bass trapping you will need in the rear corners of the control room. Another thing I would do is to get the speakers off the console meter bridge and either onto stands behind the desk, or better still, flush mount them properly in the angled sections in either side of the front window. That solves so many problems, it is well worth considering. - Stuart -

Soundman202013 December 2013#4
Wow... I'd have thought cinder block would give more isolation.
Yeah, you'd think so. But with just a single-leaf wall, you are subject to a fundamental law of physics called "mass law", which is very hopeful for studio isolation. It basically says that each time you double the mass of a wall, you only get 6 dB increase in isolation. And that's 6 dB in theory: in practice you don't even get that. More like 4 to 5 dB. So even very massive walls don't offer as much isolation as you'd think, if they are single leaf. On the other hand, if you have a two-leaf wall, things are very different! You can get much higher levels of isolation with much less mass.
Noise bleeding out (65db - yikes!) may require me to schedule tracking around other things going on. It's the same boat I'm in now, unfortunately.
Building a simple stud frame an inch or so away from that block wall and putting drywall on one side of it, can greatly increase the isolation.
I'm using Auralex Isolater pads now (Yamaha HS50m's with sub), but I think you're right... stands would be better.
Right! While foam pads under the speaker can help a bit to reduce coupling, they do nothing to deal with the other big problem of speakers on meter bridges: reflections of the console or desk getting straight into your ears, and messing up your perception of the sound.
