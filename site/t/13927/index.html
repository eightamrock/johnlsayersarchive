The issue is that it is difficult for the local enforcers to administer codes with no precedence set. It is common in a handful of states (USofA) but not so much that everyone agrees on the installation, application, electrical, etc. With bales like you mention, the process is difficult. The process is outside of your typical contractors area of expertise so you have to locate a contractor that does this type of job, lock and key. [quote=]but isn't there a huge fire risk with that?[/quote] No there isn't. The bales will in the end, be covered in mud or another approved hardened covering, depending on who is selling the product/idea. But it winds up being like doing a concrete block structure, from what I have read. Everything is placed on the interior, nothing will recess. This is typical for what people say of an acoustical environment any way, so it seems to fit that criteria. It has excellent thermal ratings, as the information I have read suggest. But in the end, the roof becomes one of the biggest flanking points, the largest leak in the otherwise high gained TL from building in this fashion. And that brings you back to code/insurance. How much weight can you safely place on this type of structure? These are the questions that need answers....

John Sayers11 January 2010#7
unfortunately jonas that video won't stream outside the US. I'm aware of straw bale construction. You stack the bales then trim them as smooth as you can get with a hedge trimmer. Chicken wire or similar is then attached to it and then plastered to seal it all. You end up with a live surface that has to be acoustically treated. Lottsa labour involved. To be honest I see no real advantage in the system when applied to studio construction, for a cool house, yes, but a studio, IMO - no. :)
