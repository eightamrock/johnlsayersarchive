Avalanche Explosion wrote:
"this idea was largely based on just a plain old gut-instinct telling me that concrete walls--just because they're so hard, I guess--are the last thing a person wants in their studio (from an acoustic perspective).
So what? A plywood panel attached on a concrete wall won't make that wall acoustically disappear! As a matter of fact, it will have insignificant effect on the reflection of that wall. You need absorbent materials like rockwool or fiberglass to absorb efficiently. Concrete wall reflects sound back in the room (and absorbs a little I guess). You have to make the wall absorb much more. And you do that with acoustic treatment like bass traps or slot resonators. For such a small room, you don't need diffusion. Cheers, John

Soundman202028 February 2009#14
but it's pretty tinny down there now and it's hard to imagine it sounding good with much absorption.
It sounds that way precisely because you DON'T have any absorption down there. Your guitar is probably exciting many modes that give the room it's tiny sound right now, and you'll have dips and peaks in the room response all across the low to mid end of the spectrum. Unless you smooth those out, it is going to continue to sound "tinny", and probably "clangy" and "cavey", and maybe even "boomy" too. Among other things. In order to get the room response to be more even, you need to put in absorption, but not so much that it "kills" the room entirely. And you will probably also need other treatment, such as maybe broadband slot resonators, to keep the highs in the room. I'd start out by taking a careful look at Ethan's web site, where he has a wealth of information on treating rooms, and also use the search button on this forum, to search for things about treating live rooms. I'd also suggest that you actually measure the response of your room as it is right now, so that you can actually see where your problems are, then figure out what to do about them. Just saying that it is "tinny" can't help you very much in that area! You need to know exactly which areas of the spectrum are causing that "tinny" sound, then you can decide what you need to do about them, in order to even out the response. It might even be that what you call "tinny" is really flutter echo, which is a different type of problem that is also usually treated with absorption in small rooms, or diffusion in large rooms. But you'll never know by guessing! Ethan describes an easy way of measuring your room, on his web site, and there are several threads about that here to. Once again, the "search" feature will help you find them.
... it's hard to imagine it sounding good with much absorption.
If you ask me, it's hard to imagine it sounding good WITHOUT absorption!!!! :)
I can see that this may involve some experimenting...
Probably not! Others have already done the experimenting for you: Once you know where you problems are, there are already known treatments for them. Identify your problems, treat them, and the room will sound much better. You can experiment if you want, but why waste your time and money when the information is already available! :) - Stuart -
