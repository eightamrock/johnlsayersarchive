xSpace4 February 2011#7
"the garage is rated for parking 2 cars so weight shouldn't be a problem. the only really heavy thing i have going in the room is a yamaha upright piano and a couch." It won't be without it's challenges...but you really have to get a better number on the load limit than a couple of cars...what is that weight about 4-5 tons? Is that including the piano? For me, what you are wanting to do is add to an already existing dead load. Things like vehicles and such are not specifically intended to be parked...forever..so that could/can influence what your limits may or may not be...follow me? Even still, the weight you will impose on this structure can modify how it was intended to resist the environmental conditions that exist in your area. Things like erosion, flooding..these have to be given their due respect in order to be able to re-hab a structure and have it remain safe for you and those that might be around/under it. I'll say this before I say the next thing...kinda should have gone without saying right :)? You gave measurements of 16X22X7'9" right? As a rule, your isolation will be reduced to the smallest distance from one hard boundary to the nearest boundary. What that means is that your over head area will dictate what your isolation will be since it will be the smallest component in your mass/air/,mass assembly (remember, we still have no visual on this so speculation is the word of the day/week). Just using some hack numbers I have check this out: I already mentioned 4-5 tons for vehicles. The framing for your walls and ceiling, plus quietrock @ one layer is roughly 5100 pounds. That is approaching half and better than your actual supposed limit of this as yet to be determined load limit. That doesn't take any additional weight into consideration...that piano, insulation, bodies, the couch...doors, HVAC, electrical...equipment, etc...cheeseburgers...what have you. There are ways to do this, but you have to be realistic in what this structure can do for you....the last thing anyone wants is to get involved in a great project that collapses because someone forgot to take gravity into the equation.

xSpace4 February 2011#11
Trying to break a hard connection between a wall and where it is attached is, well, hard. I have seen people do all kinds of things that I can't say if the methods do or do not have any effect. A dense caulk like butyl, underneath the floor plates is going to work better than say a latex caulk, but neither of the two will make the walls float. But with any floating of a floor, the new framed walls still want to sit directly on the "floated floor" to create that "room in a room" fully decoupled effect.
