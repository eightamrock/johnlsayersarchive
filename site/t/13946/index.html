Soundman202015 January 2010#7
I see several issues with your plan. First, as you already noted, the rooms are nearly square. Even worse, they are nearly cubes! That's bad! In addition, all the walls are parallel. Cubed and parallel is not good. You'll have major room mode issues, combined with flutter echos at related frequencies. I'd suggest you do whatever you can to get away from square and parallel. I would try to angle the dividing wall between the two rooms, and move at least one other wall a bit to get as far away from square as you can. Maybe angle the ceiling as well.
My plan is to build a room inside the existing room with a 2 inch air gap,
Two inches isn't really enough. It should be 4" in order to keep the MSM resonant frequency down low. However, that number refers to the distance between the surfaces of the inner and outer leaves, not to the distance between the new frame and the existing wall. So you could have a 1" gap between the existing wall and the new frame, plus the depth of the studs. That would give you more than 4" of air gap. so if you are taking about a 2" gap between the existing wall and the new frame, you could actually reduce that gap a bit, down to 1", and gain space inside the room, if you want to! Of course, if you don't need the extra space in the room, then keep that 2" gap, by all means! It will help your isolation.
built of timber frame filled with 60kg insulation,
You don't really need such a high density. Normal recommendation here is 48 kg/m3: The higher density won't do anything additional acoustically, and will only cost you more money.
with 1 panel of acoustic plaster board on the back and 2 panels on the front
Forget the acoustic plaster board, and just go with the plain old ordinary plasterboard (drywall, gypsum board, sheetrock, etc.). The acoustic stuff doesn't stuff doesn't do a lot extra for you, and just costs more money.
with a resilient bar channel between the timber frame and both panels of plaster board on the front.
You don't need the resilient channel since you will already have a decoupled stud frame. At least, I assume that by "built of timber frame" and "2 inch air gap" you are referring to a decoupled stud frame? By "decoupled" I mean that the new frame is totally free-standing, NOT attached to the existing walls or ceiling ANYWHERE. No mechanical connection at all (no nails, bolts, screws, wires, blocking, etc.) It can only be bolted to the existing floor, but cannot touch any other wall or the ceiling. If that is what you have, then you do NOT need the resilient channel (RC), since your wall is already decoupled. The RC will cost you extra money, take up extra space, and will not do anything for you acoustically.
The celling will be built in the same way and I will be building a floating floor.
Like others already said, you don't need a floating floor, and you most certainly don't have the budget for it! A proper floating floor would cost many times your entire budget, and is really hard to get right anyway.
I'm on the first floor, which is built on solid concrete then chipboard floor, underlay then laminate wood flooring. was going to put a layer chipboard on top of that with really dense foam (or neoprene) underneath to decouple the room from the floor. (good idea, bad idea?)
doesn't sound like a good plan for the floor, as others already said. I think what I'd do is to take off everything everything that is already on the floor, right down to bare concrete, and just use that as the floor. Concrete is a good floor, acoustically, provided that you go with a soft ceiling. It will also gain you maximum possible room height, which you are short of right now. If you hate the idea of a concrete floor, then just level the concrete with self-leveling cement, and lay laminate flooring directly on top of that.
(as I already have foam and chipboard floor) do you think floating the floor on this would be worth doing?
Nope. Floating your floor is a huge expense, and doing it right is not easy. Do it wrong and you screw up the isolation. You'd be much better with plain concrete floor, and a drum riser (like Lilith's) for isolating drums, amps, etc.
I know this is the wrong part of the forum but has anyone got any info about the slot absorbers do I just work out the axial room modes and treat them or tangential and oblique?
No need to. Just build John's standard slot resonator, which is a broad-band design, and the dimensions are already figured out for you.
is it ok to put acoustic plasterboard on the wall then build the slot resonator off of that or would I have to put some kind of absorbent material behind the wooden slats.
Look at the designs that John has posted here in several places, and that has been built in many studios, successfully. It works very well. Just build it like that, apying attention to all the details. - Stuart -
