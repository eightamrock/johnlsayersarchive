Ethan Winer24 October 2009#52
valentin wrote:
Remember not because you can measure something it means it correct or not correct
Yes, and my opinion about the damage done by early reflections is based mainly on listening. I remember very well the first time the value of absorbing reflections was shown to me. My friend, studio designer Wes Lachot visited me, and we played some music on my living room system that had only bass traps at the time. At his urging I grabbed two pieces of 703 from my basement and we propped them up on chairs at the side-wall reflection points. The improvement in imaging and clarity was immediately obvious. Soon after I added permanent absorbers in my living room and also my home studio, and I'd never consider going back. More: When the first prototypes of my company's diffusors were ready, I replaced the two side-wall absorbers in my living room with diffusors. My wife and I sat on the couch, I put on some music, and we both looked at each other and said "Much worse!" This was after having absorbers there for several years. More: The way my living room is set up, if you stand behind the couch and lean down a bit, your head is too far back to be in the "reflection free" zone. This makes it very easy to quickly hear the music with and without early reflections. This has become a standard demo when friends and potential customers visit. Everyone prefers the sound with reflections absorbed. Measurements came after, as a way to learn why the sound was so much better with absorption. --Ethan

valentin26 October 2009#61
here some other words from Dr. Trevor J. Cox (SALFORD UNIVERSITY) and Dr. Peter D’Antonio (RPG)
In critical listening rooms, a mixture of absorbers and diffusers is used to control the acoustics of a space. Treatment is placed to control first order reflections. When absorbers are used, the sonic images forming the soundstage are points in space. When diffusers are used, these images take on a more natural width and depth. Which material is correct, absorber or diffuser, is to a certain extent a matter of personal taste. If all the treatment is absorption, then the room turns out to be rather dead. While some people favour this for mixing audio, others do not, and for a listening room a very absorbent environment is not best. Consequently, if some liveliness is to be left in the room, a combination of absorbers and diffusers must be used

That's all fine, and I see little to disagree with. But one key thing missing is how the choice of diffusion versus absorption is influenced by the size of the room. This is crucial! When absorbers are used, the sonic images forming the soundstage are points in space. When diffusers are used, these images take on a more natural width and depth.
Who is to say what sounds more "natural?" Especially given that most pop music today is assembled from separate sources recorded with close mics one track at a time. :mrgreen: After you finish your room with diffusors at the reflection points, it'd be great if you covered them with absorbers as a test.
