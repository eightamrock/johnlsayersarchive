In the meantime, can anyone else answer the questions from my original post?
OK, I'll try!
1. Is it possible to find a room in an existing house that satisfies these needs without requiring construction?
Probably not, unless your family are all very heavy sleepers, or the house is built really tough. 80 dB is pretty loud for normal house construction (stud framing with thin drywall on both sides). You won't get much better than STC-30 out of that, meaning that 80 dB music will still be very audible on the other side of the wall. Even loud talking is audible through that kind of construction. The best way to block sound is with a decoupled mass-air-mass wall, and normal wall construction isn't it!
... what should I be looking for? Physical isolation? Certain wall/floor/ceiling construction? Ground floor vs. second floor? Garage?
Your best bet is ground floor, with a concrete slab floor (not a wooden floor on joists), brick walls, and nothing above you. A garage is great. A basement is good too, if you have plenty of ceiling height. Physical isolation (such as a totally separate garage or large solid shed, a few meters away form the house, and built on a separate slab) would be ideal. Your very worst location would be a room on the second floor, with a wooden floor laid on joists, and bedrooms right next to you. Construction: Brick, concrete, or cement block walls would be great, staggered stud walls would be second best, but most likely you will be stuck with normal 2x4 stud walls with drywall on each side. That's the worst.
1b. If not, how much would I have to reduce my mixing level to make this possible?
You'd need to bring it down to the level of normal speech, which is around the 60 dB range. In an average house, mixing at 60 dB is probably too quiet to mix effectively, since you won't be able to hear the subtle details.
2. What's the best way to test isolation in houses I visit? I plan to buy a sound level meter, but what should I use as my sound source? I'm assuming that if I want to simulate mixing at ~80db, I should find something portable that can output sound at that volume, covering the same frequency range (~35Hz-20kHz). I have portable computer speakers (Logitech Z4, 40W Total RMS Power) that output in that frequency range...will those suffice?
You have the basic idea, but you'll need a full-range speaker, not a cheap computer thingy. Something like a near-field studio monitor would be great, plus maybe a sub-woofer if drums and bass are an important part of your mixes. And 40w is probably not enough to put out a clean 80 dB signal. I'd guess something more like 100W or so for the main, and maybe double that for the sub. You need to be able to produce a full-range signal cleanly. Set your meter to "C" weighting, slow response. You will need to do your measurements at night, since that's the time when your "targets" will be sleeping. Measuring in the day wont tell you much, since the ambient level in daytime is high already. You need to measure at the same time of day that you are most concerned about, which is probably late at night when the ambient noise level is way down. First walk around the house before you turn on your speaker, and measure all over, inside and outside, taking notes of the ambient noise levels in each room. Now go to your future-studio room, and play pink noise through your full-range speaker setup, such that you get a level of greater than 80 dB on your sound level meter, measured about a meter in front of the speaker(s). I'd suggest 86 dB as your reference level, for complex reasons. Then go out of the room, shut all the doors and windows, and walk around the rest of the house again, measuring all over a second time. If you are getting readings of more than about 50 dB or so in the bedrooms (or more than about 10 dB above the ambient level that you measured before, whatever that was) then you have a problem that will need isolation.
3. If a second floor room above a garage had better-than-average isolation in the walls and ceiling (let's say RC-1), and the doors were solid core and airtight, and the other bedrooms were on the same floor, would the weak link be flanking transmission?
Maybe. Maybe not. It all depends on how about a million and forty seven other factors! :) Seriously, it's hard to say without knowing how the place is built. But if the place is all simple 2x4 stud-plus-drywall construction, then it just isn't going to do much for you. I'd guess that, at the very least, you'd need to take off all the drywall on walls and ceiling, install resilient channel across the studs and joists, and put a couple of layers of 5/8 drywall back in again.
3a. If so, how bad is it, and would that mean I shouldn't even consider a second floor studio if the bedrooms are also on the second floor?
Once again, it depends.... If the floor, walls and ceiling are all 10 inch-thick reinforced high-density concrete, then you don't have a problem! Well worth considering, for sure! Brick walls on a concrete slab floor would be good too. But standard stud-and-drywall is no-go.
5. Can an acoustically decent control room be assembled in a room with 8-foot ceilings? In this case, "decent" means that I don't have to keep going back to my car to check levels...I certainly don't need world-class.
Yes. Many folks here have done just that. If you cab get more height, then great! Or you could build inside-out to gain a couple of inches. But 8 foot is doable. - Stuart -

From one of those rooms, the music was completely inaudible. From another room, you could hear the bass, but just barely. From the downstairs, I could only hear the music from one location, and again, just bass, and it was very quiet and muffled. From outside the house, wherever I walked, the music was completely inaudible.
That's good news! It seems like you have a good place to start, and you won't need too much isolation after all. However, I should point out a couple of things: 1) You did the test during the day with an ambient level of 50 dB, which is kind of what I would have expected, so its not surprising that you couldn't hear the music. You might be surprised as to how much difference there is between a 50 dB ambient level, and a 35 or 40 dB ambient level, which is more typical of late at night. What you can't hear at all with 50 db background becomes quite noticeable at 40. That's why I suggested that you should test at the same time of day that you plan to be making the most noise, which you said was going to be late at night. 2) You did not use pink noise, and instead used only music, so you were not measuring constant full spectrum energy. In other words, there just wasn't as much energy output as there should have been. The energy content and dynamic range of music varies widely. You'd get very different results playing Chopin and Grateful Dead! That said, I'd say that your preliminary tests do indicate that you seem to have a good place to start with, and that it won't require too much extra isolation. But I would suggest that you repeat the test at night with pink noise, if you want to get a more realistic reading. Or do it again during the day, but with 100 dB reference level instead of 86, which will give you about the same level above the higher ambient threshold. Get someone to "pulse" the pink noise (turn it off and on every few seconds) while you walk around, so that you can discern the difference better. That would be a more realistic daytime test.
I imagine if I add my room treatments (OC703 or similar) and fill up the space with other absorbent materials, it will only improve the situation.
Not really. Isolation and treatment are two different things. Treating a room does not help much to isolate it. If you don't believe me, while you are plying your 86 dB pink noise, place a chunk of 703 right up against the speaker, and measure again... The change will be imperceptible, and barely measurable. It might take a little off the very high end, at best, but it won't reduce the energy level by anything worthwhile. To really convince yourself, add a second piece of 703 in front of the first. There still won't be a worthwhile difference in total level. Absorption does not stop sound getting out of your room. Even if you covered the entire surface area of the room, all the walls, the floor and the ceiling, with 8 inches 703, it would hardly make any difference to the sound transmission to the outside world (not to mention that the room would sound like garbage if you did that!). You need an awfully thick piece of 703 to totally stop 86 dB of pink noise! So I wouldn't count on the acoustic treatment of your room making any appreciable difference to the isolation. I'm not trying to discourage you, really! I'm just pointing out the simple physics of sound energy. - Stuart -
