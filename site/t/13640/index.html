Deluks6 October 2009#2
Doesn't sound great to me, Firstly you have triple leaf. Blocks>airgap>plywood> airgap (insulation is classed as airgap)>sand/drywall. I don't see the need for any isolating channels. The inner room does need to be seperate from the outer shell, but seperating the inner room from the floor isn't necessary for most applications, and certainly won't be achieved by using any alu channel. You can either float the inner room (expensive and complex), make a cut in the concrete floor, or if pouring a new slab make a construction joint filled with flexible sealant. The channel may be providing a break between concrete floor and timber, which may be necessary for local building regulation, but you'd be better off acheving this by using a single course of engineering bricks and roll of dpc (damp proof course) Serously forget the sand (especially in the roofspace :shock: ) Just use an extra sheet of drywall in it's place. solid concrete block walls make an excellent outer leaf, inner leaf could be studwork>ply>drywall>drywall to achieve decent performance. 30 grand? Get a few more quotes and insist they go with your design (you have had a thorough read through this site haven't you? (a thorough read through this website will take a couple of weeks to get your head around the entire project) That's a lotta dough for a 16 y.o. to be splashing out :yahoo:
Soundman20206 October 2009#3
9" concrete bolck, 4" gap, 3/4" plywood, 4x2 wooden frame gaps filled with rockwool, metal channel, sand filled slab.
Deluks is absolutely right. That's a bad idea. That's a three-leaf wall, with very little mass on the inner two leaves, and a very thin air gap between them. You will have rather poor isolation on that wall, mostly in the low end of the spectrum, and it is going to ring and resonate really well, way up in the audible range! Not a good idea at all. Far better to move the plywood to the other side of the studs (facing the room, not the wall cavity). Forget about the metal channel (it gains you nothing in this case and costs you money). Put the drywall directly over the plywood. The sequence will be: "Block" -- "air gap with studs and insulation" -- "plywood + drywall". You only want ONE air gap in your wall, never two or more. Everything must be sealed airtight, of course. And just use normal drywall (sheetrock, gypsum board, whatever they call it in your part of the world). Fancy stuff only costs you more, for very little (if any) additional benefit. Two layers of 16mm drywall would be plenty. Sound waves don't care how much you paid for your mass: mass is just mass. You could even ditch the plywood, if code allows it, and just use layers of drywall. Plywood is more expensive that drywall in most places, and drywall is denser. Get the cheapest, most dense mass you can.
Solid concrete base. And some sort of a flooring, not too absorbative.
Just leave it as solid concrete. Stain it for aesthetics if you don't like the look of concrete. Or if you must cover it, then lay laminate flooring. Absorptive flooring is generally not a good idea in studios, for many reasons. Keep it reflective. General rule is: reflective floor, absorptive ceiling (or diffusing, if the room is bug enough).
Is there any features of the plans that are stand out wrong?
You didn't mention silencers for your HVAC. You will need several silencers in the ductwork, to prevent sound from getting in, out and moving between the rooms. You didn't mention ventilation, only air conditioning. A min-split system does not provide any ventilation: it just recirculates the same old air. You also need to draw in fresh air from the outside world (through silencers) and to exhaust stale air back to the outside world (through silencers). You didn't mention electrical: that's a major point. You need to have all your wiring well though out, specially your grounding. And also your signal cabling between rooms. You didn't mention doors or windows, which will be the weakest part of your isolation. Get those wrong, and you won't have any isolation at all. Doing the doors and windows right is very important. It would help to see some actual Sketchup plans of the layout, in order to comment further. There might be other issues that need fixing too. Download Sketchup from Google, learn to use it, and design your studio in it. It's cheap (as in "free"), easy, powerful, and is what everyone uses here. Like Deluks said: spend some time reading over this forum, and studying the concepts and principles behind the advice you get here. Although I think Deluks is a little optimistic! It will take you two weeks of reading just to get through a single thread here, and there are dozens of threads well worth reading, with dozens of off-site links in each thread. I've been going for more than a year already, reading through this forum, and still not covered everything... :) And take Andre's advice: building a studio is 90% design, 10% construction. Don't even think about starting construction until the design is totally finalized. - Stuart -
