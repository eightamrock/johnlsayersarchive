Hola, y bienvenido al foro! También hay un foro en español aquí, pero parece que tu inglés es excelente, y hay muchos mas participantes aquí en inglés que español. So, a couple of things about the approach proposed by the acoustician that you spoke to: First, let's do what he apparently did not do: Examine the real numbers. The amount of isolation ("soundproofing") that you need is pretty large. You say you will be recording full hardcore rock bands in there, which implies acousstic drums, bass guitar, etc., so we are talking about sound levels of around 120 dB in your studio. In order to just "not be annoying" to others in the house, that needs to come down to somewhere around 55 dB at the place where the "other people" are, and in order to meet most municipal noise codes, it would have to come down to around 40 dB. So you are looking at isolation of around 65 to 80 dB, if those are your goals. That is doable, but is not easy, and will not be accomplished using the approach your acoustician quoted. His approach will give you something like high 40's to low 50's. You didn't say what the floor above the room is made of, but I'm assuming that it is either a concrete slab, or it is standard wooden construction, with joists, subfloor, and finish floor. However, your acoustician is right about one thing: the only way you can get that kind of isolation is with "room in a room" construction. This implies that all your walls are two-leaf MSM walls, with lots of mass on each leaf and large air gaps. So he's right about the basic concept, but not about the method. Let's take a closer look: First, he says that you need a floating floor. Maybe you do, but what he said is not necessarily going to give it to you: "Floor: acoustic membrane, concrete slab, wood floor". It all depends on what he means by "acoustic membrane" and "wood floor". But apart from that, you don't have the budget to do a proper floating floor. You might want to spend some time reading through this thread, so you can get familiar with the concept and the issues: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=8173 So, since your budget probably doesn't go far enough to do a floating floor correctly, and you don't have enough ceiling height to do it anyway, it seems like you are stuck with the floor that you have. But it isn't all bad news: Since you say that the external walls are concrete, I'm guessing that the floor of that room is also concrete, resting directly on the ground. That's good. It probably doesn't need any treatment, and you can either leave it as plain concrete, or stain it for aesthtics, or maybe lay something thin and hard on it, such as ceramic tiles, laminate flooring, or something similar. Keep it hard and reflective. You already have ceramic tiles, so if you happen to like those ones, then your floor is already done! :) Since you will be recording acoustic drums and bass amps in there, you might have an issue with impact noise getting into the floor, so you will probably need to build a drum riser and isolation pads for amps, to help with that issue. This somewhat reduces the need for a floating floor. Next, you need two-leaf MSM walls. You already have the outer leaf (the concrete walls of the building itself), plus the brick walls to the internal rooms. Those are fine. So you only need to add the inner leaf. That should be done with 2x6 wood studs, spaced 24" OC (60 cm), with two layers of 16mm drywall on only ONE side of those studs and 100% coverage of Green Glue between the layers of drywall. The framing should be done such that there is a gap of at least 6" between the existing walls and the surface of the drywall that faces the existing wall. You fill that gap with insulation, such as fluffy pink fibreglass or mineral wool. Then, the ceiling: This should also be two-leaf MSM. If you have a concrete slab up above you, then that's fine as your outer leaf. If you have a wood floor up there, then you might need to "beef it up" (add more mass to it) in order to make it into a good outer leaf. The inner leaf needs to be done just like the walls: wood framing (joists) dimensioned correctly for the load and span, and resting ONLY on the inner-leaf walls. No contact at all with the existing outer-leaf walls, or with the existing outer-leaf ceiling. Once again, you need a large air gap and two sheets of 16mm drywall. However, you don't have much space up there, so you could go for a smaller gap and more mass, such as a 4" gap with three sheets of 16mm drywall, to get you roughly the same isolation. This gap is also filled with insulation. You don't need windows by the looks of it, and that is great! Less weak points. But you do need doors. Build them like Rod outlines in his book. You also need to consider your HVAC and electrical installations, and they need to be designed and done very carefully in order to not compromise the isolation of the room. If you do everything right, this construction should theoretically get you isolation on the order of 65 db or so, which is the minimum you need. To get that up beyond 70, you'd need to leave larger air gaps all around and add more mass to the walls and doors, but I think you'd already be getting up to the flanking limit of the floor, so I doubt it is worth going that far. Going back to the approaches that your acoustican recommended, #2 is not going to get you anywhere near the level of isolation that you need. There are no "magical" acoustic materials that can beat the laws of physics. Those laws of physics state that the only way to stop sound is to totally decouple the path mechanically, and place massive air-tigh barriers along the path. #2 does not accomplish that. And #1 is way off base as well. Putting "double 20mm gypsum walls on metal frame" for the walls and only "16mm gypsum boards hanging from metal springs" for the ceiling is pointless! That would be like trying to build an aquarium using thick glass for the sides but cardboard for the bottom! How well will that hold water? For acoustic isolation, the entire envelope MUST be treated to the same level: All six sides of the room must have the exact same level of isolation. If one side is weaker than the other five, then you wasted a huge amount of time and money on the other five sides, because sound will simply "leak" through the weak side, and not even bother with the five "good" sides. Physics says that sound will take the easiest path out of the room. In this case, it will take the path through the thin 16mm ceiling, so the 40mm drywall on the walls is pointless.
filled with 70kg/M2 rockwool.
Way too dense! Optimum density is about 40 to 50 kg/m3. Going up to 70 kg/m3 will reduce isolation in lower frequencies, such as drums and bass guitars, which is where your biggest problems will be. Higher density is better for high frequency isolation, but you will not have any problems there! Low frequencies is where your problems will be, and for that you need lower density insulation in your walls and ceiling. Take a look at this report to understand the issues here. Three more comments: 1).
Double window
Window? What window? I don't see one on your layout, and you didn't mention it elsewhere. 2). What are you planning to do with that space under the stairs? 3). How do you plan to lay out that room? Maybe do a model in SkethUp so that we can see which way around you plan to have things, and maybe offer some advice on how to improve that. - Stuart -

Yup! Here "more is better". If your building code (and your budget!) allow you to fill the entire cavity, then go for it.
If I got this right, basically isolation and treatment are "merged" in one system, is this correct?
Yup, that's pretty much it. You do have to realize that the two-leaf is a tuned system, and whatever you change has consequences. So if you reduce the size of the air cavity, that increases the resonant frequency, which is not good. To compensate for that, you can increase the mass of the leaves, which reduces the resonant frequency again, which is good. But there are limits: you can't go down to much less about 3" inches or air gap, or if you do then you need a LOT of extra mass to compensate. And going the other way too much also isn't good: For example, if you go to a 16" air gap then theoretically you could go with just a single leaf of 1/2" drywall (12mm), but 1/2" has its own problems, such as being too flexible and having a high resonant frequency all of its own, so you wouldn't want to to do that. So you'd still never go less than a single layer of 5/8" (16mm) drywall, no matter how big your air gap is. But I'm waffling... That's just a long way of saying: Yup, your variation on John's design is just fine. The slot wall is a very effective way of treating the room, too.
I'd have to find a way to glue the insulation next to the air gap.
Gluing it is probably not a good idea. There are special "spikes" that you hang it from. You attach the spikes to the wall, and push the insulation onto them. - Stuart -
