NYMO - if you have a moisture problem that you can't solve - then what you'e doing is the worst possible thing you can do. You would have been much better off to seal the existing slab - and then add a slab directly on top of it - The problem here is that you need for this deck to breathe (if there's moisture) otherwise it will have somemajor problems with mold and eventually the wood rotting away. This is one of the reasons that building codes require free air ventilation of crawl spaces under tempered living spaces....... here in the states that is generally around 1 sf of free air for each 150 sf of deck - and this is to be installed in such a manner that cross ventilation is provided. If this space was my space - i would remove the work and replace it with a raised slab over slab. It might cost you a few more bucks - but it's not going to blow up in your face in a few years if your moisture comes back.... and it will also give you better isolation that what "m looking at in your pictues - I wouldn'tplace a whole lot of hope in that deck gaining you any degree of isolation. Sincerely, Rod

knightfly14 February 2007#18
First, since you're obviously going ahead with the wooden floated floor - your insulation needs to be a bit thicker, at least in the middle of each joist cavity, so that it snugly contacts the first layer of subflooring - otherwise, I GUARANTEE you will have a "boom room" going. The flooring MUST be damped or it will vibrate, period. Second, the more mass in the floor the better - I would consider your two layers of 3/4" to be minimum, and you may want to consider something like 30 pound roofing felt between layers (but don't overlap it like a roof) - plus, you want to offset seams in the second layer so they don't line up in either direction. For this type floor, SCREWS and construction adhesive. You don't want squeaks down the road when nails pull loose. Walls - no point floating them on neoprene, may introduce ANOTHER resonance to fight. In the states there is a product called "sill seal", it's for topping off a perimeter concrete foundation before the sill plates go down. It's basically to help fill voids and avoid air infiltration in that context. If you can't find that, a couple layers of 30 pound roofing felt between the floor and the stud channel will also work. For mounting wallboard, see the stickies section on drywall and caulk. If there is a way to get some mechanical ventilation between your slab and that floated floor, I wouldn't hesitate - if it's too loud, you can turn it off during studio use. It'll be a big help toward avoiding any further chance of wood rot or mildew growth, along with keeping a dehumidifier in the space - for any wood instrument (and your health) 42% humidity is the goal... Steve

You absolutely should not attempt to install new inner leaf wall frames on top of a laminate floor. You'll crush and/or damage the laminate. I hope this doesn't lead to confusion, but laminate floors are meant to "float" freely so that they can expand and contract through the seasons. If you install walls on top of it, not only will you surely damage the laminate in the process, but the floor will buckle or pull apart, depending on the amount of humidity in the air.
