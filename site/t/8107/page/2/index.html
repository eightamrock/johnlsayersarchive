sharward3 May 2007#48
That is not going to work, nymo. Anytime you build habitable space (and you are) without operable windows, you must have "mechanical ventilation" supplying fresh air supplied to the room while it is occupied. I believe the minimum is 15 cubic feet per minute per occupant. Quoting from the esteemed Rod Gervais from this thread (which you should read fully):
    The requirements I noted in my last post - that's for general buildings - not super duper air tight recording studios - where there is NO air infiltration due to the construction. Once you're done weatherstripping those doors - what the hell are people supposed to breathe? You have a very bad - VERY unsafe situation there - and if i were you I would seriously consider making your landlord do something about it....... I would hate to be a person in your iso booth for 10 to 15 minutes breathing in my own carbon-dioxide instead of oxygen....... you had better be installing co2 alarms in this studio of yours.
Your plan to open the door once in a while is what I had planned to do myself. When I presented that plan to my city's building department senior inspector, he literally laughed me into submission. The fresh air requirement was a major challenge for me in my design phase, along with whether or not to float a floor. (I ultimately did not float a floor.) I decided on a fresh air system that will be based on a heat recovery ventilator (HRV) with befy silencers to preserve isolation. It's really unfortunate that you didn't study my thread before you started your build. To be honest, you have made and are still making many of the same mistakes I started to make on paper and would have been destined to make in construction if I hadn't been committed to our building permit process, following building codes, and getting as much professional (and helpful amateur) advice here before driving my first nail. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward3 May 2007#50
I'm not a health expert, but I've been told by multiple construction experts that it's illegal and unhealthy, and that's all I need to know. That being said, I just looked it up -- here are excerpts from what the United States Environmental Protection Agency says about indoor air quality:
    The term "sick building syndrome" (SBS) is used to describe situations in which building occupants experience acute health and comfort effects that appear to be linked to time spent in a building, but no specific illness or cause can be identified. The complaints may be localized in a particular room or zone, or may be widespread throughout the building. . .
      Indicators of SBS include:
      • Building occupants complain of symptoms associated with acute discomfort, e.g., headache; eye, nose, or throat irritation; dry cough; dry or itchy skin; dizziness and nausea; difficulty in concentrating; fatigue; and sensitivity to odors.
      • The cause of the symptoms is not known.
      • Most of the complainants report relief soon after leaving the building. . .
      Causes of Sick Building Syndrome The following have been cited causes of or contributing factors to sick building syndrome:
        Inadequate ventilation: In the early and mid 1900's, building ventilation standards called for approximately 15 cubic feet per minute (cfm) of outside air for each building occupant, primarily to dilute and remove body odors. As a result of the 1973 oil embargo, however, national energy conservation measures called for a reduction in the amount of outdoor air provided for ventilation to 5 cfm per occupant. In many cases these reduced outdoor air ventilation rates were found to be inadequate to maintain the health and comfort of building occupants. Inadequate ventilation, which may also occur if heating, ventilating, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems do not effectively distribute air to people in the building, is thought to be an important factor in SBS. In an effort to achieve acceptable IAQ while minimizing energy consumption, the American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) recently revised its ventilation standard to provide a minimum of 15 cfm of outdoor air per person (20 cfm/person in office spaces). Up to 60 cfm/person may be required in some spaces (such as smoking lounges) depending on the activities that normally occur in that space (see ASHRAE Standard 62-1989). . .
(The above are excerpts from Indoor Air Facts No. 4 (revised) Sick Building Syndrome.) Mick (et al), the experts have spoken on this. It's a fact: fresh air ventilation is required for health reasons, is required for compliance with all modern building codes, and is especially necessary within the context of air-tight studio construction. Respectfully, anecdotal "I didn't have fresh air ventilation and I didn't have any problems" type testimony is, to be honest, not a legitimate argument against. To raise it implies this is a matter of debate, when it honestly isn't: we're humans and we need oxygen -- end of story. :) --Keith :mrgreen:
