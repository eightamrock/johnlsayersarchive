sharward4 February 2007#2
As far as the nails and staples are concerned, you could use a sheet of closed cell foam insulation as your first layer of beef. It's lightweight, will allow the staples and nails to penetrate it, and it is solid in the sense that it will act as a wimpy layer of mass. It may also help to dampen resonance a little. The wiring, plumbing, and ductwork would have to be adressed separately; without specifics, there's not much I can offer. You may have to build around them. Sometimes a compromise involving some third leaf areas is necessary, in which case you can use more mass to compensate. Beef-up is indeed challenging, especially overhead. The only way to eat that elephant is pne bite at a time. Hang in there! :-) --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward5 February 2007#4
I haven't followed the links you posted at the beginning of this thread... But assuming you were planning a textbook-perfect two leaf system, and you're asking if you can get away with a three leaf system, the answer is, it depends. Sometimes three leaves are a necesary evil. We do our best to avoid them, but sometimes we're better served compensating for their weaknesses. The south wall of my project must be three leaves... Early on I discovered I had shear panels that I couldn't remove. Those shear panels are necessary for seismic stability. Shifting the shear panels to the opposite side of that wall would have meant a tremendous amount of messy demolition and reconstruction on the living space side... :evil: ...So I decided to live with a third leaf and I made up for it by adding more mass with constrained layer dampening on the last layer (Green Glue). There's now nearly 2½" of drywall on that middle leaf, plus the 3/8" shear panel OSB. With ceilings, it's hard to make up for the middle leaf deficit because adding that much weight can be dangerous. Study some more basement conversion threads here to see how others have faced challenges similar to yours. If you post pictures of your obstacles, you may get some responses with suggested workarounds. Two years ago, I used to think basements were ideal shells for studio construction projects... After seeing what you guys go through, I no longer think that! If I had a chance for a total do-over, dating back to our home purchase, I'd have sought property with a bit of extra land on which to build a new standalone structure from scratch. Hang in there... If you're committed to doing your project right, we're committed to helping you navigate the roough spots. :-) --Keith :mrgreen:

n8nod9it7 February 2007#17
You do have alot of issues, but none that seem more than what some of us have to deal with. See my thread (Cavity filling..not the Dentists job) and you can see where i had to deal with power cables running through my studs where i needed to beef up. I am still working on the exterior walls, its very time consuming but i am about 75 percent done. Heres a trick to beefing up behind the cables with the drywall since its hard to get a full piece up behind the wires... Cut the piece so that you know it will fit in pretty close, then score the back of the drywall through the brown side paper top to bottom. You can then gently break the drywall and fold it over and get the piece to fit in behind wires when other wise a whole big piece would be unable to. When it fits in then you just flatten it out and if your careful, it will not fall apart and should re-align perfect.
