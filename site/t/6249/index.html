Guitard, welcome to the forum; wow, what a great anniversary gift! I've got about a dozen preliminary questions, most of which are listed here http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=3231 But I'll try to answer the ones I can - meantime, if you can review all the bold points in that post it'll help us help you, as well as show you what you need to learn more about. Also, will you be building this yourself or with the help of others, or contracting it out? This can bring a lot of other problems to light, namely the general ignorance of virtually ALL builders about sound isolation :cry: By "bass amps in corners" I'm assuming you meant bass TRAPS - if so, these actually smooth out ALL the frequency ranges in a room and will make a very noticeable difference in the sound within the room. (sound isolation is a totally separate set of problems from having the room "sound good") Staggered stud construction is better done during the building of the house, because it requires wider base plates and caps in the framing (usually 2x6 plates/caps with 2x4 studs) - and insulation in such a wall works better if individual 2 foot wide batts are used for each side of the wall, compressing them a bit where they pass in front of the stud for the opposite side of the wall. See the bottom illustration in attached sketch for more. You didn't mention what kind of wall construction is already there - if it's normal 2x4 stud wall with drywall attached on both sides, you have a few options depending on how much isolation you need, and at which frequencies (guitar isn't as hard to stop as bass, but close in some cases) Some options, from worst to best - Easiest (and least effective, but still better than nothing) is to just add 2 more layers of drywall to the existing walls - if this is done on only the side toward the practice room, it will improve isolation by about 6 dB at all frequencies - a noticeable improvement, but not "sound proof" by quite a bit. If you add a second frame inside the existing, but don't REMOVE the drywall on the old studs, you'll create a triple leaf wall which will DRASTICALLY worsen the isolation at all but mid/high frequencies - this isn't good, because the bass is always hardest to stop so that should be the focus of a room for music. All the other options WILL require that you remove the wallboard from one side of existing frame, or you will waste your time and money. Period. Remove wallboard (inside only), add resilient channels to existing frame, put two layers of wallboard on the channels. Better, but only at mids/highs - bass isolation no better than just adding one more layer to existing walls. Only thing that helps bass isolation is wider air gap between wallboard, and more wallboard. Staggered stud approach is very close to the above, but harder in some ways - you'd need to remove wallboard, rip 2" wide strips for bottom and top plate extension, then add studs between each existing stud but on opposite side of the wall, so they're "staggered". Lotta work for not much gain. Remove wallboard, add a second frame inside and NOT touching the original frame, put 2 or 3 layers of drywall on inside of new frame - getting REALLY good now - wider air gap from wallboard to wallboard + added mass of 3 layers 5/8 drywall gets you close to a point where you won't hear hubby even from INSIDE the house, provided you use these same methods for floor AND ceiling. (sound proofing is a "weakest link" thing - meaning it's only as good as the worst wall is. Window - a VERY weak link. Can be fixed, but isn't cheap if you still want to see out. Outa time for now, please follow the link at the top of this post and cover as many points in it as possible so we know more about your existing construction, etc - BTW, you can use the Paint program in WIndows and actually make a close to scale drawing - for a room your size, use 5 pixels per inch (there's a pixel indicator at lower right in paint program that shows you how long your line is) - so 5 pixels/inch = 60 pixels per foot = 780 pixels for 13 feet. Doing this will show us where your door, window, closet, are and we can use that sketch to suggest changes. Also, holding the SHIFT key while drawing a line forces the line to be either 0 or 45 degrees - easier to draw straight walls that way :=) Gotta go, hope this helps... Steve

knightfly25 May 2006#21
With that small a room, and considering you're gonna need to enlarge the mechanical closet for the new heating system, I'd replace the folding wall with a real double stud wall, NOT try to do angled walls (they eat up too much space), and instead just improve isolation in all walls and ceiling (we need to know how your ceiling is framed - which way the joists run, what size, whether they're part of a truss system, so we can figure out how much extra mass the ceiling can support. Then, for acoustics, corner wideband absorption (probably including the corners between walls and ceilings, since floor space is limited) and some patches of either thick studio foam or cloth-covered mineral wool randomly placed on the walls to kill flutter echo (a parallel wall problem) - if there's carpet on the floor, it may be difficult to balance out the absorption in the room - normally we recommend hard floors and a "cloud" on at least part of the ceiling - this controls flutter as well as lessening any comb filtering effects if hubby does decide to record in there. Flutter can best be explained by having you walk into the room in your house with the least amount of "soft stuff" on walls, and sharply clapping your hands - that "boing" sound is the worst part of flutter, but there are components of flutter that aren't obvious with that test but WILL show up as wierd frequency response problems. "comb filtering" is an uneven frequency response caused by a mic or your ears receiving the same sound at slightly different times, causing a varying phase of the sound - it can be a cool effect, but not if you aren't TRYING to sound that way. As to what to do with your walls, I need to know what your floor and ceiling is constructed of before answering - there's no point in building 63 dB walls if you can't match it with everything else; this also includes the door, by the way - if it's a typical bedroom door, it's probably a lightweight hollow core door with gaps under it you could throw your hat through - if so, it'll have to be replaced as well, and a proper seal kit added (around $300 just for the seal kit, and another $100-150 or so for an exterior SOLID slab door. So, when hubby's not looking you need to crawl around your attic and under the floor a bit to see how those are constructed - not sure what kind of nasty spiders, etc, thrive in Kansas but maybe it'd be a good idea to toss one of those "foggers" people use in such places, then wait a couple of days before going there... Steve
