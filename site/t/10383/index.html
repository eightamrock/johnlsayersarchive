Splayed or squared. Walls in general causes a lot of grief and problems, but, as most people will agree on, they're a necessary evil. To put it simply: rectilinear rooms are easier and more predictable. In a square room you can always angle you treatments to avoid parallel facing walls.

xSpace1 April 2008#7
I don't know a >book< answer. I'll give ya the answer that works for me, up to this point in time. Walls and rooms don't do anything, splayed, parallel or what have you. The only time these walls and things become an issue is when I want to use them for a specific purpose, like sound isolation or musical endeavors. The rectangle shape of a room has long been accepted as a better place to start. You can >hear< the difference of a nice shaped rectangle vs. the square room. In a square room noise can get out of control quick just from bouncing back and forth on the walls of same height and same length. People that build their own rooms by thier own hands, mostly still build rectangle rooms. Most room types are going to have a built in set of acoustical issues. Parallel walls can be fitted with damping material to minimize the effect of reflection. You have seen various acoustical materials installed on ceilings. These are often to break up the parallel effect of floor to ceiling reflection. Studio designers draw splayed walls. There is a lot of math that goes into these rooms. A splayed wall with a hard surface will reflect sound differently then it's flat upright counterpart. So the degree of the splayed wall is taken into consideration. Too much and it will create a potential corner where waves can accumulate. Not enough splay and it has either no effect or possible no mathematically noticeable effect. I can't say hard and fast either way on that:) And this is why designers can do splays. They understand the mechanics involved. They can predict what is going on in the rooms they design. It is not a task for the faint of heart. So each and every wall surface has it's own set of concerns as it relates to the opposite wall surface. Walls are just walls. It's when we try to make them do other things that they become damn walls :) I hope this has been of some service to you. Those that see errors...please help me correct them! (rhetorical answer to rhetorical question) angled boundary walls are generally used because there's a need to conserve space in the design. at the end of the day, you make the best calculations you can based on the design criteria and then work the treatments and adjustments based on measurements. rectangular rooms are easier to calculate but not always practical, angled walls make things trickier but might be necessary so you deal with it.
