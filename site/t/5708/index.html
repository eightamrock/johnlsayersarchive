The angled walls eliminate flutter echo, so let you only use as much absorption as is necessary for first reflection points (including ceiling, which is the purpose of the "cloud" - while Ethan is right about cubic volume, a CR is better with a bit dryer sound so you don't assume that the reverb you're hearing is "in the mix" - so a smaller, more absorbed room can work OK for a CR. If you're in doubt, get a few rolls of masking tape and tape the floorplan out on your floor; it doesn't hurt to even move things into place and do a "walk-thru", so you can see if it'll work for you or if it's too cramped (ergonomics and acoustics don't often go together peacefully, so this is good to know ahead of time) That excercise will help you decide what'll work for you... http://www.recording.org/ftopict-26889.html http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... 1&start=90 Couple of cloud ideas... kendale28 March 2006#8
Aloha Collin, Looking good. :D
#1. I'm assuming that the brown corner walls are to be similar to the design described as corner asborbers in the link: http://www.johnlsayers.com/HR/index1.htm Is this correct?
Somewhat, yes. However, if you decide to soffit mount your speakers, you want to have the area around the speaker face (bezel) a solid surface rather than slats.
Is it ok if they're a bit free standing like this?
They will still work if they're free standing, but if you're in a more permanent situation (unlike myself), and since you'll have to frame the front and side walls anyway, why not just frame the corners as well and build it into the room? You'll have a lot stronger unit overall, and a cleaner looking result.
#2. I'm also assuming that the middle pink wall is just really a frame which is covered with cloth and that has OC 703 behind it. Is this what John was suggesting?
Basically speaking, yes. Small rooms tend to need a lot more absorption. As such, the front wall/ front corners are among the key locations to incorporate as much absorption as possible. The rear wall, rear corners, ceiling and side walls also need to be treated. See attached pics for some examples.
#3. And I hate to even ask this question.... but.... We're considering soffiting speakers within the brown wall. Do you think its worth the trouble or should we just stick with the free standing near fields that we already have? Would it be a good idea to have both? Obviously all that we really need is ONE acurate monitoring system.
There seems to be two schools of thought here. Bryan Giles, Aaronw, Andy Wheeler, and many others have all soffit mounted their monitors. I've chosen to soffit my Mackies HR824's and they sound great. Some of my impressions after soffiting: - bottom end is tighter - imaging is clearer - drums sound clearer, more defined in mixes - able to hear clearer at lower volumes than before - room sounds quieter without music playing, louder & tighter with less effort when music played - less fatigue/more enjoyment in listening to mixes! Another benefit (among the several benefits) to soffit is that it saves precious floorspace. Be sure to allow adequate cooling if you are using a powered speaker. Soffit threads that come to mind: http://johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic ... highlight= http://johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic ... ight=#2596 http://johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4773 http://johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4091 Hope these help, Aloha 8)

kendale29 March 2006#11
Aloha Collin, Glad they were helpful. :D Here's a few pics on acoustic hangers. I've read posts where any of the following materials were used: pink fluffy insulation, rockwool, OC703, or polyester batting over a 1/4" sheet of masonite, fiberboard, particle board, hardiboard, or plywood. Here's an explanation by Steve on how they function:
The actual HANGING part of a trap such as this uses a different principle for absorption - it actually relies on the hanging mass to move a little bit, somewhat similar to the "soft" styles of martial arts - in order for the bass sound waves to MOVE the hanging mass, they have to release energy - then that energy is no longer sound, but (slow) movement. Also, some of the energy is converted to HEAT, same as normal absorbent types of traps. The "normal" way of building such a trap is to line it with the denser rockwool type materials, then wrap the actual hanging parts with lighter, fluffier insulation - part of the purpose of the lighter insulation is to keep the individual boards from banging against each other or vibrating; part is to absorb any higher frequencies as well. As far as I know, there is no good reason to use one type of insulation over another, as long as you do NOT use any of the foam type insulations; but any fiberglass or mineral wool insulation should work. The boards themselves can be hung as close to each other as you can get them and still have them hang freely; the more of them in a space (still free) the better the absorption. It's more efficient if the boards are neither parallel or perpendicular to the entering sound waves, but instead hung at an angle.
The "holes" behind and above the speaker area are for ventilation/cooling for powered speakers. The hole below the 5/8" plywood/insulation/cloth area is to allow the bass frequencies to enter the trap. Aloha 8)

Hey, no worries! :D Okay, according to your skp file, the right hand wall is angled @ 6.5 degrees, but the left hand wall is square @ 90 degree. (This gives you only a 3.25 degree angle for both walls, provided you angle the rest of the layout.) John writes in the SAE (http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html) section on room shape & size -
I believe that a wall must be at least 12 degrees off parallel to stop parallel wall standing wave interference. That's either one wall at 12 degrees or two walls at 6 degrees each. If you can afford to make the angle bigger, do so. Also you can create angled walls within a rectangular room by adding acoustic treatment. (I'll demonstrate that in the pages on wall treatment). (Note: having non parallel walls doesn't entirely stop standing waves - they still form within a room but along different lines of repetition). The main reason for having angled walls in a control room is because of reflection control of the high frequencies for true imaging from your speakers. John Sayers
So one of the principles I was referring to was having both walls angled (at least 6 degrees each) in order to acheive symmetry left/right of the mix position. The few inches lost are more than compensated by the benefits of having a clearer image at your mix position. There are several ways to approach this: - If the left hand wall is already built; start by considering it "already angled @ 6.5 degrees" and layout the rest of the room from there. - If it's not already built, could you angle it 6.5 degrees out into the live room area? - "create" an angled wall using acoustic treatments (this will force you to reconfigure the rest of the room accordingly) If this angle is really all you have, then work with that. Be sure to angle the rest of the room accordingly and use as much acoustic treatments as you need/can/are able to in order to control reflections, especially at the mix position. The second thing would be to center your monitors (horizontally) on the 3'7" soffit face between the side walls and the front/center wall section. This should put the center of the monitor (LF/HF) @ about 1'9" horizontally. Your monitor height – woofer (some say HF) at ear level (avoid mid point of floor to ceiling measurement – either sit higher or lower if necessary) If you want to mount them high you must angle them down so that they point at the engineer. (FWIW, my console stands 37" tall, so I ended up using the "woofer @ ear level" approach.) Finally, regarding the front wall; while there may be several other reasons why John chose to draw it the way he did, one thought that comes to mind is by having it out from the wall a bit further it allows you to have more trapping and treatment, which also improves your isolation. The 6" difference in room length will for the most part be mostly unnoticable. Hope this helps, Aloha 8)
