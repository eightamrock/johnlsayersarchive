The plan is to have one production/control room at 160square feet and a connected live room of the same size
In general, the live room should have a considerably larger volume than the control room, for the simple reason that you want to hear the "sound" of the live room itself in your recordings, and you won't be able to do that if they are the same size: they decay times of the reverberation in the live room will be masked by that of the control room: you won't hear the natural reverb tails in your mixes very clearly. However, depending on what your goals are for this studio, that might or might not be important.
I understand I will lose quite a bit of this space in the walls.
That depends on how much isolation you need (how many decibels), and there are also techniques that allow you to minimize the footprint of the isolation system. For example, if you use materials of higher density than normal, then you can reduce the overall thickness of the walls and still get the same isolation over the same frequency range. However, high density materials are more expensive than commonly used materials, so that might be an issue.

Am I best to go with a brick building or can I get away with timber frame?
Both will work. Isolation can be accomplished with either method, if done correctly. Brick has the advantage of high mass, high density, high rigidity, and therefore good acoustic characteristics. Wood-framed construction has the advantage of being easier to build, cheaper, and requiring a bit more space for the same level of isolation. You could even combine the two: use brick for your outer-leaf and wood framing for your inner leaf: that's a common solution in some parts of the world. This is the way it works: The most economical way of getting good isolation is with a "fully-decoupled, two-leaf MSM wall". What that means is that between you and the outside world, there are two "leaves" of mass separated by an air gap, where "leaf" just means a massive (heavy), rigid surface that could be brick, or could be built up from one or more layers of common building materials, such as drywall ("plasterboard"), plywood, OSB, MDF, etc. So you have your two leaves, and the do not touch each other: there is an air gap between them that is filled with acoustic damping material, such as common insulation (fiberglass or mineral wool). It is that easy. The amount of isolation you get from this method is governed mostly by the amount of mass on each leaf, and the size of the air gap between them, and the amount of insulation in the air gap. There are equations for calculating all this, and there are documents that list literally hundreds of different ways of building isolation walls, showing the precise details of each method and the amount if isolation it actually provides. That's why it is important for you to know how much isolation you need, so you can choose the method and materials that will provide that level.

Sounds about right. That would be about 60-something pounds per square feet, which is roughly what one could expect.
surely a DIY build would be better and cost a fraction of the price
Possibly, if you are going to do most of the labour yourself. DIY saves you stacks on labour, and going with wood-framed construction is something that most people can do. Bricklaying? Hmmmm.... maybe not so much. :) So the short answer is that the very first thing you should do is get that meter, find out what your legal limitations are on noise, and based on that figure out how much isolation you need , then look at the documentation to fins a method that fits your budget and will give you that much isolation. That's the normal process. - Stuart -

To be honest when I say live room I really mean a room that is isolated from the other so if I want to close mic an upright piano or guitar amp I can. Or if a singer doesn't like singing in the control room they can go there. Is it possible to use various acoustic baffles (correct word?) to remove most of the room sound?
"Live room" is more of a generic term for that kind of room, not just for rooms that are very "live", in the sense of reflective and reverberant. I'm glad you choose those two example, as they are very different in terms of what makes them sound good, acoustically. For most types of recording, a piano needs a large space to sound good, with a fair amount of "liveness" (to coin a term). If you track a piano in a room that is to dead, it just doesn't sound right in the mix. It sounds thin and lifeless. So a piano would track well in a large room with a good bit of "ambiance". But a vocal tracked in a large room with lots of ambiances doesn't sound so good: smaller rooms with a bit less ambiance are better for most type of vocal tracking. Of course, there are no hard and fast rules here, about what is "correct", and some vocalists do sound great in large rooms, but in general pianos and drums sound better in larger, livelier rooms, while vocals and some instruments sound better in smaller or "deader" rooms. Yes, you can use treatment in a room to make it dead, but dead rooms are unpleasant. You can also make the acoustics of a room variable, by adding hinged or sliding devices on the walls, so that you can either expose a lot more absorption to the room, or a lot more reflection, or even a lot more diffusion, as needed for each session. Probably in your case, the best would be to build two-sided gobos on wheels, with one side highly absorptive and the other highly reflective, so you can wheel those into position to create the type of ambiance you want. So the LR itself could be fairly neutral, and you could create a deader, smaller sounding space by surrounding a vocalist or instrument with several gobos, with the soft side facing in, or create a larger, more live sounding space by pushing the gobos up against the wall, covering absorptive devices on the walls, and with the reflective side facing the room. Most live rooms are design to have a certain "character", but it makes sense if yo also have the means to change that character as needed.
In order of isolation and cost am I right in guessing Brick > Concrete board > Timber
Depends on where you live! In some countries, brick is like gold nuggets, while in other it is dirt cheap. Some places (like where I live) fiber-cement board is about twice as expensive as drywall, but in other places it is the other way around. Where I live, timber and drywall are both cheap, so that's the way to build a studio here, but in South Africa, for example, timber is very expensive, so metal framing makes more sense than wood framing. So you might well be right about the order of materials where you live. But the point behind it all is that sound waves cannot read price tags, and do not care in the least about how much you pay for your mass: they only react to the mass in front of the, regardless of the cost, so get the least expensive material that will do the job.
So a price/performance compromise might be concrete board for the outer leaf with plasterboard for the inner and rockwool filling?
If that would be the cheapest combination for you, then yes. The only real advantage of fiber-cement board over plasterboard (drywall), is that the fiber-cement is about twice as dense, so it can be half as thick for the same mass and therefore the same isolation. So if a 10mm sheet of fibercement costs 50% more than a 19mm sheet of drywall, then use the fiber-cement, for sure. But if the fiber-cement costs more than double, then go with the drywall, sine it is easier to work with, and thicker.
A kick drum coming through a speaker vs an actual kick drum being struck.
Yup. Or a snare, or a tom, or the overheads... You really do want to be able to hear that properly on the monitors, so you can find the best position for the mic. Mic'ing drums is complicated anyway, but if you can't hear what you are doing as you try to find the best location for each mic, then that's a problem.
I'd guess the frequency response of the monitors would dictate how much sub freqs we'll have to deal with.
Hopefully you'll be using speakers that have flat response down to below the lowest frequency that you'll be recording. And hopefully the control room will also be designed so that the speaker response in the room remains just as flat as the manual says it should be... :) What speakers do you plan to use?
