From your description on the SketchUp image, I understand that all of the inner-leaf walls and the ceiling are single stud frames with a single layer of 13mm gypsum board on them? Is that correct? If so, then I guess that good isolation from the surrounding rooms is not very important for you? Or are there actually more layers of GB on there? Also, it seems like you built your floor on a wooden frame, with a thin plywood subfloor, without any insulation or mass filler in the cavity. Is that correct? Is there a good reason why you did that? My guess is that a structure like that will resonate quite badly at certain low frequencies. It's basically a drum head that you built. I'm not at all sure that your room will work as a live room like that, especially if you are talking about drum kits. Hammering away on a drum kit while it is sitting on a 13mm plywood subfloor over a 3 inch cavity, does not sound like it will do anything nice acoustically. If the cavity depth is what I suspect (around 3 to 4 inches), then the resonant frequency of that floor is almost exactly the same as that of the kick drum.
Its sounding good at the moment but the reverb is messy and long at high frequencies.
How can it be sounding good if you have serious resonance problems? :) It shouldn't have those problems anyway. The predicted overall RT-60 for a room with those dimensions is around 270 ms, which is reasonable. Your worst problem will be in the low end, where the RT-60 will be over 500 ms, but you say you have ringing in the high end? Once again, predicted times for those dimensions for everything above 3.5 kHz is in the range around 170 to 370 ms, which is long but not fatal. You shouldn't be getting flutter echo in there either, since you have non-parallel walls. How about if you measure the actual response of the room, and post a waterfall plot so the experts here can look at it? But before you do anything else, you might want to reconsider what you have done already, because it doesn't sound like it will be helping your acoustics much. It's not too late to fix it, either. - Stuart -

Soundman202030 March 2009#5
Hey Arie. Glad to be of service!
I am building a studio with a live room, control and vocal booth.
OK, maybe you could complete your SketchUp model with the positions of all of those, in 3D, with accurate construction details of how your walls, ceiling and floor are built. The reason I suggest you do that urgently, is because from your descriptions and the photos, I strongly suspect that what you are doing is not going to accomplish what you are hoping it will do. For instance, I see what look like roof trusses inside your "finished" room, which implies that your outer leaf and inner leaf are mixed up somehow, and even worse that they are coupled. You also say: "wall measuring 5760mm divides the live room from control room and is essentially 2 layers of 13mm gib divided by insulation on a single stud frame". Well, that wall is not going to provide much isolation between the rooms. My guess would be in the region of an STC 30-something rating, which is pretty much the same as a normal house internal non load-bearing wall. The kind you'd find between the living room and dining room for example, or between two bedrooms. The level of isolation from that wall is not going to be enough to be able to mic a drum kit in the live room (LR) and be able to hear exactly what the mics are doing on the monitors in the control room (CR). There will be so much bleed of the drums coming through the wall that you'll never be able to be certain of what you are hearing. I'd strongly suggest that you might want to re-think that wall. It should be two separate stud frames, with at least two layers of 16mm drywall on the LR side of one frame, and another two layers of drywall on the CR side of the other frame, and the cavity in between filled with insulation. In other words, going from one side to the other of the entire wall, you would have: two layers of 16mm drywall - stud frame - cavity with insulation - stud frame - two layers of 16mm drywall. That should get you an STC of at least 60, which means that, subjectively, it will be about eight times quieter than what you have now.
The spaces between the angled walls and original walls are filled with leftover carpet and excess insulation.
The carpet is not doing anything useful for you, and in fact is probably doing some harm. It is most likely creating some amount of flanking between the two leaves, if it is packed in tight and touching both sides. Ordinary carpet doesn't have a lot of uses acoustically (although it does have some), and stuffing a wall with is isn't one of them! Ditch the carpet. Also you say that the wall is filled with "excess insulation". That sounds like you just put a bit of leftover stuff in there. In reality, the entire cavity should be filled with insulation, either mineral wool or fiberglass (the usual pink fluffy stuff). At the very least you need a four-inch (12cm) depth of fiberglass that completely covers the surface of one leaf of the wall, between the studs.
Regarding the floor, the cavities are stuffed with old carpet underlay. Do you think this is adequate?
Probably not. While that might damp the cavity slightly, it isn't really the damping that is the problem. It is the fact that you have a cavity with a thin membrane over it. Your floor is basically a resonant chamber, and it appears to be tuned to roughly the frequency of a kick drum. That room is going to be extremely "boomy" when you play the drum kit in there, and probably also the bass guitar. If the floor resonates at, say, 80 Hz, then it will also resonate at the harmonics of 80 Hz, at lower levels. So you will resonance problems at 160 Hz, 240 Hz, 320 Hz, etc. From your description it seems that each of the walls has a different construction technique and a different level of isolation. But the sound waves don't care about that, and they'll just take the easiest path in or out. Basically, if you have three walls the roof and the floor all with STC 60 rating, and one wall with STC 30 rating, well that means that all you get is STC 30. You wasted time, effort and money on the other fives sides. So if you have a great wall directly between the CR and the LR, but the side walls and the ceiling are poor, then sound will just "flank" around your fantastic wall as if it wasn't there. A chain is only as strong as the weakest link. Studio isolation is only as good as the weakest wall. You should build your studio so that all of the walls have the same level of isolation, which usually(but not always) implies the same construction method all around. Soundman202011 April 2009#7
Mixed up? They're definitely physically coupled to each other by the roof trusses and concrete floor.
If the outer leaf and inner leaf are coupled to each other mechanically (excluding the floor), then you have a problem: Your isolation will not isolate well. If there is any solid mechanical link between the inner leaf and outer leaf, then that creates a flanking path for sound to bypass your isolation. That seem to be the case with your build, since your inner leaf walls appear to be connected to your outer leaf trusses.
Ok... thats quite a mission. I see where you're coming from though. Definitely want to hear things through the monitors, not the walls! What do you think would be the best STC rating I could achieve with the single stud wall frame
? With a single leaf stud-frame wall that has a single sheet of drywall on each side and insulation in between, you might get STC-36 if you are lucky. Put another layer of drywall on each side, and theoretically you might make STC-42, once again if you are lucky. Probably more like 39 to 40, in real life. On the other hand, put the exact same two layers of drywall on the outsides of a double stud wall with insulation, an you will get STC-63, which means that it will be more than four times quieter (subjectively), or roughly one hundred times less power transmission through the walls (objectively).
. Ah ha, I have this! I suppose I can splash out on some cheap insulation to fill the rest of the gap, but will it be ok with the air gap?
Yep! Put as much insulation as you can in that air gap, completely covering one of the surfaces. Adding insulation into the gap of a single stud wall will buy you maybe 3 dB of isolation. Adding it to a double-stud wall will get you about 10 dB of extra isolation. Well worth it! - Stuart -
