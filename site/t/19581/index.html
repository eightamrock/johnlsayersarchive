Soundman202028 August 2014#3
Hi David, and Welcome! :) Just some general comments on your post:
Live Room: Dimensions: 19ft 6" length X 15ft 3" Width X 10ft 2" height
That's a nice size, with a decently high ceiling. Lucky you!
Flooring is carpet on concrete
:shock: Ooops! I guess the carpet will be the first thing to go! If the live room was carpeted before, it makes you wonder what else was done wrong...
Reduce width by 2feet but have a slanted wall with Wall #1 being a resonator wall
Is that necessary? Losing 2" of width sounds like a lot. Is there a reason for that? Does the room sound so terrible right now in the mids and highs that you need to sacrifice so much space for slot walls?
Glass would have slight tilt facing floor
What's the reason for doing that? It isn't needed for acoustic, and can actually make isolation worse (reduced air gap = higher MSM resonant frequency). Is the tilt for aesthetic reasons? Glare? Something else? Also, how thick is that glass, and what type of glass is it?
Place Hardwood flooring
:thu: Smart move! As long as there are no air gaps under it: hardwood flooring often does need air cavities. A really good alternative for studios is laminate flooring: that can look almost as good as real hardwood these days: there are some pretty nice looking ones, and the total cost is probably lower than going with true hardwood.
Place Absorption panel in center of ceiling
For a room that size with hard flooring, you'll probably need more than that on the ceiling. I'd suggest several clouds of various thickness at various angles, perhaps some fo them hard-backed.
Control Room: Dimensions: 14ft 3" length X 8ft 7" Width X 7ft 6" height
That's a pretty low ceiling! Do you know the reason for that? Is there any chance you could make it higher? That's also not a very good ratio. In fact, it's pretty bad; it fails two of the three critical tests used by the BBC for deciding if a room can be used as a studio.
Wall #1 - Concrete block with 2' x 4' studs lying flat and home type "pink" insulation with OSB plywood
Are the studs attached to the concrete block? If so, you have a problem... Also, when you say "OSB plywood", do you mean one layer of each? As in one layer of OSB plus one layer of plywood? How thick is each layer?
Wall #3 - Carpet on Wall
:shock: :roll: This sounds very much like it was an "amateur built" place before you took it over! About the only thing missing so far is egg crates on the ceiling... :)
Turn control room width-wise to benefit from the depth of the room
In other words, you will now orient it so that the speakers are firing down the longest axis, and the front of the room will be the shortest wall, correct?
2 Bass traps in corners with monitors in front
have you considered doing proper flush mounting for your speakers (sometimes called "soffit mounting")? That would be the best solution.
Remove built up flooring get an additional 8in.
Excellent! That will also improve the overall isolation.
The 2ft gained from live room makes the room more manageable
Ahhh ! OK, now I get it: when you said you were going to take 2 feet off the LR, I thought that was for the slot walls: now I see it is so you can make the CR wider. That makes sense.
Will try to slant ceiling
I wouldn't do that in your case: the ceiling is already low, and angling it will only make it lower. Even after recovering the 9" from the lousy floor, it's still not enough to justify an angled ceiling, since you won't be able to get enough angle on it to be useful. 12° is what you need (minimum) for flutter echo, and more if you want a true RFZ. You might be able to angle just the front section, but I'd suggest that the best solution is to just hang a hard-backed cloud at an angle. Simpler, less expensive, and still very effective. I brought out the wall 2 inches and going to 0 inches at the door.
Why? That's not enough to make any real difference. And as I pointed out, isn't necessary.
I did angle the window very slightly, I always thought that the angle causes the dispersion of sound to not go back to the singer in a straight line.
You thought wrong, and by angling the glass you also reduced the isolation between rooms, so it is now worse than it would have been by keeping the glass straight. Here's why:
Image not preserved: angled-windows-dont-work.gif
Image not preserved: angled-windows-dont-work-#2.gif
In other words, by not taking our advice, you have wasted time, made your isolation worse, and not improved room acoustics.
I stuffed R30 Insulation in as tight as I could have gotten them.
Bad idea. By "stuffing it in tightly" you have compressed the insulation, thus making it more dense, and therefore reduced isolation in low frequencies, as well as potentially creating additional flanking paths across the wall cavity, linking the two leaves. Insulation should only be put in place sufficiently to just fill the cavity, and no more. Compression increases density, and more dense insulation has worse damping properties for low frequencies So once again, instead of increasing isolation, you have reduced it.
Acoustic Caulking and ensuring that there are no gaps
Wrong again! I see HUGE gaps in there! You have cut large HOLES in the wall, and then poked some electrical boxes through those holes, which basically means that you have totally eliminated all of the isolation you might have had. So you wasted a lot of time and money on all that caulk, since it is doing nothing at all. Those enormous gaping holes for the electrical boxes totally wreck your isolation. It is a fundamental rule of isolation that there can be NO penetrations of the wall at all, except where the missing mass and isolation has been duly compensated for, using the applicable technique. From the photo it also looks like you built that wall on top of carpet! :shock: Is that correct? It certainly looks like the sole plate is sitting on top of the carpet. If so, then you have lost even more isolation like that, and I'm also very surprised that the framing ever passed inspection if it is resting on carpet! You DID get a building permit before you started, and all the legal inspections along the way, right?
DIY Bass Traps
Plus more gaping holes in the wall, and it looks like you only used 1/2" drywall, and did not mud/tape the joints between drywall panels. There also does not appear to be any sealing around the window glass, there seems to be only one pane of glass in that window with no room for the absolutely necessary second pane, and there appear to be no header or cripple studs above the window. And as Brien pointed out, the wall is just a plain old standard fully-coupled house wall, so it would not have gotten you much isolation anyway (30 dB at best) but by adding all the other things you did wrong, I'd be surprised it it gets much more than about 20 dB isolation! If you would have built it properly, you could have gotten about one thousand times more isolation... Then there's the drop ceiling, which Brien also pointed out... If you want good isolation and good acoustics, I'd suggest that you take that wall down again, and re-build it correctly.
DIY Bass Traps
"Traps"? Plural? I only see one bass trap in there, not more, and it is very small. How did you build it? What is it made of? Where will all the other bass traps go? - Stuart -
