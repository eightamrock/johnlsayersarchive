sharward5 July 2006#2
Any idea of the weight of each door? Can you find out? If they're roughly the same, and assuming you plan to add something (either acoustic treatment or another layer of mass, or both) to the door, then I would think it wouldn't matter (i.e., get the cheaper one). Although, be aware that if you plan to add mass, you may want to hang the door yourself on beefy hinges or see if you can get the hinges upgraded (i.e., four instead of three, heavier duty hinges) and perhaps also the frame itself. Where are you considering buying? You may want to see if a custom door manufacturer can do better -- either more for the same, or the same for less. --Keith :mrgreen:

Luftweg5 July 2006#4
If they are really truly both solid doors, I might think that the particle board could be heavier (is the 'birch' door a veneer of birch over some other wood core?)... But of course particle board cannot hold screws or nails good. What I thought was that a particle board core door had a solid wood framing surrounding the core (in order to fasten the hinges, and allow adjustments to edges); if you have to shorten or narrow the door, you may go past the framing and expose the particle board. If in a high degree of moisture, the particle board edges can swell, and even ultimately break down the material.... I have some thoughts of using a dense and stable plywood like baltic birch (some are made with exterior resorcinol glue), built up, to create a door, or to laminate over a solid core door... very much like, and inspired by, the ones in Rod's book (that sandwich a layer of lead sheet between layers of wood). If you've never worked with or seen baltic birch, it's a wonderful material (even the 1/2" stuff has NINE plys without any voids whatsoever, and rivals x-code sheetrock for density; 3/4" has thirteen plys; all plies are birch -- you can even make furniture with exposed edges that looks awesome! (think: nordic/scandinavian)). It's so stable that it's used for jigs in many industries, including machine shops; and if you're worried about strength, forget about it (this stuff is strong). Mostly it comes in 5' by 5' sheets, but the 1/4", 1/2" and 3/4" also comes in 4' by 8 ' sheets. Alas, it's not cheap, but compared to the cost of some doors, it isn't bad at all. http://www.boulterplywood.com/ K
knightfly5 July 2006#5
While checking weight, check that both doors are same thickness; exterior doors are 1-3/4", while interior are 1-3/8" thick. Different hinges are used also due to difference in thickness. If you beef up your door (and even if you don't, preferably) you should use extra long screws (and more of them) when fastening the door's finish frame into your rough openings - also extra studs/blocking on the hinge side can't hurt, so the door STAYS the way you hang it... Steve

Len, I use magnetic seals as part of a 3 seal system on all of my studo doors - GM trunk rubber for the 1st 2 seals and magnetic for the last - It's easy to use the magnetic seals with wood doors - you just install a 1/2" x 1/8" steel "latch" on the face of the wood door. You can get that at any steel fabrication shop - then just prime/paint to suit your taste. Rod
