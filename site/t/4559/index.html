Aside from Keith's warning about shared space, which I totally agree with, it looks like you also have shared rafters as well as the common wall - this means that any inner mass you use for ceiling (hopefully you've followed the top "before you post" thread and read at least the first 3 threads in the REFERENCE section??) MUST be isolated physically from your outer frame. Looking at the common block wall, I have questions about its makeup - are those solid blocks or hollow? Is there really NO MORTAR in any of the vertical joints, or is this an optical illusion? What are your insulation (for heat) requirements between you and the neighbor's side - will both of you heat the adjoining spaces? What is the neighbor planning to do to HIS side of the party wall, and has he already done it? (Be specific and detailed please) Are you planning to record live drums or not? Mic'd bass guitar, or other? What kind of outer wall construction is planned? Specific materials, layer by layer, with dimensions of each material. Door - will you use a single door or two? (looks like the door needs to be pretty good since it's toward the neighbor's little shed) Wall - single frame or double? The only way you'll get a decent stereo image in your space is to sit with your back to the common (party) wall so that the ceiling slopes up to your rear; this will eliminate any early reflections from the ceiling, although you'll need to put a fairly large absorbent, broadband trap across the peak of the ceiling and the common wall. Any other orientation will keep you from having a balanced symmetrical sound field. When thinking about these things, keep in mind that decent isolation requires TWO masses separated by ONE air/insulation gap - this is true of walls, ceilings, floors, windows, doors, etc - so when visualizing what you'll do, first consider your outer mass and make it continuous all around your space. Then do the same with your inner mass. Inner and outer should NOT touch with any hard contact, or isolation will be LOTS worse. This isn't as bad for floor, since vibrations will be partially absorbed by the earth; but you have a COMMON SLAB for both people; this is concrete, which passes sound very efficiently so your neighbor will be getting a LOT of your sound thru the floor. Back to Keith's (unfortunately belated) warning about sharing space... At this point, you have a concrete slab that's too thin to support a SECOND, floated concrete slab, so the best you'll be able to do for isolating your floor from your neighbor's floor is a medium-weight wooden floated floor. To keep the weight down (too-thin concrete for a heavy one) and still get decent isolation thru the common floor, you will need to use taller wood frames than usual, support them on better isolator material and damp the floor layers with expensive materials (green glue) and not-quite-so-expensive mineral wool (complete fill between floor joists, plus light compression of the fill) After you consider all this and answer all questions I've raised, we can get on with salvaging your project so you can come as close as reasonably possible to your goals - but please be aware that some of your previous choices/requirements may make this much more difficult... Steve

knightfly29 October 2005#8
Somewhat in order - Your rafters are tied to your neighbor's rafters with iron plates; this is a HARD CONNECTION and essentially makes the rafters one piece; ANY sound that reaches ANY PART of your rafters (like the part you want to leave exposed) will travel lengthwise thru the rafter, thru the iron plates, into the common wall and the neighbor's rafter, and will get re-radiated into your neighbor's space. This is true of all frequencies more or less. So your plan of "nesting" your "second leaf of mass" up INSIDE the rafters will NOT work. The rafters themselves are part of your OUTER LEAF of mass, and must have an air space (insulation is considered an air space, BTW, as is anything you can blow air through) and then an INNER MASS, which needs to be on a separate frame. By "separate", I mean physically NOT touching, or at least separated by flexible connections. This would include rubber, springs, light gauge metal framing, or no connection whatever. The lack of mortar in block joints will need to be fixed; if your side will be rendered, maybe the cracks can be filled at that time. You want a SOLID mass there, not a perforated or pock-marked one. Speaking of which, I still need to know if your blocks (common wall) are made of SOLID concrete, or if they each have air spaces INSIDE the blocks - if you're not sure what I mean, I'll look for some pictures of each. This is VITAL information. Well, it is kind of double framed but mounted together so I guess that counts a as single frame Yes it does; this will cost you about 10 dB of isolation if you can't change it. If I then create bass traps in the corners will I be able to suffit mount active monitors into those? Yes, but you'll need to follow specific methods in order not to lose the benefits of soffiting; I'll have to cover this in more detail when I have more time. do I have to have exactly the same wall structure behind me to get a good stereo image? This will partially depend on your side and rear wall treatments; mainly first reflection points being absorbed adequately with mineral wool or equivalent. How do I set up the frame so it does not have contact with the block wall and still is stable? Your plywood and gypsum paneling will solidify the inner frame, assuming you're continuing the separate inner frame around the perimeter of the room; then the inner ceiling's framing will rest ONLY on that inner wall. If local zoning requires more stringent seismic bracing, there are a few companies who supply rubber isolated sway brackets. BTW, will floor-based heating affect something here? Definitely; if you intend to use internal floor heating, you can't float a floor at all. The two are not compatible.

knightfly2 November 2005#11
1) Will 2" by 3" framing be sufficent for inner wall structure (ie handle 3 layers mounted)? I'm not sure if Sweden uses "nominal" values for framing lumber as the US does; if those studs are actually 2" x 3", they'll be fine. IF they are "nominal" sizes as used in the US, they'd be actual size of 1.5" x 2.5", and would NOT be strong enough. For better wall performance, you'd want to use 24" centers for framing so it needs to be a bit sturdier than if it were built on 16" centers. 2) Mounting the inner wall frames on the concrete or not? And how, in order to not let it lead sound (I have read about pucks...is that what I should have beneath them?) This would depend on whether you plan on building some kind of floating floor; if so, the inner wall would rest on that floating floor, so would be isolated from the main slab. If no floated floor, then how you mount the inner wall is a moot point since the slab will likely be your limiting factor for isolation between you and your neighbor. 3) The blocks themselves are solid but as we agreed upon earlier, the mortar between them does not fill all the 25 centimeters through. Would an 7 centimeter frame structure filled with rockwool and separated from the common block wall be ok if 2 layers of gypsum are mounted on top of that framing or do I need bigger air gap than these 7-8 centimeters? You're confusing things here maybe - a 70mm deep frame isn't likely to support a double gypsum layer very well. Are you meaning that the frame itself is only 70mm deep, or what? As far as sound control is concerned, a 70mm gap between 2 15mm layers of gypsum and 250mm of concrete will get you about STC 85, with around 40 dB TL at 50 hZ. For purposes of sound control, air gap is measured from solid panel to solid panel; framing that protrudes into the air space doesn't change things enough to measure, as long as the frames do not touch each other... Steve

You could stagger joists parallel to but slightly lower than the existing joists, with the lower joists being firmly fastened to your inner leaf wall framing. This has been described on the forum in several places and I think it's covered in Rod's book -- which, if you don't have it, you should buy right away!

If I'm not mistaken, an "inside-out-wall" has all of the insulation on the inside (i.e., studio side) and then it's covered with fabric (again, on the studio side). If this is the case and you build it this way, I don't think you're going to have to worry about absorbtion in the room - you're going to be building one big absorber. If this is what you end up doing, I can offer one little piece of advice (based on experience :-( ) put some 1x4s on edge between the studs on top of the sole (base) plate. This will give a solid backing that's taller than the 1 1/2" of the bottom plate for you to attach baseboard or cove molding to. I found out the hard way that cove moulding doesn't stick to fabric well. len
