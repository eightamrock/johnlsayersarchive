This site popped up near the very top, when I Googled "soundproof door", so I wanted to boost it a bit, to help out other folks who might be looking for the same thing I'm looking for. I'm not building a studio; all I want is a substantially quieter bedroom, so the wife and I can sleep while the kids are talking or watching TV in the next room. There's some VERY useful info in the "door" thread on this board, here: viewtopic.php?t=1662 The main things I would add: (1) "MDF" stands for "medium density fiberboard". Basically, it's like sawdust held together by glue. It's denser and heavier than plywood, which makes for better sound absorption and deadening. (2) When I went looking for pre-made supplies (I'm in the US, in St. Louis, Missouri), I found that a US supplier (Trustile) makes MDF doors in 3 thicknesses, to any dimensions specified by a purchaser (to 1/8 inch tolerances). These thicknesses include standard 6-panel veneer on both sides. They sell only through local retailers; the Trustile directory provides names and addresses. The 1-3/8" thickness offers a major sound improvement over standard hollow-core doors. The 1-3/4" thickness has become very popular among people who want high-quality soundproofing, for things like bedrooms. A typical interior size weighs about 115 pounds. Cost - about $300. They also make 2-1/4" thicknesses; those are mainly for heavy industrial use, and sound studios. For more info on those types of doors, go here: http://www.trustile.com/techinfo/sound.asp These doors need to be installed with a specialized kit. The folks at Trustile recommended the "Package B" from: http://www.zerointernational.com/ Don't know the details of that yet. They said that a really good carpenter can usually get a 1-3/4" MDF door to fit into an existing door frame, but most carpenters will want to take a hard look at the frame structure, especially along the supporting side, and may want to redo that part of the frame, such as by replacing any pine studs with hardwood unless there is a double-stud structure. So . . . I hope that helps. And hey, this looks like a very nice site. Good stuff here. I used to have an ADAT system with a Mackie 1202 mixer in my basement, in an earlier house, mostly for MIDI keyboard tracks but with a small booth for guitars and vocals. If I ever decide to do something like that again -- but better -- I'll look here first. Cheers (but not too loudly) . . .!

"They said that a really good carpenter can usually get a 1-3/4" MDF door to fit into an existing door frame, but most carpenters will want to take a hard look at the frame structure," They would concern themselves with the jamb structure and how it is secured to the framing, or framing structure as you referred to it as. A typical hollow core door measures in at 1 3/8" so you would most likely have to change the jamb anyway. Blocking at integral points, usually at hinges, are required for a retro fit of a solid door into a conventional hollow core door frame, assuming that the door depth is not more than the existing door depth. Simple process to take off the existing trim, examine the jamb, block as required, usually solid from top to bottom for acoustical purposes, caulk joints that may exist, reinstall trim as required, caulk trim.

Engineer28 January 2011#6
Mr. Sayers - apologies for the inconvenience, and thanks for a great site. Re: the wall Q, Brien indicated that what I'm doing re: the wall is called "beefing up" a wall, and a quick search for "beefing" led to some good info. Basically, a layer of low-density "fluff", to separate MDF sheets, apparently is a bad idea, and I should put in more mass and bulk, instead. Such as 3 layers of 5/8" MDF, glued together by (and also sealed around the edges with) acoustic caulk. I would also add that I've heard that a slight angle, between two flat surfaces, will help prevent the surfaces from "resonating" together. So, I'll use some extra caulk, along alternating left and right sides, to give slight zig-zagging angles to the MDF sheets.

A door is a door and should not have, or rather, if built properly, will not have breaks or require acoustic or any type of caulk in making the assembly per the door specifically. So whatever you are trying to do sounds like something different than making a heavy door. Purchase a solid core door and be done with it...it's just easier and costs less.
