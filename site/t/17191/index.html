Hi RM. Please check the rules for posting: you seem to be missing something... :) Also, you double-posted this, so I removed one of them as they were identical. Anyway, building on the second floor is usually not a good idea, for many reasons: in order to properly isolate the rooms of a studio, you need huge amounts of mass, so the structure of the building has to be able to take a much heavier than normal load. But since you are designing the entire building, including the first floor, you can take that into account in the basic building design. So you might be OK, but it is something to keep in mind. There will be many tons of extra load on the second floor that a normal garage just doesn't have to support, so it cannot be built like a normal garage.

And yeah, I kinda skipped the "Read this before posting"..
Yep! So maybe bow would be a good time to take a look at it? This is the one I'm talking about, (click here).
So I cant really make any choices about the building and its interior.
It's not the interior that I'm talking about! It's the STRUCTURE that matters. In order to isolate your rooms so the neighbors aren't calling the cops on you each time you play, you need to have isolation (sometimes also incorrectly called "soundproofing"), and the ONLY way to do that is with very heavy materials. To isolate that room, you will need thousands of pounds of drywall, studs, insulation, and other things. You cannot put that amount of load on the second floor, if the structure of the first floor is not designed to support it. That's the issue. Normal single-story garage construction just isn't designed to take the huge extra load of a studio on the second floor.
The topfloor will be properly isolated, or alteast thats what Im aiming for.
Are you sure about that? Did you do thr math? Drums are at least 115 dB loud: Most municipal noise regulations specify around 45 dB as the highest permissible level. Therefore you need a floor, walls and ceiling that provide at least 70 dB of isolation. That implies 2 layers of 5/8" drywall on the entire outer leaf, a 30cm air gap, then another 2 layers of 5/8 drywall on the entire inner-leaf. 4 layers of 5/8 drywall weighs about 50 kg per square meter. You need about 80 square meters for your walls, plus another 28 for your ceiling, plus the equivalent mass for your floor (wood, not drywall), over another 28 square meters. That's 136 x 50 = 6,800 kilograms of drywall and OSB, without even thinking about studs, mineral wool, doors, windows, HVAC, electrical, etc. Get your dad to check with your structural engineer, to make sure that the garage structure can handle an ADDITIONAL dead load of at least another 10,000 kg, plus the live load of whatever else you want to put in there. Are you sure that the top floor will be properly isolated? Do your numbers match mine? Did you check that the structure can take an extra ten tons of load without collapsing? OK, so the point is, if you want isolation for a full rock band (enough to meets your local laws), then you better explain to your dad that the structure of his new garage needs to be tougher than normal, to support the very large weight of your studio. If not, I hope he doesn't mind waking up one day to find ten tons of drywall crashing down on his car! :)
I was thinking about a 2 by 4 "control room" with the main PC, the mixer etc. Would this be a good idea or just a waste of money?
ITU, EBU and others recommend a minimum room volume of 45 m3 for critical listening rooms. A 2 x 4 control room, averaging 2.5 m high is 20 m3. Less than half the recommended minimum size. Now, that doesn't mean you can't make a 2 x 4 control room! It just means that it won't be anywhere near optimal in sound, no matter how you treat it. Also 2 x 4m is a bad size anyway, since one dimension is twice the other: it is exactly twice as long as it is wide. You will have serious modal response issues in that room, because all the first order modes of the width will line up exactly with the second harmonics of the length. Bad idea. Also, don't forget that you will lose about 33 cm on each side if you want that level of isolation, due to the MSM system, so your actual interior dimensions will be 1.34 meters wide by 3.34 m long, and 2.17 high, for a total room volume of 9.7 m3 (compare with 45 m3 minimum recommended). Of course, you could reduce the MSM air gap to make the walls thinner, at the cost of isolation... For the same reason, your 7 x 4 live-room space will be down to 6.34 x 3.34, after you have the isolation in place. Now, in both cases, those dimensions do NOT take into account any acoustic treatment: That's just the raw theoretical interior dimensions of the inner leaf. You will still lose at least another 20 to 40 cm on each side, for treatment. That leaves the usable space inside your control room less than one meter wide, and less than 3 m long, and the usable space in your live room about 6m by 3 m (roughly). There are methods you can use to save some space here, but they require more expensive materials. Which brings us to another point: You didn't mention your budget! How much are you prepared to spend on this studio? That's also a key point.
The reason I would like to implent a Control Room is to have a seperated, noise-free room for some mastering for my bands recordings. In this room I would like to get me some monitors, instead of "mastering" on the normal PA.
Definitely! You certainly cannot master at all on PA speakers!!! :shock: You can't even mix successfully on PA speakers! So, if it is mastering that you want to do, then you certainlyneed a bigger space than just 1 meter by 3 meters :!: Mastering studios are most definitely considered to be "critical listening", so you should aim for that 45 m3 volume.
Or would I be better of placing a desk with my PC, screens and the monitors in the regular room, thus increasing my overall volume for the band practise room?
That would probably be the best idea, especially if you want to master. But that will make it much harder to track accurately, especially for noisy instruments like drums. Perhaps what you COULD so is to consider a large space for your control room / mastering room, and a small space for a drum booth? That might be a good alternative. Put your drums in an isolated booth, and everyone else in the control room. You can turn down the volume knob on a bass cab or electric guitar amp, but there's no volume knob on the acoustic drum kit! :) You can't turn it down, so isolate it in a booth, and turn down the knobs on the things that have them.... - Stuart -

BriHar9 November 2011#14
Perhaps a quick description will help. A leaf is a mass-layer. In your diagram, starting at the left, you have your first mass layer comprised of the concrete tiles plus (provided they are well bonded to each other) the wood. Continuing to the right, after the rockwool insulation you have a hardboard layer, this is the second mass layer. After the airgap you have a wood mass layer - number 3. Finally after another rockwool partition, you have your 4th and final mass-layer of hardboard. Although logic seems to imply that more layers should equal more isolation, this is in fact not the case. Search for the keyword MSM (Mass-Spring-Mass).
