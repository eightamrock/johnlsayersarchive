Johnnie22 October 2013#2
Have you considered doing some testing to identify which frequencies in your room actually need to be controlled? The diamond panels look really nice, but don't do them just for looks. They need to serve a known / planned purpose. Looking at your room dimensions, the 18* 20* 8, while it's close to a square... it's not a square. A small amount of distance can go a long way towards the room being good or bad. A Room Ratio: 1 : 2.25 : 2.5 actually comes out ok. The control room ratio though, Room Ratio: 1 : 1.85 : 2 isn't nearly as good. If you really can't make any adjustments to that, I'd seriously recommend that you test the space and treat based off those results.

Bass traps should go in corners, not on walls. All room modes terminate in corners, so that's the best, most effective place for bass trapping. Walls are good for broad-band mid-range absorption, but not so much use for bass trapping.
the floor is also carpeted
Ooops! That needs to be fixed! Carpet is not much good in control rooms!
at the back of the room is 1 or 2 QRD(s) with 13 or 17 wells (same length as the sofa). do i need a QRD or will a skyline or diffusion foam panel be sufficient?
Just plain absorption panels on the rear wall is all you need. That room is way too small to be able to use numeric-sequence diffusers.
a further 2 Bass traps in the corners at the back of the room to help with the low end.
YEs, but they are in the wrong place! You have them in the MIDDLE of the corner, but they should be at the top and bottom (up at the ceiling, and down at the floor), where they have the most effect. Or even better still, make them full length traps, from floor to ceiling.
again i know the dimensions (and also the picture) look square or bad dimensions however the left wall (bottom of the picture) has a very lite slope and causing the bottom of the room (by the drumkit) to be narrower than the top of the room, causing the room to be less square or help break up the reflections.
You need to have an angle of at least 12° in order to break up flutter echo reflections. You can do that with any combination, such as angling both walls by 6° each, or one wall 9° and the other 3°, or any combination you like. As long as the total is more than 12°, then you will get results. Less than 12° does nothing useful.
i have done alot of calculations with a few calculators, the perhaps i should have posted the results
calculations are one thing, but actual acoustic response is usually different. I think Johnnie was suggesting that you should do some actual tests in your room, with the REW acoustic software, to see how it is behaving right now, then design your treatment based on real results, not predicted results.
we dont really have access to testing gear or software
REW is free! You download it from the Home Theater Shack web site. It runs on any practically any PC, and also runs on some Macs. All you need in addition to that is a good studio speaker and a good omni-directional mic with flat response. That's it.
i have got a small diaphragm condenser but its a cardioid not able to work in Omni, out off curiosity would it readings still work with a cardioid pattern?
It would not give you accurate results, but it could still give you a rough idea of how the room is working right now. But a good measurement mic is not expensive: you can get them for less than US% 100. And you WILL need one anyway, when you get to the point of actually treating the room. So you might as well buy it now, and do the initial "baseline" testing with it.
and where would the best placement for the mic?
For the control room, set it up in the exact place where your head will be while you are recording and mixing. For the live room, take several readings, and one of those should be with the speaker in one room corner while the mic is in the diagonally opposite room corner. Another reading with the speaker roughly where you will have instruments set up and the mic roughly where people might be listening. And a few more readings at typical places where people might be playing / listening. The idea is to get representative readings that show the overall response, so you can figure out if there are general problems that affect all locations and need major treatment, or other problems at specific locations that can be fixed perhaps even with movable acoustic panels, such as gobo's.

I think you need to check your calculations! The Schroeder frequency for your room (critical frequency) is 129hz: Bob Golds' also shows it.
which afaik, any thing above this frequency will not directly be be affected by the standing waves caused by the fundamental freq of the room. Which in this case is 24/25hz
Not really. The Schroeder frequency is the mid point of the transition zone between modal dominance and reflective/diffusive dominance. Modes are still relevant for at least one octave above Schroeder, and diffusion/reflection are still relevant down to at least one octave below.
the fundamental freq of the room. Which in this case is 24/25hz
Not correct. The lowest modal frequency for your room is 40.4 HZ, which is your 1,0,0 axial mode. That is the one that occurs between the front and rear walls. The room has no modal support lower than that. The next highest mode is your 0,1,0 axial at 43.5 Hz, which is across the width of the room. The comes the width/length tangential (1,1,0) at 59.3 Hz, followed by the one that is probably causing one of the issues you are hearing: your 0,0,1 axial mode, at 75.3 Hz. That is your fundamental height mode, a standing wave forming between the ceiling and floor. There's another dozen or so after those that are still potential problems, before the modal density gets to be reasonably high.
I am puzzled on what sort of bass trapping to use to try and clear up the low end effectively.
The best, most effective, and least expensive is superchunks in the corners, or at the very least 703 straddling the corners.
2. 3 Helmholtz Perforated Trap (holes) and tune it to 24/25hz or 48hz and hopeful tame the harmonics in the series
That would not work: Firstly, because you don't have any modes at 24 or 48 Hz, secondly because you don't have enough spare volume to be able to treat those modes effectively (you need at least 1% of the room volume per trap to do that), and thirdly because it is extremely difficult to tune resonant traps to hit the exact frequency of any specific mode: Modes are normally very narrow band, perhaps 1/48 octave or even narrower, and building a device to be that accurate at such low frequencies is a daunting task. There's also the issue of how you would even measure the exact frequency accurately enough, to start with...
