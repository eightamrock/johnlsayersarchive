Hi Jay, "I figured it would most likely be on an angle to keep the rooms from being square." Being square is most certainly something to avoid since you increase the modal activity, the only thing worse is a cube. But changing the orientation of a wall isn't to avoid a square room it is to reduce flutter echo in the effort to create a smoother response from the room. It's more than what you think it is so I would spend a few weeks getting my head around some of the basic concepts, reflection free zone (TM) (control room), non-parallel walls (tracking room), overhead isolation techniques (ALL rooms). "so I figured a standard tile style drop ceiling would be sufficient." The thing with what you are suggesting currently is that the ICF (insulated concrete form) have a pretty high sound isolation ability. So when you take that high isolation ability of the walls and then dismiss the overhead ceiling with a drop ceiling, you can expect the contained sound from that basement to make every effort possible to get into the upper rooms, since there is little or unequal mass in this overhead cavity to suppress it. Looking into room-in-a-room, mass/spring/mass or mass/air/mass will help you get some more information directly relating to this. Sound is like a balloon in that once it starts it goes in all directions like blowing up a balloon, so the overhead ceiling becomes your weakest part of the rooms ability to isolate sound. And that works both ways, the sound can also get back into your recording environment from the above rooms from simple footfall, loud TV or home theater, sub-woofers, et. al. And don't forget, NFL pre-season is right on top of us and that means noisy room mates ;) It's something to consider, Brien

and I'm probably going to build a 2x4 frame ceiling with drywall but not exactly sure how to attach it.
Assuming that you need isolation between rooms, and isolation between inside and the real world, then the inner-leaf ceilings will have to rest on top of the inner-leaf walls, and nothing on the inner leaf can even touch anything on the outer leaf. And I doubt that 2x4 joists are going to be good enough, unless you are talking really short spans. Those joists have to support a huge amount of weight, and 2x4's can't do that for spans of much more than a few feet. You will most likely need 2x8's or even larger. - Stuart -

Soundman202017 August 2010#9
so cooling isn't an issue either.
You are going to be building a hermetically sealed airtight chamber that is wrapped with many inches of insulation on all sides, and filled with lighting, people and equipment, all of which emit heat. People also have this bad habit of breathing, and if they don't do it quite a few times per minute, the tend to die after not too many minutes. :) So you might want to re-think your HVAC plans. You will need cooling, and you will need fresh air coming in, and stale air going out. Maybe the ducting that you have is no use for that, but you will still need to figure in ducting for the new HVAC system.
So the 2x8 joists should be mounted on the inner leaf of the walls and not fixed to the bottom of the house whatsoever?
Correct. And in turn the new inner-leaf walls will rest only on the concrete floor, without touching the existing walls, or the existing floor above. Also, don't take that "2x8" comment as being written in stone! You need to do the calculations and figure the load and deflection, then check span tables and type of wood, to determine just how to dimension those joists. The load, in turn, depends on how much isolation you need, which determines the amount of mass that you will need on your ceiling leaf.... etc.... It's a whole interconnected process. You can't just guess. And you might want to get a structural engineer to look over your design and calculations, to make sure you are doing it all safely.
This way there should be an air gap between the ceiling and the bottom of the house frame as well as between the control room walls/ceilings from the live room.
Exactly. The depth of the air gap and the amount of mass on each leaf determines the resonant frequency of the wall system, and it has to be designed such that the resonant frequency is way lower than the lowest frequency you need to isolate. The air gap will also be filled with insulation material, such as fiberglass or mineral wool.
Since the support beams are mounted to the cement floor of the basement and the steel beam frame of the house, I assume this will result in some transmission of vibrations to the upper floor,
Correct. That is commonly referred to as a "flanking path", which is just some route that sound takes to bypass your isolation. I'm assuming that your basement floor is a concrete slab on grade, which is great, and in that case the flanking from floor through floor, walls, poles, steel beams, etc. is not going to be too much of an issue, but probably still needs to be dealt with.
should I just box the beams in with wood/drywall? they are round with a 6"x6" base on the top and bottom.
Or you can try to design your rooms such that the poles end up inside the air gap of the dividing walls. If that isn't possible, then you can do what you say: box them in, but in such a way that the "box" does not touch the pole at all: The box can only touch the floor and the inner-leaf ceiling, but it must not touch the pole itself. If it does touch the pole, then flanking becomes a much bigger problem.

Soundman202017 August 2010#11
Haha, yes I know it will get warm in there, but the performing will be done on the side of the basement with 3 windows, so there is some ventilation there..
Actually, those windows won't be any help at all, since they will be sealed behind several layers of drywall and multiple inches of fiberglass insulation! It seems that I haven't managed to explain the concept of isolation yet, so let me try again: Sound is like water: it takes the easiest path out. Your room is like an aquarium, in the sense that if you want to isolate your room, you MUST enclose it on ALL SIX SIDES, just like you have to put glass on all sides of an aquarium. If you only put walls on three sides of your room, then its the same as trying to build an aquarium with glass on only three sides: it wont work. It doesn't matter how thick the glass is on those three sides, or how beautifully you sealed the glass in place, the water will simply ignore all of that, and gush out through the sides you didn't do. Same with your room: if you leave out a wall, or have an open window, then you entirely blew away all of your isolation. Period. Gone. Nothing left. Like I said before, if you want to isolate your room, then the ENTIRE ROOM must be sealed hermetically, air tight, on all sides. No gaps. No operable windows. Not even a tiny little crack underneath one wall, where you forgot to seal. Even doors need multiple seals. If you don't do it perfectly, then you might as well not do it at all. ANY gap that you leave is the place where sound will leak out. In other words, to isolate your room, you wont be able to use those windows for ventilation. You need HVAC.
I might have to figure out something because installing a hvac system above would lose me more ceiling height.
Not necessarily. If you design it well, you can run the ducts in places that don't matter much, and that won't take out any headroom.
and the dividing wall between the control room and live room also need to be on this frame for supporting the house.
No it doesn't! You haven't built the dividing wall yet! Therefore it does NOT need to "support the house", since the house is already supported very well, without it! The dividing wall will just be part of your MSM system: one half of it will be the inner leaf for the control room, and the other half will be the inner leaf for the live room. And none of it will touch the existing walls or the existing ceiling (the floor above you).
So I would have to attach some joists so the studs of the inner leaf walls because the top is fixed to the bottom of the house frame..
No you don't need to do that at all! Each inner leaf is totally self-supporting, and cannot be attached to the outer leaf at any point. You cannot attach the walls to any part of the existing structure. The simply sit on the floor, and that is it. Let me backtrack and explain... Right now, you have an outer leaf, which is the entire basement. It is already doing a great job of holding up the house, all by itself, and doesn't need any help. Inside of that outer leaf, you are going to build two sealed "boxes". One box will be called "live room" the other box will be called "control room". Both boxes will sit on the existing floor, but they will not, and cannot, touch anything else. They cannot touch the existing walls, or the existing ceiling, and the cannot even touch each other. That is the principle behind sound isolation. Each "box" is its own inner-leaf. the basement is the outer leaf. The boxes have to be hermetically sealed, and there has to be an air gap around them, filled with fluffy insulation. That's the only way to do it.
if this causes me to lose some of my isolation it's not too big of an issue.
Actually, it would not cause you to lose "some" of your isolation: It would cause you to lose a large portion of it! Any flanking at all bypasses all of the money, effort and time that went into building the rest of the structure. Unfortunately, sound isolation is an "all or nothing" proposition. You can't have "just a bit of isolation on the ceiling, but don't worry about the walls". That simply wont work, any more than an aquarium with "just a bit of glass on the bottom, but don't worry about the sides" would work. Any place that sound can get out, around, or through your isolation means that all the rest is wasted effort.
I'm not some huge producer (yet) and this is mostly a hobby of mine.
That's pretty much true for most of us here on this forum, who are building their own studios. Some are professional producers, but many of us are hobbyists, just like you. Unfortunately, the sound waves don't care how professional you are, whether this is just a hobby or not, or how much cash you have available to invest in the room: All the sound waves care about is the laws of physics. You can't bribe them with lots of money to take a different path, and you can't get their sympathy by telling them that you are just a hobbyist, and can't afford to put up good isolation. They really don't care. Sound waves are impartial, and treat us all the same. They simply react to the physical objects around them, period. If the objects create a fully enveloping barrier that they cannot get through, then the stay in the room, where you want them. But if the objects around them do NOT create an enveloping barrier, then they escape. It's that simple. - Stuart -
