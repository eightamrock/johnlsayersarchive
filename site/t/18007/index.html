A quick comment on your planned materials: Sound waves do not care how much you pay for the mass that stops them. They can't read the price tags anyway, and all that matters to them is how much mass they find in front of them. So just get the least expensive mass you can find, which is normally plain old 16mm fire-rated drywall. They can't read the price tags on the studs either, so if there is a cheaper option for studs, such as plain old 2x4s or normal steel studs, then I would go with those. There are no ways around the laws of physics, and no magical materials that can somehow bypass those laws. Sound waves react to just two main properties of the barrier in front of them, regardless of the price. They see "mass" (weight) and they see "stiffness" (rigidity). So if you put enough mass and enough stiffness in the way, that's all you need. I'm not knocking the products of any particular manufacturer, and they might well work exactly as advertised, but the simple issue is this: you can save a lot of money by using common, cheap building materials, instead of exotic expensive ones. Having said that, if it turns out the the products you mention are cheaper in your area than 2x4s and ordinary drywall, then by all means use them! The point is this: don't waste money unnecessarily. You are on a very, very tight budget anyway, if you plan to do that room for just 5k, so you had better make every cent count!

Once again, I think I'm not understanding what you are proposing, but the correct way to do this is just to build a simple stud frame about one cm away from the existing brick walls, all around the room, and put a layer of 16mm drywall on only ONE side of that. Fill the cavity between the drywall and the bricks with insulation. Done. That's all you need. If you need more isolation than that, then add a second layer of 16mm drywall directly on top of the first layer, with or without Green Glue in between, as needed. And you can get even more isolation by adding a third layer directly on top of those two. Also, isolation should not be a "trial and error" shot in the dark. Rater, you should first determine how much isolation you need, in terms of real world object decibels, then look at the literature to find methods and materials that are know to give you that much isolation, and that fit your existing situation and budget. In acoustics, guessing is a bad idea. Much better to use the science and research that has already been done, rather than becoming your own guinea pig.
Q3: Should I offset the walls slightly and if so, what angle would be recommended for which wall(s), taking into account the fact the shower stall breaks up the square nature of the room?
That depends on what you are trying to achieve. In a live room, non-parallel walls are useful for breaking up flutter echo, and if that is your goal then there needs to be a 12° differential between facing walls. So you could angle one of them 12°, both at 6°, one at 10 and the other at 2°, or whatever else floats your boat. But flutter echo can also be dealt with in other ways (treatment), and angled walls can waste space if not done intelligently, so there are pros and cons. The room can sound good either way.
Q4: Should the studs be decoupled?
For isolation, yes, absolutely they must be decoupled. The inner-leaf wall, which consists of the stud frame with the one, two or three layers of drywall on just ONE side of the studs, cannot touch any existing wall at all, nor can it touch the existing ceiling. It is built as a separate, self-supporting, stand-alone structure that rests on the floor, period. Likewise, the new inner-leaf ceiling will rest ONLY on top of those new inner-leaf walls, without touching any existing walls or ceiling. That is the whole point and concept of "room in a room". The inner "room" cannot have any mechanical contact with the outer "room".
Q5: As the second (right, smaller) studio will be a room-within-a-room,
But you just described building the LEFT one as a "room in a room" too! So they will BOTH be "room in a room". In other words, you will build two new rooms next to each other, inside the existing garage shell. That's all you need to do. One stud frame for each room, not touching anything else, and drywall all over just one side of those studs. Simple, cheap, effective.
Furring channels will be horizontal.
You do not need furring channels: each room is a simple stud frame, nothing more is required.
Q6: As the ceilings are already quite low, can you provide me with some reference material as to ceiling design whereby the ceilings, including 13mm Soundshield, will be approximately 70mm in depth (total)? This will mean the ceilings will sit in between the 2 beams?
You could build the ceilings using the "inside out" technique, which means that the stud bays would be exposed to the room, and therefor can be used for your acoustic treatment. That can save you a lot of room height: You will need thick acoustic treatment up there anyway,so if you build your inner-leaf ceiling conventionally (drywall facing down, studs facing up), then you lose even more height. But even so, I can't see how you can lose only 70mm of height. You are going to need at least 2x6 joists to span those distance, and maybe even 2x8, depending on the wood and how you build it, so you are going to be losing at least 20 to 25 cm, no matter which way you build it.
