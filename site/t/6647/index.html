sharward18 July 2006#5
At the very least you should consider making sure that no walls are parallel to each other. Basically, no more than one corner at 90 degrees. That will improve the acoustics of the room quite a bit, even without spending money on treatment, because sound won't be bouncing back and forth and back and forth between the parallel walls like a ping pong ball. You'll also want to determine whether or not the build will require a building permit. I'm betting that the air conditioner in particular makes it a sure bet that a permit is required. knightfly19 July 2006#8
Scott's right, the bigger the room volume (cubic, that is) the better the sound. The reason for sloped ceilings is partly to control flutter echo (stand in an empty room sometime and clap your hands; the "boing" you get is extreme flutter, but if he want's to record in there later then even small amounts of this can mar the sound quality. If you were to build a room that's 10' wide by 14' tall by 17' long, the modal distribution (evenness of sound, basically) would be pretty smooth; if you use 24" centers on the framing and stay with a single layer of either plywood or sheet rock 1/2" thick, these panels will act as bass absorbers so less IN-room treatment will be necessary for good quality sound. If you could make those dimensions the AVERAGE, as in, build the long walls so they are NOT parallel by about 1 foot in ten, then slope the ceiling so it AVERAGES 14 feet (like, from 13 to 15 feet lengthwise), and finally build at least ONE of the shorter walls so it sticks INTO the room at its center by about 6-8 inches, then you would need only some corner absorption (like 4" thick mineral wool) across the corners to take care of most of the acoustics. This is generally what I meant by all the blather in the previous paragraph - if your pole barn is similar to ones we build around here, it's on 12 foot centers so this should fit into about 1-1/2 "bays"... Steve

knightfly19 July 2006#12
Electric - measure the voltage between the two "hot" leads on those circuits, and if it's ZERO it's OK to use both. If it's 240 volts, then ONLY use one of them for lights/HVAC and the OTHER for music gear. Keeps noise and shock potential down. Floor; should be fine, in Austin I'd put 6 mil black poly down on the dirt first THEN blocks THEN framing. Carpet - better without; carpet only absorbs high and high mid frequency, so won't give a balanced sound. Better to keep the floor bare (maybe a throw rug or two) and put more of that mineral wool absorbent here and there on the ceiling if the room has too much treble once it's complete. Do NOT, repeat NOT, put any inexpensive FOAM in the room for acoustic purposes; if in doubt, google the phrase great white fire and you'll understand... Steve

sharward20 July 2006#23
I don't know if this is typical around the country, but permits here expire after six months and each inspection extends the expiration date by another six months. Projects with expired permits are deemed to be "abandoned" and in order for inspections to continue (and, ultimately, to get a certificate of occupancy), one must apply for a permit again as though the project were new. This means that one must be in compliance with whatever code changes that may have gone into effect between the first permit being granted and the second permit being applied for. That can be a royal pain if there are significant code changes that apply to work already completed, even if past inspections were passed. :!: In my case, I went past my six months, since my slab work took me so long to complete. I was able to get a one-time courtesy extension of six months to keep the permit alive. In the unlikely event that I need any more six month extensions, it will cost me 50% of the original permit fee. Generally speaking, it is not the building department's responsibility to ask the permit holder if they're ready for inspections. Rather, it is the permit holder's responsibility to call and schedule them. If you get a permit and don't follow through with the inspections, the last of which earns you an official certificate of occupancy, then it's an illegal build. :roll: Applying for and getting the permit is only the first official step. You've got to follow through... --Keith :mrgreen:
