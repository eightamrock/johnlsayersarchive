Soundman20209 September 2009#2
Two things: 1) That looks like plastic sheeting. If you already have a vapor barrier in your wall, using that plastic sheeting might end up creating a second vapor barrier, which is probably not a good idea. 2) Yes, you can compress the insulation a bit without causing any problems, provided that you don't over-do it. I'd use wire mesh, string, plastic strapping tape, or something like that to keep the insulation in place. That's what I'd do. Perhaps others have a better method. But the big question is: why do you want to compress it? - Stuart -

Soundman202012 September 2009#21
with 35 cm(approx 1" and 2') airgap.
That isn't much of an air gap! The normal recommendation is for at least 4" of air gap, and more is better. A bigger air gap has a large impact on isolation, and also reduces the resonant frequency of the wall system. Are you SURE that is your air gap you are talking about? I don't see how it can be, and you certainly don't want to compress six inches of insulation into a 2 inch gap!. Or is that just the gap between the FRAME and the brick wall? That would only be your air gap if you are building inside-out: is that the case? To me, it sounds like you have a nearly six-inch air gap, which consists of that two inch gap between the frame and the wall, plus the nearly four inches of depth in your studs. In which case I don't see the need to compress the insulation at all! If it is six inches thick, and you have a nearly six inch air gap, then it will fit in that gap really well already, with only slight compression. If I were you, I'd just put it in there as is. It will completely fill the cavity, which is a good thing, and isn't dense enough to bridge the gap and cause flanking.
The reason I'm asking is becouse I think I've heard somewhere on this board that compressing insulation between studs towards drywall is to get good contact with the drywall, hopefully damping out resonance inducted in the drywall.
I don't recall reading that here, but the reason why you want insulation in the cavity is not really related to damping the resonance of the drywall itself (which will be a fairly high frequency), but rather to damp the resonance of the entire MSM system, as well as to drive down the resonant frequency of the system, and thirdly to kill flutter echo in the cavity.
But I wonder, will the design somehow be degraded by the closed plastic wrapping, not letting air pass the insulation to the airgap?
Like John said: he has done it successfully. Air does not need to pass through the insulation: sound does, but not air. The plastic will probably reflect back some of the highs into the room (if you are building inside.out walls), but it wont have much effect on the lows. But it seems to me that you are a bit confused about some of the terminology and the dimensions.
