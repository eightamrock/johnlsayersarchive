xSpace30 January 2009#2
Two things you can do. One is to remove the hard connection with the overhead joist for both walls. The closed cell rubber between them probable is of no use . This is a short circuit area that allows vibration to go from one wall, up into the overhead floor system and back down into the opposite side wall. You can understand how this is defeating your purpose? The other would be to add mass into the overhead cavity of this 14 inch space and then install insulation after that as usually.

Soundman202030 January 2009#4
What xSpace was telling you is not an "either/or" option. It is a "both" option. You need to do BOTH of the things he said, not choose between them!!!
There is no way to remove the header from the joist at this point so mass is my friend?
Well, mass is your friend, but flanking is your enemy! :) And in this case your enemy is going to beat your friend, unfortunately. The war is already lost. Your friend is already defeated, before the battle even starts. Mass will only help up to the point where the flanking path transmits more sound around your mass, than your mass is stopping. Think of it like a leaky roof in a thunderstorm: you can apply all the plastic, sheet metal, tar, tiles, bitumen, sealant, caulk and everything else you can think of, all over the roof, but until you fix the six-inch hole in the middle of the roof, the rain is still going to come in. The rest of the roof might be fantastically sealed (mass), but the hole in the middle is the problem (flanking). Your direct mechanical connection between the two walls (called the "flanking path") is the acoustic equivalent of a six inch hole in your roof. Unless you can fix that, no amount of mass is going to help much, just like no amount of sealing is going to stop the rain coming in with the example of the roof. You have to fix the hole, to stop the rain. You have to break the flanking path to stop the sound. You cannot beat the laws of physics. Not even by wishing them away! I'd seriously consider ways to break that flanking connection, and re-build at least one of those walls, preferably both. If not, then you have put in a lot of effort that is going to go to waste. And you will have to put in even more effort, knowing that it will not bring any useful return. Not to mention the money... What is the reason that prevents you from taking down those walls and re-building them correctly? Time? Money? Pride? Lack of workers to help you? Lack of space to build what you want? There are solutions to all of those... - Stuart -

xSpace4 February 2009#15
You want to do the mass addition directly to the overhead subfloor. I wouldn't screw it into the subfloor, instead, use a 1X2 or 2X2 cleat and press this material up against the drywall in the overhead cavity, then screw the 1X2 cleat[or 2X2, which ever you have] to the joist(s) to support the rock. Caulk the connections that the cleat makes with the joist AND the drywall. Add insulation into the cavity. Then I would install another set of cleats as described above, only have them raised above the bottom most part of the floor joist enough to install 2(two) more layers of rock. It's kind of a two fer, maybe a three fer. This makes a mass/air/mass between your upper and lower area. It also closes the open ceiling line so that this is not a weak spot in the event of fire.
