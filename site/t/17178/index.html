Metric is fine. Most of us here understand both metric and imperial, and for those who don't, they can change the settings on SketchUp to show your plans in imperial.

There's a big difference between isolating for acoustic guitar and vocals, and isolating for drums. About 20 dB of TL! That's a lot. So we know how loud you will be: either around 95 dB or around 115 dB, but you didn't say how quiet you need to be: What are the municipal noise regulations for your area? What is the maximum permitted noise level? That will decide how much isolation you need. Call your municipality, and ask for a copy of the regs.
Isolation is only needed for the "live-room". beacause my wife sleeps 2rooms further. Neighbours are not directly connected to my house.
Yes, but that's not a number! You need to put a decibel number to how quite you have to be. "Wife sleeping two rooms away" might be fine at 90 dB if she sleeps heavily, or might be a huge problem at 60 dB if she sleeps lightly. Not a good guide: Get a sound level meter and put a number on that. "Neighbors not connected" is also not a number: the legal limit is, so also get a copy of those regulations, to see what that number says.
Mixing has a moderate loudness.
Once again, that's not a number! :) Measure what you mean by "moderate loudness" on a real sound level meter. But your control room should be calibrated for 85 dB, regardless of how loud or quite you mix: That's pretty much the industry standard.
As you can see there is a big window and a glass door (to enter a small terrace), and i would like to keep them somehow
Depending on what your isolation requirements are (not your guesses! :) your actual numbers), you might be able to keep the window just like it is, or you might need to build another identical window, a few inches away. By the way, the "movable curtain absorbers" over that window will do nothing at all to help isolate the room. They MIGHT do something to help treat the room, but that depends on many things... Your SketchUp also shows a "floating floor" in the live room: Why do you need that? You didn't say what the existing floor is made of, but I'm guessing it is concrete, since you seem to be on the ground floor. So if that's the case, why do you want to float your floor? Especially considering that you only have 2.6m of ceiling height! You'd lose a lot of that if you try to float. And you also don't have enough budget to float a room properly. - Stuart -

Soundman20201 November 2011#11
the maximum permitted soundlevels are: 50db from 6am -10pm 35db from 10pm - 6am
OK, so to not break the law, your levels need to be down to 35dB, which most likely means measured at the location of the person complaining. So if your level outside your wall is around 40-something, you should be OK. Call it 40, for safety. On the other end of the scale, your monitoring will be 85 dB, vocals can hit over 100 dB, and drums can hit over 115db. Assuming that you drop the idea of the drum booth, your loudest problem is vocals. Call it 105, to be safe. 105 - 40 = 65 That should be your goal for isolation of the vocal booth. 85 - 40 = 45. That should be the goal for your control room. HOWEVER! Those are just rough estimates: you can't base a build on rough estimates! You need to actually get a meter and actually measure how loud your monitoring is, how loud your vocals and guitars are, and how quiet you have to be outside your walls in order to get 35 dB at your neighbor's property line.
The "floating room": The room is tiled now. But i want to have laminate floor in it. looks much better. I would like to put a floating timber floor on it. Is it so expensive? With a ceiling i will stay round about 2.20m - 2.30meters height?
Well, which is it? Is it "floating timber floor" or is it "laminate floor"? That's two very different things! If you want laminate flooring, then it is very simple: take off the existing tiles, level the floor below, and put down laminate flooring. Done! But that's not a floating floor. A "floating timber floor" means that you have to build a mechanically isolated structure, supported on some type of spring system, with a large air gap underneath and a very large amount of mass on the floor itself. You do all the calculations to tune the resonance of that floor to a frequency well below the lowest frequency that you need to isolate. It is not easy to do, and is very expensive.

i didn´t know, that it is not necessary to float the floor for good isolation.
That's OK! Many people who come here do not know that. I was one of them... :) It took me a while to figure out that floating a floor is not necessary 90% of the time: there really aren't many cases where a home studio needs to be floated. In fact, there aren't that many cases where a large professional studio needs to be floated! If the studio is built on top of a concrete slab on the ground, then that 0s usually fine. Only if you need very high levels of isolation, only then do you need to float. Doing it right is not easy, and expensive, and if you do it wrong then it does not isolate at all! In fact, doing it wrong can the situation worse than not doing anything. They German guy with the drum booth is a classic case: there was no need at all to float that room, and it is done so wrong that it just isn't working. That's obvious, since he himself admits that his $3,500 "floated" booth is only getting about 25 to 30 dB of isolation.
