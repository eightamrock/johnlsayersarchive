100 SFr. will pretty near cover the cost of a few panels. Isover fiberglass insulation 20mm thick 600 x 1200 can be bought at Coop or OBI for about 65 Sfr. a bundle. Construction grade (i.e. unplaned) 20mm x 40mm lumber from the same sources will cost @ 15-20 SFr. Fabric from Coop or Migro will take the rest of your budget. OK you'll need glue (wood glue for framing and UHU spray adhesive for bonding the FG panels to each other and the frame). You'll also want screws or nails for the frame construction, and staples for attaching the fabric to the frame. I'd say getting yourself a few of these absorbers built will be your next priority. Truthfully, 100 SFr. won't be enough - double it (2 bundles of FG) and you can build 5 - 6 absorber panels - remember youll want a couple hanging from the ceiling as clouds.

Soundman202014 February 2012#34
do I really need hundreds of dollars of room treatment? I'm a hobbyist :cry:
That depends on how good you want your room to sound! :)
If I only had $100, where should I use them?
If all I had was US$ 100, I would not expect outstanding results: just a small change. I'd use that to buy as much cheap fiberglass insulation as I could (around 30kg/m3), put two thirds into bass traps in the rear vertical corners, and the other third into the first reflection points.
My take is that I need to bass trap treat the primary reflection points first and foremost;
Bass traps and first reflection points are not the same thing. Yes, you do use the same materials to build them, but the purpose is different. Bass traps help to damp room modes, first reflection absorbers help keep the area around your listening position free from early reflections, so you only get direct sound from the speakers.
then if I have extra cash, I treat other corners and the overhead in the same way, in that order
I'd do the overhead first, since that ceiling is a major first reflection point.
- monitors: sound dispersion seems important but is it as important as, say, frequency range? The JBL LSR 2325 are nice from a dispersion point of view, but how about the rest of the important factors?
Don't forget that you got that advice on a home theater forum, where folks will happily pay tens of thousands of dollars for a nice set of speakers, then spend hours, or days, or weeks, sliding them around, an inch or so at a time, to try to get the perfect sound! There's nothing wrong with that, but they are more focused on listening rooms, not studios. To them, the speakers must be beyond perfect to get the perfect listening experience. Also, home theater walls are seldom splayed, and their treatment is somewhat different than what you want in a studio: they don't mind, and they even like it if the speakers have a good "sound" of their own, or if the room "colors" the sound agreeably. Their final goal is a room and speakers that sound great for showing movies. But for a studio, you want neutral sound, not colored sound. You want the speakers to tell you the truth, not enhance it. And you want the room to tell you the truth as well, not disguise it. I have known theater buffs who have visited a professional control room and commented that it sounded terrible, flat, lifeless, surgical, and things like that, when in reality it was a great control room. The trouble is, when you hear good speakers with flat response in a well-designed neutral control room, all you are hearing is exactly what is in the soundtrack itself, and sometimes that isn't "pleasant". For a control room, "neutral, flat, lifeless, and surgical" are exactly what you want! You do not want what "warm", "pleasant", "fat", "melodic", and things like that, that you often hear home studio guru's talking about. Don't get me wrong! I'm not saying that they don't know what they are doing! Not at all: some of those guys are experts, and they were giving you good advice, in general. But they have different priorities than we do. Anyway, that's my long way of saying that your speakers should be the best you can afford, with flat response on-axis, and reasonably tight Q, hopefully better in vertical than horizontal. Then you take those characteristics into account when you locate the speakers in the room, choosing the best position to maintain neutral response, arranging the furniture in the room to minimize early reflections, then treating the room as well as possible to take care of the rest.
- can I have furniture in the room, for example in the back? Wouldn't this help prevent reflections off of the back wall (near the door)?
You can, and many studios do, but once again it is designed, positioned and treated to minimize the effect that it has on the room, or even to have a positive effect. You very often see sofas at the back of control rooms, but they are soft and absorbent. Seldom do you see closets, wooden chairs, large tables, or things like that. In a small room, the rear wall should be as absorbent as you can make it. Yes, rear wall diffusion is an option for LARGE rooms, but not for small rooms. Or at least, most types of diffusion have no place in small rooms: there are some types that you could consider. Anyway, you have the right basic plan with what you said: symmetry, bass taps, reflections, and measure before and after each round of treatment, to see how things are changing. And make certain that you get the mic in the exact same position every time you measure, accurate to with a one cm or so. That is important: If you measure in different places each time, then you are not measuring the same thing! - Stuart -

Soundman202014 February 2012#37
Much better! But I would move your chair back into the room a bit: Your speakers are angled too much. The acoustic axes of the speakers should intersect a few inches behind your head, at about 60 degrees, and no more than 90 degrees, maximum. If you go more than that, then you have no sound stage, and the sweet spot is a tiny, narrow sliver, just a couple of inches wide. so angle your speaker such that the intersect is less than 90 degrees, and then set up your chair in the position where your ears are located a few inches forward of that intersect. I would also put two more absorbent panels on the walls, right behind the speakers (between the speaker and the wall), and something on the rear wall, directly behind you. But apart from that, it looks good! - Stuart -
