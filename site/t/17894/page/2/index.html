There's a whole bunch of "good" ratios (Louden, Bolt, Sepmeyer, etc.), and very much larger bunch of "not so good". The idea with ratios is not to go overboard, trying to finesse things down to the firth decimal place and fraction of a millimeter! Just get away from the bad ones, reasonably close to a good one, and you'll be fine. All that a good ration does is to ensure that the natural modes in your room are spread around the spectrum more or less evenly, not bunched up together, and with no big gaps in between. There is no such thing as a single perfect "golden ratio", despite what some audiophile magazines and web sites want you to believe. There are just good ratios and bad ratios. So plug in the biggest dimensions that you think you can get with your room (after accounting for the isolation walls), see what comes out of those calculators, then play around a few inches either way on each dimension to see what gets you the best ratio.
Is there ways to get some kind of calculator with the side walls angled in at say 12 degrees, guess not?
Unfortunately, not. The problem is that the simple calculators, such as Whealy's and Golds', can only deal with perfect rectangles, since the equations for calculating modes between parallel surfaces are dead easy. But as soon as two surfaces become non-parallel, you can no longer use the simple equations. The reason is easy to understand: a mode is simply a path around the room that a sound wave can take and get back to its starting point, going the same way, in phase with itself. For parallel surfaces, the most basic modes (axial modes) simply bounce back and forth between two parallel surfaces to do that. But if one of those surfaces is angled, then the "bounce" no longer takes the sound wave back along the same path: it now takes a different path, which is no longer related to the distance between the two surfaces, because that distance changes along the surface. So all simple mode calculators can only do rectangular rooms. For non-rectangular rooms, or rooms with more than six sides, you have to resort to FEM/FEA software, which is a lot more complicated and expensive. You need a skilled operator to correctly build the mesh and create the boundary conditions, then analyze the results. Not within the budget of most home studio builders. And not necessary, either! However, that said, in MHoA F. Alton Everest states that, for small variations from rectangular, you can use the average value of the wall separation and come up with a reasonable approximation of the modal behavior. It will not be accurate, of course, but will at least give you a rough idea of how well you are doing. Especially if only one pair of surfaces is angled. So if you do want to splay your side walls, then just take the average distance between them and plug that into the calculators, to see if you are in the ball park. I also like to plug in the largest and smallest separation as well, just to make sure. But once again, you don't need to go to extremes with your ratios and modes: Stay away from the bad guys, get close to one of the good guys, and you are done. - Stuart -

gullfo3 December 2012#43
looking good imho - one additional thought - on the exterior windows - block them from behind to ensure the mass is equal to the walls otherwise you will not have the isolation you expect. both the live room and the control room. you can paint the wall behind the window to mask the fact its sealed. ensure the sealed exterior windows can "breath" to avoid any significant moisture trapping. not looking to make a moral judgement but you might consider the ramifications on your business if you do anything someone in town considers "desecration" as that may give you headaches... starting from obtaining permits and going forward... just a thought that while you may be targeting "metal" clientele you'd probably be best served by using sensibility gauged from other people in town in how you use the building and surrounding space... especially noise ordinances etc where someone can shut you down pretty quickly... just sayin' as it's pretty common for people to do searches on the Internet and find things said online and then they may make hasty judgments based on incomplete information...
