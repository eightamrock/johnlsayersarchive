Soundman202019 October 2012#11
but what about real low standing waves. I tend to record really loud abrasive bands where the bass content is stupidly excessive
Standing waves, also called room modes, are a fact of life: There is nothing you can do to get rid of them. They are directly related to the dimensions of the room, so changing the angles of the walls does not eliminate the modes: it just moves them to a different frequency, and makes it much harder to predict the modal behavior of the room. If the room is rectangular, then it is easy to predict the frequency of each mode. As soon as you change the angles of the walls, making the room non-rectangular, it becomes very complicated to predict the modes.
Square rooms always sound weird don't they?
Not if you choose a good "room ratio" and treat the room correctly. All rooms need treatment anyway, so it simply boils down to choosing a good set of dimensions for your room.
I thought having walls not parallel, running at 6 and 12 degree angles, made for better sounding rooms?
Well, yes and no. As Bert mentioned, non-parallel walls do help deal with flutter, but flutter can be dealt with using simple absorption too. The only time that angled walls are really necessary is if you are aiming for an RFZ design, or something along those lines. The shape of your control room is more suited to a 5.1 setup, but you only show 2.0. If you are planning to go 5.1 at some point in the near future then that shape could be one possibility, although treatment would work well too. But if you never plan to have 5.1, then that shape doesn't make a lot of sense. And like Bert also said: the shapes of your live room and dead room are a bit over the top! Plus, you seem to be wasting a lot of space with that layout. OK, sure, you have a lot of space in there, but it could be put to better use. - Stuart -

Soundman202025 October 2012#18
I guess the answer to the question "To splay, or not to splay?" is that either will work, if done and treated correctly. There are good arguments on both sides. On the one hand, a purely rectangular room with parallel surface makes it easy to predict the modal behavior, and therefore easy to choose a good ratio. On the other hand, splayed walls make it much easier to create an RFZ design, as well as eliminating flutter echo, etc. So it's sort of a toss-up. If you want a true RFZ design with soffited speakers, then splaying at least the front section of the side walls (or the side sections of the front wall, if you prefer that perspective) makes that a hell of a lot easier. There's lots of opinions, both ways. Personally, I think splaying looks good, from the purely esthetic point of view, as well as having the above acoustic benefits, so I'd go with it. But it does make the design process more complex, and does tend to waste space in the angles that you lose. So there's my "non-answer" for the day! :) - Stuart -

Soundman202025 October 2012#22
My last live room in the old studio ... accidentally was a rfz
I'm not sure how a live room can be RFZ! By definition, RFZ implies a fixed sound source and a fixed listening position. How can you accomplish that in a live room? You would have to always place every instrument in the "correct" location for the sound source, and always place the mic in the "correct" RFZ location. Sounds like a very strange way of recording. It also seems totally irrelevant for a vocal booth. Do you have photos of these rooms that you mention? It would be interesting to see how they were set up.
... but can anybody tell me the benefits of a reflection free zone control room
Bert, if I'm not wrong, it was RPG that coined the term and did the original design, as an adaptation of LEDE. They published a paper on it, way back in 1984, it seems, and interestingly Peter D'Antonio's name is no that: https://secure.aes.org/forum/pubs/conve ... elib=11598 Apparently, according to a Google search, they also tried to trademark the term "RFZ", but it appears that they might not have succeeded: http://trademarks.justia.com/736/87/rfz-73687109.html http://trade.mar.cx/US73687109 If I recall correctly, the basic idea is to arrange the front surfaces of the room so that no reflections can reach the ears of the engineer until at least 20ms after the direct sound, and even then 20 dB below the direct sound. The alleged benefit is that the engineer hears only direct sound from the speakers, with little to no Haas effect from reflections. And since the speakers would almost certainly have to be soffit mounted to create a true RFZ, you also get the benefit of no SBIR regarding the front walls, bass response extension, etc. There's probably more in the paper, but I don't have a copy. - Stuart -
