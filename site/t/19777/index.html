Hi there Bukarin, and welcome back! It's been quite a few years since your last thread here on the forum.
I'm hoping to build my own private facility from the ground up.
Excellent! That's the best way of doing it, as you aren't limited by any existing walls, floor, or ceilings: you can do it right from the start!
starting to speak to architects about the whole project. We're exploring using an architect to design the additions as there are some strange angles to contend with and we want to make the whole rear of the house feel consistent.
You'll need the architect for the looks, for sure, but DO NOT let an architect design your studio! That would be like letting a fisherman design your guitar, or letting a luthier design your fishing boat... wrong specialty. Your architect will be the key person to making the studio design fit in with the rest of the house, drawing up the plans, and walking you through the convoluted process of permits, inspections, building codes, materials, costs and perhaps even structural engineering, but he's the wrong guy to be designing the studio. Just like the best qualified plumbing engineer in the world is still the wrong guy to do open heart surgery on your ticker, even if he understands the concept of pipes and fluids and pumps... In other words: get a studio designer to design your studio, and get him working together with your architect to make it look pretty, meet code, and pass inspection, as a team. Right guy, right job.
My plan is to construct a new detached building following the same roof level and line as the garage, it will be located close to the garage (not sure how close yet?)
It can be as close as you want it. The new slab can be poured directly next to the old slab, with just a very small isolation gap between them. Or no gap at all, if extreme isolation is not needed. The two slabs can be in direct contact if you only need moderate to high levels of isolation between the two rooms.
In terms of soundproofing I'm aiming to achieve 70dB STC based on the idea that I want to be able to blast something at 100dB at 2am in the morning and nobody hear a thing.
That would be fine... if STC were actually a useful rating system for determine music isolation! But it isn't. STC was never designed to judge sound isolation across the full spectrum. It only takes into account a small segment of the spectrum. It completely ignores the bottom two and a half octaves of the spectrum, and the top two and a half octaves as well. So it tells you nothing at all about how well a wall, floor, window, door or ceiling will isolate drums, bass, keyboards, electric guitar, or other similar instruments. STC was designed to tell you something about isolating voices, fans, dishwashers, photocopiers, telephone, small radios, and other typical home and office types noises. Not rock bands. I can build you a wall rated at STC-60 that does a lousy job of blocking drums, and I can build you another wall rated at only STC-50 that does an amazing job of blocking drums. STC is no use for you here. So you should start thinking in terms of "transmission loss", which cover the entire spectrum that you are interested in, and you should start thinking in terms of decibels, not STC numbers (they aren't the same thing, and aren't even closely related). Since you are starting with a peak level of only 100 dB, it seems that you are not planning to record drums or bass in your studio, and are only considering quieter instruments, but it would be good if you could explain in more detail what type of instruments you plan to have in there for your 2 AM session.
I don't know if its realistic to try and get 70dB reduction at 40Hz
Ummmm. NO! :shock: Not even the best isolated studio in the world gets 70 dB TL at 40 Hz! That would be Galaxy Studios, in Belgium, and they get a fraction over 100 dB of isolation. It cost them many millions of dollars to get that, and took them years to do it, but I totally doubt they are getting much more than maybe 50 dB TL at 40 Hz. But why do you need such extreme isolation at 40 Hz? What do you expect to be so loud in your studio? Are you recording canon fire? Grenade explosions? Earthquakes? On the other hand, you mentioned peak levels of only 100 dB, so something doesn't jibe here.... :?
I work on a wide variety of music and that definitely includes bass heavy electronic music, I'd love my room to be relatively flat, clean and loud down to 30Hz.
Well, the acoustic response of your control room can be fairly flat and reasonably clean down to 30 Hz, but it can't be "loud". That's a function of the sound system you put inside it, not the room itself. What speakers do you plan to uses are your mains?

I think I need to start the slab about 800mm below ground level,
Here's an idea: Since you need major extreme isolation, and you need to excavate anyway, then why not excavate deeper and put the entire studio below ground? Problem solved. Earth is a good acoustic damper, very massive, so if the entire studio is below ground, then you can attain very high levels of isolation. But there's something that doesn't make sense here. You say: "The studio needs to excavated as to achieve an internal head height of 2.4m ... I've trying to understand how the foundations, wall and slab will be laid out,
That's fairly simple: you'd need to pour a monolithic slab for the floor and inner-leaf walls, then a separate foundation for the outer leaf wall. The gap between them only needs to be small: you'll seal it with some type of resilient compound. They are poured separately. The monolithic slab is done exactly the same as a normal house slab would be done, then the foundation for the outer wall is done the same as for a typical retaining wall. The hardest part is ensuring that they don't touch each other, and that whatever you use as the separator during the second pour can then be fully dug out afterwards, so it too is not causing flanking. Of course, you need to do all this 80cm below grade, and build up the outer-leaf walls from there to grade level, as though it were a basement. So you'll need to follow the usual procedure for waterproofing that, really, really well, to ensure that not a single drop can ever get in. You won't have access to the inner faces of those outer-leaf walls once the place is built, and a sump pump is out of the question, so you need to ensure that those wall are totally watertight.
Will isolating the slab give me a significant improvement in the TL/STC rating?
For TL yes: For STC: Hard to say. STC doesn't measure flanking, and doesn't deal with low frequencies or impact noise on structres, so it isn't an appropriate measure.
in a nutshell I need a highly soundproof concrete bunker with opening external windows
And I need a salary of a million dollars a month, but I don't want to do any work or make any investments to get it! :) ( It's more likely that my wish will come true, than that yours will... :) ) Jokes aside, you really are asking for something close to impossible. It probably can be done, but the cost of such hugely massive operable windows, and the hinges needed to take the extreme loads, and the multiple seals... well, you'd need my dream salary (see above) to be able to afford your dream windows! Even for non-operable windows, that's still a very, very tall order. You can get fixed-pane commercial windows rated at about 45 dB TL for several thousand dollars each. A pair of those back-to-back with a large air gap could feasibly get you in the high 50-something range, maybe even getting close to 60 dB. But you say you need to get ten times more.... :!: Theory says you could probably do that with a couple of two-inch thick laminated glass panes (25+25mm and acoustic PVB interlayer) and a three-foot air gap between them, ... But the cost... And those are for fixed-pane, sealed windows. As soon as you make them operable, you introduce the issue of seals. How do you ensure that the seals can provide continuity for the same surface density as the glass? Answer: you can't! Rubber is much lower density than glass (about half), so even if you had rubber seals that run the complete thickness of the glass, you still don't have enough mass to complete the circuit, and even the tiniest imperfection on the mating faces, or a tiny bit of air trapped in there, has detrimental consequences for isolation. You'd have to mount the glass in operable frames that are at least twice as as thick as the glass where the entire edge area is fully covered in rubber, and fully seals against the jamb, with the same area, also fully covered with rubber. Doable, but complicated engineering, and not cheap.
In terms of budget I've got £35000-£40000
That would be fine for a typical home studio around the size you are talking about, with good levels of isolation. But not for the extreme levels you mentioned. I have just finished designing a similar sized place in Scotland right now, with similar construction, and the owner has estimates for total construction costs in that same region as your budget. His is a bit bigger and a bit more expensive, but the ratio is about right (£ per square meter). However, for his place I am projecting isolation of around 50 dB TL, not the 70 dB TL that you want. That's one hundred times more energy that needs to be stopped, in your case. Much more expensive, and much harder to do. When I started out with his project, initially we did discuss higher levels of isolation, isolated slabs, and a fully subterranean build as options, but he eventually discarded those, due to the much higher cost. He got quotes for doing those, and dropped the idea quickly. Cost was enormous. It is your need for extreme isolation that is going to push your budget up. Way up. Add a zero to your current budget, and you won't be far wrong.... I guess the whole issue here boils down to this: how much isolation do you actually need? You mentioned 70 dB, but that's unrealistic for a home studio. So how much can you live with? What level do you actually have to get down to, legally and morally, at your neighbor's front window? How loud will you really be inside your studio? I'd suggest that you work on coming up with a more realistic number for your isolation, that fits within your budget. If you were to re-think your needs and get your isolation number down to say 55 dB TL for the entire spectrum you mentioned (30 Hz to 20 kHz), then that would be do-able on your budget, using techniques and materials similar to what you have in mind, provided that it is carefully designed (not by an architect!), and carefully built. Id suggest that you get a sound level meter, measure the levels of a typical session that you'd expect to have in your future studio once it is finished (set the meter to "C" weighting, slow response), then also measure the typical ambient level in your yard at the time of day when you'd like to be doing that type of session. The difference between the two is how much total transmission loss you need between the interior of your room, and your neighbor's house wall. Then factor in the natural air attenuation, plus the distance from your studio wall to his house wall, and you'll have the amount of actual isolation that you need for your studio construction. I suspect it will be rather less than 70 dB. Based on that number, and on the spectrum of instruments you plan to have inside making that amount of noise, you can then figure out what type of wall, floor, door, window, and ceiling construction you need would need in order to attain the calculated level, and also what class of HVAC system and electrical system you'd need. Those last two are major key points in studios, and very frequently overlooked. For both of them you need to punch large holes in your wonderfully isolated and sealed walls, then you need to do the necessary things to add back the immense loss in isolation from having those holes there. So those are critical issues in figuring out isolation. When I'm designing a studio, I probably spend about a third to a half of my time on HVAC. It's a huge part of the entire isolation system. There's also the legal factor to consider, so go visit your local municipality and get a copy of the noise regulations that govern your area. That will tell you how loud you can be legally, and how they would measure that if there ever was a complaint. Factor that in too, but do be aware that most noise regulations use "A" weighting, not "C", which favors you greatly for low frequency noise. So re-check your levels with the meter set to "A", to see how you stack up against the law. In summary, overall I'd say that if you reduce your isolation needs to something more moderate, then there's a good chance that you can end up with a great studio, within your budget.
