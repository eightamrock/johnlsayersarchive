It is in the middle of the city, but at nighttime it is real quiet out here. Under the studio will be my wife’s workshop. That won’t bother me. An architect didn’t see a problem to build a studio on this floor... A few holes (for the (removed)oven ventilation and the flour supply) need to be filled with concrete (when it stops freezing over here). The outside walls are brick and are between 20 and 25 centimetres thick. The big window will be replaced quite soon, as well as the door. It’s the tar-like stuff on a roll you burn to the roof.) Distance floor-downside of joists is 2m92. The joists are not in optimal condition, but are cured and should definitely be good for some years. I’m planning on putting on a new roof in about a year or five (when I have new funds...). If the consensus is to put on a new roof NOW, then I suppose this studio project will be delayed by a few years. :cry: The climate over here is quite moderate, so I’m not really concerned about heat or cold. Also, I’m not planning on using the studio for commercial tasks where clients need to be inside for several hours. I suppose I could do with an electrical heater in winter (it’s “really” cold here now, just under freezing temperature) and opening the doors while having a break for fresh air (it rarely gets hotter than 30° C in Belgium). I guess a decent HVAC is definitely overkill. My budget for this project is up to 3000 Euros or slighty above. Doesn’t sound like a lot, I know... After reading around here, I’d say I’m still in the research phase, looking for some knowledgable hints I’d like to start with one room just so I can take off the headphones, get out of my bedroom corner and make some decent noise... I’m still not sure whether I should go for a big control room where I can also jam with a band (is this a good idea at all???) and a small recording booth, just big enough to put in a drum set... or a small CR and a big live room/booth. I’m sure some senior members can say something logical about this. My main activities are still electronic solo projects and the occasional jam. Everything will be based on a computer DAW with various outboard gear and lots of MIDI controllers, some hardware synths and a few guitar amps. My monitors are Event SP8’s. http://www.ciao.com/Event_Project_Studi ... __10045790 In general I was thinking of something between these two designs: viewtopic.php?t=6968 http://www.johnlsayers.com/Studio/Mainpage/MP-Sonar.htm Some first random questions/remarks: Sliding doors do seem expensive and not needed... I also reckon that I won’t be recording drums and vocals that much, and plain simple talk back, maybe a camera will do for CR/booth communication. Can I start by stuffing the space between the roof joists with insulation, and what would be the most effective material for this? Finish this off with some kind of cloth? Putting on a ‘mass leaf’ seems unpractical as I will replace the entire roof in a few years time. Hence, the complete design will need to be ‘room inside a room’. I know, still a lot of reading for me to do (hope to get THE book tomorrow), but hope some knowledgeable guys can help me get on. Thanks for having a look! Cheers, Andy Included are some pics, Sketchup when my main machine gets back from the shop...

Hi Andy, and welcome! Some comments:
I’ve set up my monitors in the naked room, and turning up the volume to what seems to me the max I’d like to get for electronic music is about 97 dB when playing music. I also put some pink noise through the speakers and got to about 90 dB. This was all done using C-wheighting ans ‘slow’ and measured from the assumed mixing position (about 2 metres from monitors). All propositions to measure more precise are welcome!
That's how loud you are INSIDE, but how loud are you OUTSIDE, and how loud do you need to be? If you repeat that test at the same level inside, but then take the meter to a few places outside the room, how loud does it show out there? That will give you an idea of how much isolation you already have. Then repeat the test one more time, measuring on the outside while slowly bringing up the level inside until you get to the level that you want outside (ie, the point where it becomes "too loud" outside). That will tell you how much isolation you need to add. These are very rough ball-park tests, of course. But it will give you a basic idea of what you are trying to achieve. If you could repeat all of the above but with a spectrum analyzer, you0d get an even better idea of where you are and where you want to be. You also need to consider sound that is already OUTSIDE, and that you need to prevent from getting IN! Any loud noise sources around you? Roads, railway lines, airport, industry, disco, etc. How about rain / wind noise? All of that you need to consider too. Sound goes both ways through walls. You need to design the walls to block the loudest sound that you don't to hear, going either way through the wall.
There used to be a metal silo on this floor that could contain up to 8 tons of flour!
That's good news! At least it sounds like you have enough structural support to be able to do some serious isolation.
The roof is made of joists (6x17,5 cm) with timber on top and finished with bitumen roofing (Sorry, couldn’t find appropriate translation, hope you understand. It’s the tar-like stuff on a roll you burn to the roof.) Distance floor-downside of joists is 2m92. The joists are not in optimal condition,
That's not so good news. It does not sound like you have much "beef" up there, and maybe not enough structural integrity to add much mass. That might be an issue.
I suppose I could do with an electrical heater in winter (it’s “really” cold here now, just under freezing temperature)
I'm not so sure that this will be your problem! It might be the other way around... You are going to build a double-sealed air-tight shell around you, with lots of thick insulation all around. I rather think that you might have a problem with too MUCH heat, not too little! especially if you have several people and a bunch of lights and equipment in there. But regardless of heat / cold, you will probably still need ventilation, as in fresh air. Even though you have a fairly large space, you still need to replace the air inside with fresh air, for the same reason as above: you will be sitting inside a double-sealed airtight box. You can't be in there for too long before you start running out of breathable air. So you might need to re-think your HVAC situation. My guess is that you will need some form of cooling, perhaps heating too, so maybe a mini-split that both heats and cools might be an option. But you still need fresh air, which means both inlet and exhaust ducts, with silencers, fans, etc.
I suppose it will be better to not have windows? Definitely cheaper. I also reckon that I won’t be recording drums and vocals that much, and plain simple talk back, maybe a camera will do for CR/booth communication.
If you can live without windows, then it certainly will make construction simpler and cheaper, and make it easier to achieve good isolation. IMHO, on a limited budget, eliminating doors and windows is a good place to save money, if you think you can do without them.

what sound levels are you trying to reach? :) you only told us how loud you plan to be inside, not how quite you need to be outside, or how loud things are outside that you don't want to hear inside... See above...

Once again, without seeing your numbers, it's hard to say, but I suspect that will need to do more than just put insulation up there. If you need good isolation, then you need the mass. You can't sneak past the laws of physics, unfortunately. - Stuart -

Soundman202012 January 2009#5
Fill the space between the joists with MDF board or similar to add mass to the timber, and caulk around the edges. Then fill the gaps further with rockwool or similar. Is this the way to go?
Sheetrock is probably cheaper than MDF, kg for kg, and easier to work with. But the basic idea is right. Two or three layers of 5/8 sheetrock should do the trick. Use cleats to hold it in place firmly, seal with abundant caulk. Many inches thick rock wool or fibreglass underneath that. How big are those joists? Can they safely take a few hundred kilograms of extra weight? Do you have as structural engineer who can give solid advice on that? You don't want your roof coming down on your head from overloading! - Stuart -
