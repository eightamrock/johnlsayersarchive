xSpace6 September 2010#2
"The first and biggest rule of the build is that any walls I put up to split the rooms have to be temporary..." You did not mention how tall the basement is...8 feet? Temporary isolation walls do not exist, in the sense that you can just "take them down". To be the most effective to meet your isolation requirements, that you did not mention so we guess 110dB, you may have to build walls for each room/half that never meet each other and you would pay a lot of attention massing up the overhead/ceiling, then you would have joists installed directly on top of the new framed walls for the two individual rooms and add several layers of sheetrock to the newly installed joists. You can see how this is no longer temporary. If you just built new walls all around the perimeter, added mass to the bottom side of the upper floor and then had a decoupled ceiling installed via clips and hat-track or possibly RC-2, replaced all doors with solid core doors with proper gasketing, built window plugs for any windows, re-worked any HVAC ducts to reduce air-borne passage you could play there but the isolation would be completely different than if you actually broke all the flanking paths between the two rooms and installed a seperate ceiling onto the newly framed walls. Good luck,

Soundman202011 September 2010#11
I wouldn't give up hope entirely! Rather, I would try to set your goals more realistically, and re-think the entire plan. As you'll often here folks say here "If that's what you've got, then that's what you've got. You just have to make the best of it". One of the original problems here was the insistence on using "acoustic treatment" that really won't do you any good at all. Carpet on the floor is a bad idea, acoustically. Temporary walls in studios are not realistically possible. And Drop ceiling are not meant to do what you are proposing. They are meant for offices, and are designed to deal with typical office noises, such as voices speaking and general background office noise. They work well in that environment, but they are next to useless for a studio with a rock band in it! They also do not isolate. That is not their purpose. They treat. There is a huge amount of confusion in the general public about the difference between acoustic "isolation" and acoustic "treatment". In reality, they are two totally different and even mutually exclusive things. Materials that isolate rooms well, are pretty bad at treating it. And materials that treat rooms well are pretty bad at isolating. It is a common misconception that isolating is the same as treating, that if you put things like egg crates and carpets on the walls, or even proper acoustic foam, then that will stop sound from getting through to the next room: it won't. It might make the room sound different, perhaps even better, but it will do nothing at all to stop sound getting in or out of the room. Isolation and treatment are entirely different, totally separate, acoustic issues. That seems to be the basic issue here. The very first post from you guys says that you are stuck with rules imposed by your parents, and those rules include things that just will not work, such as carpet on the floor, a drop ceiling, and the walls have to be temporary. The problem is not the room: The problem is the rules that have been imposed on how to treat it! In essence, the rules totally PREVENT anything useful being done! That's the issue. Those rooms could probably be made to work great as a really nice live room (practice room, studio, tracking room, whatever name you want to give it), and a pretty decent control room (mixing room, engineering room). You have a reasonable amount of space, you have a reasonable basic structure, you have reasonable isolation needs, you have a reasonable budget, and your goals are reasonable. But the restrictions aren't reasonable. Let me put it this way: If your parents said "You can have drums in there, but the shells have to be made only of plastic, and the heads have to be cardboard, and the cymbals can only be made of tire rubber, and the sticks and beaters must be made of old shoe leather", then what would you say to that? Clearly, it is a lost cause: There simply is nothing at all you can do to get a usable drum kit, given those restrictions! It is pointless to even try. The restrictions themselves prevent success from the outset. That is the issue here: You guys have a good space, it could be made great, but not with the limits that are being imposed on you. So here's the plan: I'd suggest that your first approach would be to get the restrictions lifted. Your build can be done in such a way that what you do is "removable", but not in such a way that it is "temporary". In other words, you won't be able to re-use much of the materials afterwards, since you will have to damage them to take them out. But the need to make them removable (not temporary) can be designed in from the start: (screws instead of nails, bolted "modules" instead of nailed framing, no gluing, etc..) Problem solved! It can be done: just re-define your goals, realistically. It seems that the big issue with your parents is not how the room will look while you guys are using it, but how it will look afterwards. Fine: The room can be left looking good afterwards, by painting the walls and laying carpet and installing a drop ceiling, if that's what you want, but those restrictions CANNOT be part of the actual build, since they fly in the face of what needs to be acoustically to make your rooms work! Carpet on the floor? Bad idea, acoustically. Drop ceilings? Bad idea, acoustically. Temporary walls? Bad idea, acoustically. But it can be done after you take out the removable stuff! Your original post says:
When I say finished, I mean 4 walls, a ceiling, and probably some carpeting. That's how my father wants the room to be when I leave, so he can have his basement as a regular old room.
OK, then the deal you make with your dad should be this: When you take out the studio, you will do exactly what he wants: You will leave the room with 4 walls, carpet on the floor, and a drop ceiling. You will set aside enough of your budget upfront to cover those minor costs of re-finish after you dismantle the studio. Problem solved! Re-set your goals. If your folks accept that deal, allowing you to do what NEEDS to be done now, then putting it back their way AFTERWARDS, then you guys can make a go of this thing. But you cannot make a go of it, if those restrictions have to be part of the studio! Lets look again at what you have:
The room is a good size, 30ft by 20ft. ... And yes, the ceiling is 8ft tall.
Great! Nice dimensions!
The walls are concrete blocks and the floor is concrete as well.
Excellent! That makes a GREAT outer leaf for a studio! Hard to get better than that.
The ceiling isn't finished and we have the beams exposed and everything.
Fantastic! You already have access to what you need up there, for both good isolation AND good treatment!
It looks like the typical everyday unfinished basement.
Perfect! Just what the doctor ordered! You don't need to tear down anything before you start. So right now you have the best possible situation. But if you are forced to cover up that GOOD stuff by hanging a drop ceiling, carpeting the floor, and building thin flimsy temporary walls, then basically you destroyed everything good about the room, before you even started. That's the issue. I'd really suggest that you re-negotiate this with your parents, and explain to them the false goals that they are setting, and how those restrictions are going to mean that you won't actually be able to use the room at all. They seem to be quite reasonable parents (after all, they DID give you guys permission to take over their entire basement, and play really loud music in it!!!! :!: :shock: How cool is that??? ) so perhaps if you just talk to them! Even print out this post and show it to them, and just agree that when you guys are done and remove the studio, THEN you will leave the basement they way they want it. That should enable everyone to be happy and get what they want. Just re-define your goals, and re-negotiate the deal, so it is win-win. Worth a try, I reckon! :) - Stuart -
