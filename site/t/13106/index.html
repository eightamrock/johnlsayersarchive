Soundman20201 June 2009#2
Hey there Nate. Welcome! With such a low ceiling, and since you also need some isolation for the room above, I reckon your best bet is to build an "inside-out" inner leaf ceiling, and fill it deeply with mineral wool or fiberglass insulation. So, if it were my room, I think what I'd do is first beef up the underside of that floor above you, using 5/8" drywall (16mm plasterboard) cut to fit exactly between the existing joists, held in place with cleats, and caulked all around the edges carefully. If you have loud instruments (EG, drums) then you'll need at least two layers of drywall up there, and more if you can afford AND I>F THE STRUCTURE CAN TAKE IT! This is an extremely important point, often overlooked: Get a structural engineer to check that floor and make sure it can handle any extra load that you plan to add to it. OK, so now you have your floor beefed up and sealed, but you are still going to have major "leakage" around that ceiling, due to the sound "flanking" through the walls, which are directly coupled to the rest of the building. To deal with that, you need to remove that "inner skin of as yet unpainted MDF approximately 5-7 mm thick", beef up the "outer skin of fibro-cement sheeting" in the same way as you did for the floor above you (a couple of layers of 5/8 drywall fitted between the studs), then build a second timber stud frame, an inch or so away from the existing frame but not touching it anywhere, and put a couple of layers of 5/8 drywall on only ONE side of that frame. Fill the gap between the two leaves with fiberglass or mineral wool insulation. So now you have the basic structure isolated and decoupled: Now you can get to your real problem: the ceiling. Here, you need to build that "inside-out" ceiling that I was talking about. This ceiling will consist of joists across the new inner-leaf frame that you just built, with drywall on the joists. "Inside-out" means that instead of putting the drywall underneath the joists like you normally would, you put it on TOP of the joists. This obviously complicates construction, but it does gain you 9cm of room height for your ceiling treatment: the space between the joists. The hassle is that you have to build the ceiling in sections, on the floor, with the drywall on top of it, then lift each section into place, nail/screw/bolt it in place, and seal it. You need to calculate the height just right do that the drywall ends up just below the existing joists of the floor above, like maybe 4 or 5 cm away, and you have to fill that gap between the floor above and the new ceiling, with mineral wool or fiberglass insulation. So now you have your total room height just a few cm shorter than it was originally, but well isolated and with all that space between the new ceiling joists for your "disappearing ceiling" trick. You can't make it disappear completely of course, but you can minimize it by installing stacks of mineral wool or fiberglass up there, between the joists. Fill the entire gap, and then cover it with acoustic cloth or something like that to hide the "ugly". Here are the numbers: You say you have 220 cm to work with from the bottom of the existing joists. If you leave a 4cm gap below that to the top of the drywall, that leaves 216 cm. 2 layers of 5/8 drywall takes up another 3.2 cm. Call it 4, to allow for imprecision and Murphy, so you now have 212 cm at the bottom of the drywall, which is the final acoustic height of your room. Assuming you used 2x5 joists, you can get a depth of roughly 12cm of insulation across your entire ceiling, and the bottom of the new joists will be at almost exactly 2m above the floor. Which is VERY low, of course (you already knew that! :) ), but still usable, I think. And those 12cm of insulation up there should help considerably to make your ceiling sound higher than it is. You can also build those new inner-leaf side walls "inside-out" if you want to: that will give you extra sideways space in the room. - Stuart -

synthetic27 June 2009#9
Thanks again to everyone who's weighed in with advice on my build, as low tech and low end as it is :roll: Here are a few pics of the latest addition to the room, some over engineered window cover/plugs/ absorbers. They are made with a pine box supported at the corners with metal reinforcers to take the strain of being hung on the wall, with 20cm (apparently approx 8" if I got my conversion right) of rockwool wrapped in black graden fabric to keep in the fibres with a hessian front cover to make it look a bit nicer. The windows themselves are plugged with a layer of plastic, a layer of quilted type material and then a slab of compressed chip board type stuff I had laying around screwed driectly to the window frame, then the frames hung over the top. They have nopticeably reduced outside noise, I used to hear birds etc outside in my recordings - now the extraneous noise from outside is restricted to the halfwit down the road trying to make 100km/h in the 100m's of road from my house to his and the occasional large truck working on the new appartments down the road. I think thius noise will be reduced even further once I have finished the last wall and done the roof according to Johns suggestion - well you can always hope anyway :lol:
