When the term “outer wall” is used around here most of the time we are referring to outer leaf wall, which could also be interior. Have you seen the term triple leaf yet? As I understand above description and your plan that’s where you are headed, please correct me if I am wrong. See link: http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=598 And make sure you read through the reference section. Both leaving an air space (insulation counts as air) in your existing 3 exterior walls and the hollow cinder blocks you’ve added where your garage door was will produce triple leaf which is less sound isolation than double leaf. If you fill those hollow cinder blocks with sand or concrete you’ll be better off.
1. Will a 4" solid cinder block inner room do just as good a job as standard framing and plasterboard construction?
Please see the STC Chart here: http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html Yes I’m guessing you’ll have better sound isolation how ever you will have more weight. Your existing garage slab may or may not be able to take standard framing weight. Weight of concrete is obviously more. You could be building on the edge of a slab not connected to foundation walls. You need to figure out what type of footing/slab/foundation you have before you consider the weight of your walls.

Exactly, in general you would need 5 to 7 layers of drywall on wall to make floating floor worth it, then you would have to build it and not screw up. Also most building code states 7’ or 7’ 6” minimum for room height of habitable space. Pergo (laminate wood flooring) will be fine. Depending on how live you want the room to sound any reflective floor will do fine. Wood seems to be the preference, although it’s arguable. Yup you got your walls existing leaf configuration correct. Adding mass to an outer leaf wall, where you can not add on the outside face is kind of destructive. Many on this forum have done it and taken lots of pics. For example let's take an interior wall, drywall both sides and for some reason you can not add to the outside face. On the inner side of the wall (which next to you'll be building an inner leaf frame 1" or more away from) you would take all the drywall off. Let's say the studs are 24" OC. You would cut new peices of drywall 23 1/2" wide and the hieght of stud cavity minus 1/2". Place them directly against existing outer layer of drywall, you will have 1/4" space all around to insert 1/4" backer rod and then caulk. Then use 2x? or 1x? cleats up against corner of existing 2x4 studs and new drywall to to hold these new "beef up" layers in place. This method has been done on ceilings and on all sorts of exterior walls. Look at Shawards thread (a mighty long read), Dan F or Andy Eade's for pics of this actually being done and more description. Sharward is using a lot of "beef up" drywall, like 4 to 5 layers and there is hardly any room left in the cavities of his existing (pre studio room) walls. Marc

Alfadog, Well, yes that would be a better option presuming your existing wall surfaces are plumb on the inner side and you can somehow insure solid contact with no air space between existing walls and new blocks. BUT how much better is the question AND will it be worth $2,600 compared to other methods you could or could have use. One of the suggestions on this forum and in Rod’s book is, do not use unproven construction methods. The double wall MASS AIR MASS requires the 2 masses to be decoupled. Since you are talking about adding mass your existing inside leaf which is coupled to the outer leaf via concrete foundation wall below and 2x4 studs & plates, you would still have a lot of flanking (sound transfer between coupled mass). I really don’t know how much better off you would be, I could do some research and make a wild ass guess, others on this forum could make a quicker wild ass guess. BUT I would not want to spend $2,600 on a not suggested method when I was aware of other methods, similar or less in cost, however destructive they may seem that I knew would achieve better results that have been proven, of which I can see test data and be confident of expected results. You may even be better off as is when comparing the cost to actual isolation gained. Note I’m not an expert at all this, but I have studied a lot for known construction methods of my own room. (I say “known” because I’m currently dealing with concrete slab/footer which I did not expect). In order to make that 4” block wall worth it you will also have to match the isolation on the ceiling. You still got to figure out if your garage slab can take that weight on its edges. My guess is “don’t know” and I would not trust a guess with that much weight. With a 2x4 wall and sheetrock you “should be” alright with weight. If you do decide to tear down existing material from inside garage wall you may be able to recycle it. A few guys on here have taken existing drywall down, cut it out carefully using studs as a guide and reused the drywall in between studs as beef up on outer leaf. I don’t know about the celtox. I presume you are the person who installed all this, even though it would be difficult (I would not want to do this myself) think you can take it down the all the drywall and celtox with out destroying it? I realize you’ve put a lot of work and money into celtox and 2 layers of ½” drywall on the upper part of those walls. Maybe all you want to do now is properly take care of the ceiling to match the current isolation of your walls. If I were you before I spend $2,600 on 4” brick inner leaf I would: Buy Rods Book for less then $30, read it 2 or 3 times (don’t know anyone who has got everything on the 1st read) While waiting for the book, go through this web site and others, spend hours reading & studying. :?
