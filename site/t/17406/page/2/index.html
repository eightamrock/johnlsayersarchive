Soundman202028 March 2012#29
the acoustics of the room should not be optimal regardless of the monitors and the position where I put them?
The acoustics will be different in different locations in the room, and the response of the room as a whole can change, depending on the position of the speakers. For example, if you put a speaker in one of the corners of the room, then it will easily excite many (perhaps all) of the room modes, but if you put the speaker in a position that is a null for one or more nodes, then things will be very different. The same happens with your listening position: as you move around the room, you will hear different things in different locations. If your head happens to be in the null for a certain frequency, then you will not hear that note or any of the harmonics of that note, or you will hear them at a much lower level. Or if your head happens to be at the location of a peak for a certain frequency, then you'll hear that frequency louder than it should be (and also all of its harmonics). There are also other issues: if you put a speaker right against a wall, then you will get a boost in the bass levels, and you will also get comb filtering and other effects from the reflections coming off the wall, and the way they interact with the direct sound from the speaker. As you move the speaker away form the wall, you can easily hear the change in comb filtering moving down to lower and lower frequencies. Etc. That's why it is critical to set up your room correctly, and adjust the location of your listening position (your chair) and the locations of all of the speakers, for optimum results. There are general guidelines for setting up your room, based on percentages, angles, heights and distances: The normal way of optimizing is to set up the room like that, then make small changes to the positions of the chair and speakers, and see if you can get an improvement. So it is also imperative to take the acoustic readings at the exact position where your head will be, and to carefully measure the location of the microphone that you use for the readings, so that you can get it back to the exact same position (accurate to within a half inch or so), for future readings, as you change the room treatment, or the position of the speakers. If you don't get the mic in the exact same place each time, then the readings are not valid and cannot be compared, since you are looking at a different part of the overall room response pattern. So yes, a room can have great acoustics, but you'll could still hear different things in different locations around the room, since the sound waves are traveling around, interacting with the surfaces they encounter, and with each other. It's complicated.... :) - Stuart -

Soundman202028 March 2012#33
regarding the settings in the rear of the monitors, I have not used any because I believed that the acoustics of the room should be good without corrections from the monitors...
The controls are there for a purpose! They have nothing to with the acoustics of the room: rather, they correct the interaction of the speaker with the room boundaries. It fixes the problems that the room creates for the speaker itself. If you place a speaker in the middle of a large room, far away from all walls, then it needs no correction. If you place the speaker up against a wall, then you have changed the environment of the speaker. It is no longer radiating freely in all directions (normally called "full space"). Because it is now against a wall, the sound that normally would have gone backwards behind the speaker and never returned, is now bouncing right back immediately, and increasing the apparent output by up to 6 dB. In other words, the speaker is now radiating into "half-space", instead of "full space". So you have distorted the power response curve of the speaker, simply by placing it close to a wall. The problem is that this effect is not the same for all frequencies. It only affects very low frequencies, and is different for every speaker. This has nothing at all to do with the room acoustics: it is simply a matter of the way sound waves interact with walls. It is a law of physics. The situation would be exactly the same for your speaker, even in the best studio in the world. That's why the manufacturer put the controls there: because you need them! Two of the controls on the back of the HS80M are pertinent here: First and foremost is the one marked "Room Control": Set that one to -4dB and run your tests again. There should be a big improvement. But if that isn't enough, you might also need to set the "Low Cut" control to 80 Hz, and try again. Post the results from those tests, so we can take a look. One word of warning here: The HS80M is a rear-ported speaker, so you can't put it right up against the wall. You need to leave at least a small gap at the back (a few inches), or you could change the tuning of the rear port. - Stuart -
