Soundman202018 June 2013#2
Hi Robbie. Please read the forum rules for posting (click here). You seem to be missing a couple of things! :) It doesn't matter "why" you do what you are planning, it will still act the same as any other light-weight floor over a cavity: as a drum head. Like it or not, that's what you will be creating. Any time you put a membrane over a cavity, it becomes a resonant system, regardless of your intentions or the reasons behind it. It will resonate at a frequency given by the surface density of the floor, and the depth of the cavity. In order for that frequency to be low enough to not cause issues with your room, you need a large amount of mass, and a deep cavity. If the floor is light weight (just a couple of layers of plywood, for example, or hardwood planks, or suchlike) and the depth is small (just a few centimeters) then the resonance will be up where you don't want it. That will do several things to your room, such as reducing isolation around the resonant frequency, and also acting as an un-damped panel trap, which will absorb that frequency (and its harmonics) out of your room. If your issue is just "cold feet" ( :) ), then the best solution would probably be some good thick laminate flooring on top of a good thick thermally isolating underlay pad.
Remembering that I'm not particularly in need of further sound isolation.
The way you describe it, you would not get an increase in isolation, but rather a reduction in isolation.
wasn't quite sure what to lay to stop standing waves under the floor & the floor itself resonating? Rockwool?
You can't stop standing waves: all you can do is damp them. Since they are modal in nature, and are due to the dimensions of the cavity, it is impossible to get rid of them. The only thing you can do is to damp them as much as possible. But that still won't solve the problems created by a light-weight floating floor. - Stuart -

and also a 2mm underlay/vapour barrier as 'Thick'?
2mm underlay doesn't sound like enough, especially if your floor is very cold. Normal underlay is at least 1/8" (3mm) which is 50% thicker than the stuff you mention. I have also seen 5mm, but you should check with the manufacturer or your laminate to make sure you can use that. Some underlay has a moisture barrier built in, and some also has a "metalized" surface, to help with thermal insulation. I have never tried it myself, but some people recommend using a thick layer of cork glued to the subfloor. But whatever you choose to do, call the manufacturer of the actual laminate to make sure it is OK. You don't want an underlay that is either too hard or too soft, as both can lead to cracking and other issues as you walk on it. --
could someone please point me toward finding out how to mathematically calculate what the floor's resonant frequency would actually be? I'm searching madly....
Assuming that the solid outer floor is much more massive than the light-weight inner floor, and also well damped (concrete slab on grade fits that bill), then the simplified version of the equation can be used: Fmsm = 60 / ( SQRT (m * d)), where "m" is the surface density in kg/m2, and d is the depth of the cavity, in meters. If not, then you need the full version of the equation: Fmsm = 60*( SQRT ((m1 + m2) / (m1 * m2 * d ))) Both of those assume standard atmospheric conditions.
