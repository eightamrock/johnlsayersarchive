knightfly13 March 2006#2
Eric, this partially depends on the gauge of the steel and on the spacing of the studs. The "flex" factor of steel goes away if you have to use load-bearing, or "structural", gauge studs (like 20 gauge) - also, as soon as you go to double frames it's a moot point; this, because the main advantage of light gauge steel studs in a SINGLE framed wall is that they FLEX more than wood ones, thereby decoupling one wall panel from the opposite one. I've learned in the last couple of years that stiffness in a wall is NOT a good thing - ideally, you could have infinite mass separated by lots of air (or better, vacuum :wink: ) from a second infinite mass, and have both masses just floating - this would be the best you could get. Not being able to do that, the next best thing is a frame that allows the wallboard to be as floppy (floating) as possible; this is why 24" centers gives better isolation than 16" centers. It's also, in a way, why wider air gaps between masses is better (softer coupling "spring") . Yet another deciding factor - water retention. On the Building Sciences site (see the REFERENCE section) there is one paper that tells of a test house (around 2000 sq. ft. IIRC) built from steel framing and gypsum, the entire house could support a moisture content of around 5 gallons without causing damage; second house, wood frame, wood panels - 50 gallons. Third house, all masonry - 500 gallons!!! For this reason, I'm a bit nervous recommending steel framing unless the individual plans on a dehumidifier running in the space 24/7 except when actually USING the space. (with the humidistat set for 40% humidity, that is) HTH... Steve

knightfly14 March 2006#4
Stringers, no; unless you're over 10 feet high, then code requires them for fire stops. RC - if you're using double frames (paneling only on one side of each frame) then no RC is needed, and it can even make things worse by adding another resonant frequency to the mix (therefore another frequency that gets thru the wall easier) - in fact, even using wide caps and plates and staggered studs the RC does little if anything; it's only needed where BOTH masses in a mass-air-mass wall would otherwise be rigidly connected to each other through the studs. Here's the Building Sciences paper http://www.buildingscience.com/resource ... ldings.pdf I find these are much faster if you right-click on the link and "save target as". It's not too huge, 6 pages in all... Steve
