rod gervais15 February 2007#28
rbacker, the room design itself looks alright to me - but this frame of yours- is a disaster waiting to happen. Your ceiling should be bearing directly on top of your wall assemblies - but instead it frames inside of them - which means that for years to come - the only thing holding it up in the air is the shear of the nails tying the ledger board to the wall frame. AT the very least yu need to add joist hangers to the foot of the ceiling jist. In addition to that - the balloon frame requires fire stops at the ceiling level- and now you still have to figure out a way to create a fire stop at that level between the innner and outer walls. These are some very srange framing details you used here - not anything that I have even seen in over 30 years of construction - you could have saved yourself some time and effort (and material) if you had only investigated the basic of standard framing in any of a hundred books on the subject. good luck........ Rod

The CR frame design is a light-weight framing to support a layer of 703, plastic, cloth and a couple of light tracklights. most of the load is the lumber itself but it does need to be framed correctly. this shapes the ceiling space, above which is a number of hangers on the main ceiling. Best bet is talking to the building officer to review the plan so you can determine if there are other requirements you need to address and also to apply for the permit. If the construction is to code, then an inspection shouldn't be too much of a problem although having closed up some walls will likely be a sticking point. the downside of not getting a permit (without rehashing Sharwards' excellent stickie) is a strong possibility of things getting stopped and exposure to [more] liability if something bad happens due to faulty construction.
sharward16 February 2007#42
Hey rbaker, First of all, no apology necessary. I didn't interpret that as a "rant." You're just expressing yourself and doing a nice job of it actually. :) Next, my condolences to you and your girlfriend -- witnessing a murder? I have to believe that has to leave a mark on one's soul. :( My advice to you is the same advice I give to anyone in a similar situation -- I think your best bet is stopping what you're doing and then presenting yourself and your plan to the building department, hat in hand, expressing remorse for not going through the proper channels earlier, and expressing a commitment to doing whatever it takes to get on the right track. While I cannot guarantee success with this approach, I think it offers the best chance of a positive outcome. I have to believe the building department folks deal with this kind of thing all the time: people jump the gun out of fear or ignorance or both. You'll be giving them an opportunity to help you do things right. I would think that's a lot more satisfying for them than catching people when they're noncompliant. There is, of course, a risk that you'll have to tear down some of what's been done already... And I suppose it's possible that the requirements you'd have to meet in order to "be legit" would be cost-prohibitive. I can only imagine the anguish you'd experience if that becomes the case, and I am sincerely hoping for you it doesn't. But think of the risks of proceeding in spite of not having pulled permits. What if something goes wrong? Maybe not right away, maybe later... A fire... A collapse... Loss of property, or worse, injuries or loss of life. What started out as an error in judgment could become a criminal matter. Perhaps the most infamous example is The Station in Warwick, Rhode Island -- the Great White show with pyrotechnics combined with the inappropriate use of flammable packing foam as acoustic material, emergency exit doors chained shut, and other emergency exit doors illegally installed so as to open inward. 100 people died in that catastrophy and the venue's owners faced a criminal trial for their part in the tragedy. I say, come clean, and let the chips fall where they may, and be emotionally prepared for some expensive retrofits to get into compliance. By the way, I would not volunteer that you had a carpenter/musician you know do the wood framing, and I would verify that the licensed contractor you hired to do your steel framing has workers' compensation insurance for any employees who worked on the site. California law is very strict in terms of hiring contractors. Don't quote me, but I think you are prohibited from paying someone more than $300 per job unless they are a licensed contractor, and if the contractor has employees, they must be covered under the contractor's workers' compensation insurance policy, else the liability rests with you personally if they are hurt on the job, not to mention it being against labor code for you to do so. When applying for a permit, if you are acting as your own general contractor, you can only do work yourself or hire licensed contractors. In other words, come clean about your current situation, but be skimpy on the details on how you got there. I'm sure you've got some heavy thinking to do, and I wish you the best. --Keith :mrgreen:
