Soundman20205 March 2013#35
The research I've been doing has been leading me to just doing fluffy pink insulation in those walls, and maintain a larger air space,
Exactly. You already built the framing so it is too late to increase the air space, but it looks like you have a decent amount of air space there anyway, so just fill all of that space with "pink fluffy" that weighs about 30 kg/m3 (about 2 pcf). But do check your fire code: you might not be allowed to completely fill the air space with insulation. If not, then fill it as much as you are allowed to, but at the very least fill the stud bays completely.
and maybe hang a couple of baffles in the voids from the inner ceiling joists inside of the void?
What do you mean by "baffles"? There is no reason to hang anything in the air space except insulation.
With regards to the control room... (MSM design) If I angle my walls to help achieve a more "RFZ" like environment,
It seem to me that you already framed your control room, or at least that's what I think I'm seeing in the photos. Or not? If so, then it is too late to be changing the angles of the walls, unless you plan to take down that framing and re-build it a the correct angle.
would this mean that I won't need bass traps in the front corners of my room,
You will always need bass trapping in any small room. The smaller it is, the more bass trapping it needs. Cutting off corners does not eliminate the room modes: it just makes the harder to calculate and harder to treat. Room odes are a consequence of the walls, not the corners. Yes, the all terminate in corners, but if you cut off the corner that does not make the mode go away! It just moves the termination point to new locations. It's the same as trying to cut the end off a piece of string, so that it no longer has an end... :) All you succeed in doing, is moving the end to a different place.
since there will be no corners for sound to build up in?
That's actually just a way of describing what happens in terms that are easy to understand. In fact, bass does not "build up" in corners. rather, it builds up against walls, but since a corner is where two walls meet, that's where it sounds louder, since you get the effect of both walls at once. And if you listen in a "tri corner" (where two walls also meet the ceiling), then the effect is really loud, because you are hearing the bass from all three surfaces at once! Chopping off a corner does not change anything: the walls are still there, still doing the same thing they did before, and all you have succeeded in doing is to change the pattern. Actually, you did one more thing by chopping off the corner: you also chopped off the best possible place for treating bass! Since corners are the place where the bass is loudest, they are also the best place for putting bass traps, since once single trap can deal with the bass on all three surfaces! But if you chopped off the corner, you lose that advantage, and now you need even MORE bass trapping, since you have removed the most efficient point for trapping bass... :)
From what I have gathered, placing a superchunk or something similar behind the drywall within the void of the two walls would accomplish little to nothing.
If you place a superchunk "behind" drywall, the it is no use at all! :shock: The superchunk just goes in the corner, with nothing in front of it, except for some fabric to hide it. Putting drywall in front of it would prevent the sound from getting to it, so it would be pretty useless...
I'd basically need to absorb my entire rear wall, superchunk the rear corners,
Corret. For a modern RFZ design in a small space, yes, that would be the way to go.
and add diffusion to taste, if necessary?
Probably not. Your room does not seem large enough to be able to use diffusion. And adding to "taste" is appropriate for a live room, yes, but not in the control room. Control rooms should be neutral, not coloring the sound in any way at all, so diffusion should be added or removed "as needed" to get that neutrality.
I guess I'm trying to figure out if I should splay the front and side walls in attempt to achieve RFZ,
Once again, from looking at the photos, it seems that your walls are already built. So yes, achieving true RFZ is highly desirable, and a great idea, but in order to do that now, you'd have to take down parts of the framing and put it back up in the correct place. Yes, you sure would get a better room like that, if you are prepared to change the framing. Or maybe I'm not understanding your photos, and you haven't actually framed the control room at all yet?
I do not plan on soffit mounting my monitors at this time, because I believe my focal solo 6's do better free standing,
ALL speakers can benefit from soffit-mounting, since it eliminates the artifacts that make them sound bad! It eliminates SBIR, comb-filtering, phasing, early reflections, power imbalance (baffle step response), and all other artifacts that the front wall of the room causes. By soffit mounting, the speaker is no longer "inside" the room, acoustically, so therefore it cannot be affected by the room. Soffit mounting allows the speaker to perform at its best, without the sound being tainted by the artifacts associated with the front wall. The only speakers that cannot be soffit mounted are those that are are-ported or side ported. Yours are front ported, so they can be soffit mounted, and in fact they are designed for soffit mounting, since they have the necessary controls on the back to allow for that.
In the live room where I'm employing quietclips, do I need to have insulation on both leafs with air in between, or is one layer sufficient... if so, would I want it on the drywall (interior) wall side, with the air gap facing the block (exterior) wall?
The purpose of the insulation inside the wall cavity is to damp the resonance that occurs in there, thus improving isolation. Some people think that the insulation stops the sound from going through, but that is simply not correct: insulation cannot and does not stop sound. Rather, it damps resonance. There are several different types of resonance that occur inside a 2-leaf wall, and the insulation is there to damp them all. The difference in isolation between a wall that has no insulation and one where the entire air gap is filled with insulation, can be as much as 16 decibels. That is huge! Since an ordinary house wall gives you maybe 30 dB and a really great studio wall might give you 60 dB, you can see that as much as 50% of the total amount of isolation provided by the wall, is due to damping the resonance inside it. That's very interesting.... So, to answer your question: put as much insulation in there as permitted by your local building code, and as much as you can afford.
Hope the photos have been helpful, and that we haven't broken any rules yet!
It's looking good so far, and the photos sure help! But it would be good to see your final design, so we can check that before you get too far advanced with the build: there might be issues with the parts of that, so please post your final SketchUp model, and we can take a look at it for you. - Stuart -

Yes, it should be re-thought. 12° is usually not enough to create a true RFZ. RFZ can only be attained by actually ray-tracing outwards from the acoustic centers of the speakers, in three dimensions, and confirming that you really have created a reflection free zone, or adjust the wall and ceiling angles if you didn't get there yet. so it shouldn't be a problem to soffit mount in the future as long a I make electrical and cable provisions now?
Not just electrical and cabling, but also acoustics: If you install soffits later, the acoustics of the whole room will change, drastically, and you will need to change all/most of your existing room treatment. The entire basis of your room acoustics, and especially of the speaker / listening position geometry (which is absolutely critical in any control room) depends 100% on the front end of the room. Change anything in the front end, and you change the entire room. The sides and rear of the room are basically just "complements" to the front, and just "help" the sound on its way, bu the front end is what defines the room. So that has to be done right NOW, or you will waste a lot of time and effort later, when you have to pretty much tear down most of your treatment, and start all over again. So at least build the soffits now, even if you don't put any speakers in the yet. Trying to add them later will be a huge pain, and a major expense. Doing it now is a piece of cake.
Can I obtain an RFZ without soffit mounted monitors?
Yes, but it is much harder to do. And since the entire purpose of soffit mounting is to take the speakers "out" of the room (acoustically speaking) so that they cannot interact with the front wall, you will not be able to get the same benefits and the same control room acoustics without soffits. Basically, soffit-mounting eliminates pretty much ALL of the artifacts associated with having speakers close to walls: No more SBIR, comb-filtering, phase shifts, other phase related issues, reflections, or baffle step power imbalance. All of those just cannot happen with soffit-mounted speakers, and most certainly will happen without them. It's just the nature of the laws of physics, and the way sound interacts with surfaces. So my advice would be to design and build the soffits now anyway, just with blank front panels, then mount your new speakers in them a few months down the line.

Please don't take this the wrong way! But unless you design your studio completely in advance, taking into account all of the details before you even lift a hammer, the result will not be good. If there's one thing that I have learned about studio design over the years, it is that "everything affects everything". Making one apparently minor change to something can have a snowball effect on things tat you never would have expected, and you need to take everything into account before you even start. For example, one thing I don't see any sign of at all in your build so far, is HVAC. This is a critical, and I do mean CRITICAL aspect of studio building, and is very different from normal HVAC installation for a house or office. The ducts have to be over-sized to reduce the airflow to much lower speeds, you need silencer boxes at every point where a duct penetrates a leaf (ans silencers take up a lot of space), and you need long straight runs into low-noise registers (grills), among other things. The calculations for the HVAC system (flow rate, flow speed, cooling capacity, humidification capacity, latent heat, sensible heat, occupancy, change rate, fresh air intake, stale air exhaust, equipment, etc., etc. are all extremely important. Your studio is basically a triple-insulated double-hermetically sealed isolation chamber. Without proper HVAC, not only will it become unbearably hot and stuffy inside, in just a short period of time, it will also be a health hazard: without oxygen, you cannot stay alive for very long, and since each of your rooms has two complete, very effective air-tight seals around it, there is no way that oxygen is getting in there by itself (like it does in normal house rooms): you have to make it happen. If not, you die. It really is that critical, but I don't see any signs of that on your plans, and it is a bit late to be thinking about that now! In fact, it is way too late, just like it is way too late to be thinking about control room ratios, wall angles, RFZ, soffits, ceiling angles, room volume, and acoustic prediction. All of those should have been carefully thought out, planned on paper, and calculated in every detail, long ago. Yes, I understand you are in a hurry to get this done, and are working many hard hours every day, but with no design behind it you are working much harder than you need, and will have to spend a lot more time (and money) fixing things along the way that would never have been wrong in the first place if they had been properly planned. I would really urge you to stop building for a while, take a step back, and complete the FULL design, properly and in complete detail, before you do anything more. If you can't do the design yourself, or don't want to, then hire someone to do it for you. The money you spend on design fees will be saved, many times over, from NOT making mistakes that need correcting. You are at the stage where you can still do that and fix things that become apparent. If you advance much more with the build, it becomes harder and harder to go back and fix things, and less and less likely that the place will be great. Anyway, please don't take that as insulting or a put-down, but more as constructive criticism to help you! I want to see your place turn out well, just as much as you do! - Stuart -
