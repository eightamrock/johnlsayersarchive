Walls - I’d like some advise on which of these wall constructions is the better way to go, where I have a brick wall to attach to. I do wonder whether that is overkill. On that note – I have been looking at acoustic door sets and they are around £1000 +vat per set (44db) and I’m going to need 6!!! … surely there is a cheaper way to do this? Well I think that’s all for now folks – I hope this isn’t too much of a howler. Any advise would be VERY welcome. Thanks again all – keep up the good work. Am loving this site.

Musicianista12 December 2006#2
Regarding the floor design... Don't couple the internal stud frame to the external brick with neoprene or anything else. You need a decent airgap instead. You don't want to couple the walls to the existing floor. The most common approach I have seen to wall building, is to build the floating raft for the floor first on a series of rubber pads and then the wall studs on top of that. Therefore, if there is more than one room(s), each is isolated from the existing structure and from each other. These concepts are focused on vibration isolating & soundproofing a new room. The effect of these procedures on the acoustics is that your new, suspended, airtight space has nowhere for the trapped audio energy to go and bass trapping, side diffusers, ceiling treatment etc is inevitable. Plan for this now and allocate space in your space to iron out the freaky freqs that will come to your party. :D

sharward13 December 2006#4
Musicianista wrote:
You don't want to couple the walls to the existing floor. The most common approach I have seen to wall building, is to build the floating raft for the floor first on a series of rubber pads and then the wall studs on top of that.
Yes, that's a common approach. I also question (and I believe rightfully so) the wisdom of that approach. How carefully was such a floating room engineered? What kind of rubber pads were used? What was their durometer specs? How much were they loaded? Were they underloaded or overloaded or "Goldilocks" just right, with 10-15% compression that will yield long life and ensure its "springiness" that provides for true isolation? Is the subfloor slab-on-grade? Did the city/county building department approve of the use of the product? Is it in a seismic or hurricane zone? (If so, pretty much forget it!) Was the floating room built on an upper floor, where isolation from the downstairs occupant was critical? If so, was a structural engineer consulted to ensure that the thousands of pounds of building materials would not cause a catastrophic collapse? And if not, why float at all? Is there concern about flanking through the floor to adjacent spaces? Are the walls and ceiling built beefy enough to exceed the flanking limitations of the room? And were the results really better than not floating? How does one know? Were two identical spaces built, one floating and one not, and each one tested to compare the results? Is it possible that the floated room actually performed worse than if the room hadn't baeen floated? (This is quite possible! Floated floors can be boomy!) Properly engineering a floating room is difficult and expensive... I venture to guess most people get it wrong. Rod discusses this to some extent in his book. Rod is a pro who knows how to properly float a floor/room... And he thinks they're overrated in most cases. Study more of the many threads here about floated floors/rooms. I've discussed it at length many times, including my own experience trying to design and engineer a floating floor that my city rejected (and rightfully so). Don't just blindly assume you have to float a floor/room.
