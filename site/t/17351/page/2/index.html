xSpace19 February 2012#29
"RC to decouple the walls that already are framed and framing the new walls decoupled." What does that mean? You have three paths to choose for your framed walls, Either you mount them hard to the existing framing, and install RC on the wall framing which will decouple the framing. OR you install sway braces to the interior framing, there by decoupling the framing and having it stay vertical. You do not add RC to this wall assembly since it is now decoupled via the sway braces. And lastly, if you install joists overhead, you would make certain the wall framing is decoupled from the exterior, install the joists (temporary wall support to keep them vertical is required) then you install OSB sheathing, vertical onto the interior framed studs to add shear ability. No sway braces are required, no RC is required on the wall assemblies, and you can now install the interior sheetrock directly to the OSB.

There's more framing to be done. The new framing should be done properly " Ok...let's have a little talk about mass-spring mass. This is the properties of a wall that will get you high isolation...I assume you have read nothing in this respect or have yet to get your head around the idea, so we have to pursue this idea until we both can say..."he get's it ". You have a basement, lets act for a few minutes that it is empty, not a thing in there...but the basement walls. Now if we were trying to build a high isolation wall assembly using mass/air/mass principles which is the same thing as a fully decoupled wall assembly AND a two leaf wall assembly, we consider this basement wall, the exterior perimeter as one leaf. It is the one leaf in a two leaf system. Like it is the "mass"...but we have not added the air/mass yet...ok? Now we construct a framed wall assembly inside of this basement, a wooden framed wall that does not touch ANYTHING but the heavy Earth damped concrete slab. And then we instal insulation into the framed assembly and install, for brevity, one layer of 5.8 inch sheetrock on to the interior side of this newly framed wall. We assume that the ceiling was done to these same specs, and once that is complete, this is your mass/spring/mass, your fully decoupled room, your room in a room or your two leaf wall assembly. Hell man, if you want to spend the money like it's no tomorrow...do it...but there are better ways...that is all I am trying to stress here. You and people that come behind you have got to get a handle on these simple basic aspects or you are going to not only waste money, but who ever reads this, and does not do the due diligence, is going to do the same thing as well.

"My question is whether cross bracing is necessary for the new joists (there will be cross bracing for the existing joists)? Would sway braces be an alternative ($7 each adds up with all these joists). Would OSB sheathing be an alternative (if so, this would allow for green glue application). Mike, You may not save a penny from either the purchase of sway braces versus adding the OSB to the framing to help with the lateral load (I said shearing up-topic, but I mis-spoke). But the pattern for installing the sway braces is not every 16 inches...so your numbers are way to high. The difference is that, if you were not including a framed ceiling then the walls would not stay in a straight line and you would have to use sway braces. Would also apply if you were using RC on the ceiling, since there is no way to keep the wall framing vertical and in a straight line when the ceiling is decoupled from the wall frames. To use OSB/plywood the requirement is that you have to include a wooden framed ceiling. Since it reads like you are using a wooden framed ceiling, then this is the better option, if not for the only reason that no one has to learn or experiment with an unknown (to them) attachment and how to correctly install it. Explain or ask your carpenters this: Why do you install 4' X8' sheets of OSB to the corners of the exterior perimeter of a structure? There are two correct answers. One is that it aids in the shearing ability of the wall due to the elements, the lateral load as well. Two is that it is cheaper than plywood, but you can't give the second answer with out hinting at the first answer. The reason you have bridging in a wood framed floor system, whether it is diagonal or blocking, is two fold. The first is that this connection between joists helps in the prevention of "roll over" of the joists. Now I cannot say with any authority that is only done to keep the framing crew safe when framing, or if it is too prevent roll over which would be a product of exterior forces on the structure that overcomes the ability of the structure to remain vertical...like a tornado, well, may not a tornado they do tend to make things explode, maybe more like a hurricane, a sustained high wind on the main body of a structure. The second is, and this is more important to me not with standing the safety of the framer which I have been and could be again, is to make the entire floor joist system work as one unit. Rather than have 15 individual joists taking the live load singular you develop a "unit" that is stronger than the individual. But to the ceiling of a room in a room, I wouldn't have any issue with not including the blocking/bridging...but everything would matter. A typical procedure in a ceiling is to include a strong back. If you have access to the attic of your home and climb up there you will see something like the following pic:
Image not preserved: strong-back.jpg
This does essentially the same thing but it takes more over all head room to achieve, since the main component in the strong back, depending on the joist member sizing and span, is at least 5 and 1/2 of an inch. So if the span of your joists required this type of mating together of the members, like if it is 12 feet or more, then this method of strong backing would not suit your build since it will reduce your height, and bridging or blocking is the natural alternative. Also, cross bracing, while difficult, will yield the highest possible ceiling height available due to the criss/crossing voids of the bridging that is seen in Rods book.
