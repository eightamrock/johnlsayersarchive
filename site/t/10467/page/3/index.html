arthur noxon24 April 2008#74
EAR PLUGS The drum kit has a bandwidth of probably 30 to 16k Hz, but I haven’t measured one. However, the goal is to not damage our ears. What we want is to protect is our hearing. Google and read much good information: “Drummer Hearing Loss” One drummer measured 125 dB,A exposure level with snare, rim shots and cymbal. We want to figure out what part of the frequency range produced by the drum kit is damaging our ears. Then we want to turn the volume down in that frequency range. Use a mic and some spectrum analyzer to figure out what the loudest frequency range is for the instrument you play. At loud sound levels, the ear is equally sensitive at all frequencies. Probably anything over 100 dB needs to be cut down. Look at the dB attenuation response of the ear plugs you use. And remember, just because you have ear pugs, doesn’t mean you have them installed right. Good and simple plugs are the EAR expandable memory foam material. Use once and throw away. You compress roll the foam cylinder into a pencil lead thin roll, slide the compressed cylinder into your ear quickly before it expands, let it slowly expand to fit snugly into your ear. It protects the top end, the most vulnerable range of your ear. The ear is generally gets wacked in the 2 to 6k region. That’s the typical hearing loss “notch”. Ear plugs that protect that region are all that is necessary to provide general protection. Nothing is gained by over damping noise in a lower frequency range, provided that the low frequency energy is not too strong. If you fire cannons for a living, then you probably have to worry about low frequency damage. Drums, probably not. I always wondered about using one of those dog shades to help keep the upper treble sound of the drum kit from getting up around the ears. Didn’t try it yet, though… Possibly I’ll coat both sides of the cone with some good sound absorbing foam, not the kind commonly available, but the stuff noise engineers use for machine noise. The problem with EQing the sound you are making is that you tend to want to correct for the deficiencies in what you are hearing. So if you use foam to EQ sound above 1k, then your drum kit will feel too heavy in the mids and bass and you will lighten up on the power you are delivering to the bass and toms. It’s just like recording engineers. If they work in a room that is bass light (as in acoustic tile ceiling) then they will bring more bass into the mix and end up with a bass heavy record. The musician style ear plugs promote full range damping, all frequencies equally. That sounds nice at first but when the volume goes down, we lose sensitivity to the bass more than we loose sensitivity to the treble. That’s why we have to use bass boost when we turn the volume down. So we don’t want to turn the volume down a flat 20 dB. It has to slope some, 20 dB in the treble is OK but maybe only about 15 dB in the midrange and only 10 dB in the bass. See “Fletcher Munson curves”. Arthur Noxon Acoustical Engineer
