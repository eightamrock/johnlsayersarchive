Greetings everybody, and thank you for the wealth of information on this site. I have read many of the posts here, as well as Rod's 2nd edition of "BILTP". (which was a fantastic read, except for the math - my goodness). I have a commercial unit inside a warehouse. The usable space of the unit is 27' wide, 60' deep, and 14' tall. The unit is seperated from neighbors on each side by concrete block walls, using standard 8x8x16" hollow masonry blocks. The ceiling of the warehouse has exposed steel Z beams running the width, an insulation on top of those, and then I can only assume the roofing structure. The flooring of the unit is a concrete slab of undetermined thickness. No neighbors above or below, as this is a single level construction. My plan is to build a rehearsal studio inside the warehouse. This means the only recording that would take place is simply the live sound of a practice. The studio is more for the rehearsal side of the business. Sound isolation is very important to me to stay in good graces with the neighbors, while letting bands practice at or near full show volume. I wand to build a completely enclosed studio to make this happen. My initial plans would be; Double stud walls on three of the four sides. (The fourth side would be placed within inches of one of the concrete clock walls) Double drywall with Green Glue on the inside and outside, and insulation. If I understand correctly, this is a good double leaf system with an STC of 63. (In the case of the "fourth wall", it would be double drywall with Green Glue:stud wall:insulation:air gap:concrete block wall) The ceiling will be newly framed, with the joists attached to my inner stud wall. This brings me to my question; If a system is only as good as it's weakest link, that would be my ceiling. As I understand, my best option is to hang double drywall on the ceiling using a RISC assembly. This accomplishes mass and decoupling. Insulate the ceiling for absorption, and the GG does the dampning. But this doesn't provide the same amount of isolation as my double stud walls, does it? So, are the double stud walls just wasted money? Is there a ceiling option that I have overlooked? Thank you to eveyone for your input and ideas! -Bryan

Hi Bryan, and welcome to the mad house! :) One question here: are you planning to use the entire space for the rehearsal room? Or are you planning to include other things in there too? Alternatively, are you planning to leave a large gap between the existing concrete block walls and the studio? The reason I ask is because it seems like you are planning a three-leaf construction. If you build a double-stud wall with drywall on each set of studs, then you are adding those two new leaves to the existing leaf, which is the concrete block walls, making a total of three leaves. I'm not sure I understand why you want to do that. The only thing I can think of is that you plan to leave very large air gaps around the studio, between the existing outer leaf and the two new leaves, but that seems like a waste of space to me.
The ceiling will be newly framed, with the joists attached to my inner stud wall. This brings me to my question; ... As I understand, my best option is to hang double drywall on the ceiling using a RISC assembly.
If you are going to rest your inner-leaf ceiling on your inner-leaf walls, and they are fully decoupled, then you do not need RSIC clips. The clips are designed to decouple surfaces that would otherwise be coupled (eg, on two sides of a single stud wall), but in your case you say you are going to build separate double stud walls, fully decoupled, so you don't need the clips. Partially decoupling something that is already fully decoupled serves no useful purpose.
If a system is only as good as it's weakest link, that would be my ceiling.
Most likely not. It is much more likely that your weakest link will be your doors, windows (if you have any), HVAC ducting, or electrical installation. It is relatively easy to get 65 dB of isolation from a wall or ceiling, not so easy to get that from a window, even harder to get it from a door or HVAC system, and really hard to get it from a badly installed electrical system. All it takes is one ignorant electrician to cut a hole in your wall to install a light switch or outlet, and your entire isolation just went down the drain. IT sounds like your wall and ceiling plan is heading in the right direction, but you didn't mention anything about your plans for doors, whether or not you will have windows, what type of HVAC system you are thinking of, or how you plan to do your electrical work. I'd be focusing on those things, after you get your wall issue sorted out. - Stuart -
