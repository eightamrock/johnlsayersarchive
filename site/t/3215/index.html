knightfly2 March 2005#17
The term "flanking" as used in acoustics did indeed originate from the practice of "getting behind their flanks", as in warhorses; in the case of acoustics, it's used to describe several things, all of which "flank" your attempts at blocking the sound. On those bare joists, first of all they will firmly tie the two walls together through structure; so whatever vibrates one side will also vibrate the other. This can be an effective "short circuit" clear through one room and onto the opposite one, since sound travels so wel (and so fast) through most solids. The things I would think could happen with your open joists are, one, a complete "pass-through" of sound from the room on one end of the joist to the one on the opposite; and two, any sound impinging on those joists would also then travel to whatever structure they are fastened to, and from there on to anything in the building that isn't de-coupled, finally appearing in any wall/ceiling/floor panel that is ALSO firmly attached to structure, then re-radiating into that area's air space. The amount of sound affecting the joists could be lessened slightly by absorption, but the MAIN use of absorption in this case would be to "disappear" the joists acoustically WITHIN THE ROOM, not to stop much in the way of transfer to other spaces. In order to do that, you would need to box each beam with resiliently mounted double drywall, which is NOT attached solidly to ANY structure - then, those "boxes" would need to be acoustically treated for room acoustics, and they would also isolate flanking both into and out of the room. Since this is a relatively short-term deal, only you can decide how important that is to you; I'm only making the point so you're aware of possible problems. HTH... Steve
