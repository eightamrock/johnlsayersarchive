From the negative comes the positive, good for you! Your interior wall only need be a frame with the mass added directly to the frame. The clips and hat track which are designed to "decouple" the mass, are not required since by virtue of the single frame that is not attached to anything...the wall is already decoupled:) In order to overcome the existing exterior wall you will need to have a larger air space in between it and the interior frame. So the question is, what kind of isolation does the current wall configuration provide...is it adequate for your needs?

xSpace19 April 2011#4
"Thanks for this tip. Unfortunately the extra hat and clips have already been purchased...so I can't save any money by your excellent suggestion..." I didn't bring up saving money. It isn't important, assuming you already own the material. The assembly, a mass/spring/mass that you are wanting to do does not require a framed wall with clips and hat track on any of the frames. The mass/spring/mass is a simple image but a difficult to understand process. The process of decoupling is as simple as this. One hard boundary (usually exterior brick or OSB, etc) attached to a framed assembly. Then there is the air space:) Then the next framed assembly with an interior hard boundary usually sheetrock). Currently you have a decoupled mass/spring/mass with the exterior wall assembly. NOW...you want to, just because you have it, install clips and hat track on the interior assembly. If you review the data on clips and track you will see that that TL(transmission loss) suffers in the low end and the low frequency area is where you want to get the most support. So if we look at your current, plus the pending...we see that the lose in low end frequency isolation is multiplied by two basically making all the work you are doing and all the money you are spending...useless to your goal.

xSpace24 April 2011#10
You are wanting to overcome an existing triple leaf correct? What Glenn mentioned, the 8"-12" will work...but, in order to really get over this 3 leaf, you need more distance. 3 leaf assemblies exist everywhere like the floor/ceiling/roof hard boundaries, yep cant get away from them:). Or like in a hotel corridor, which some might call a four leaf. But the thing that makes it work is the huge distance between wall assemblies. So while the 8"-12" will work...if you can get more you NEED to get more space in between the existing triple and the next hard boundary.
gullfo24 April 2011#11
Brien is right in that to be fully effective on a triple leaf or quad leaf you need more space. that said, looking at your exterior construction and the 2x drywall you already have there with the "third" leaf the sheathing and stucco, i think that if you can go 8-12" i think you'll be OK because the outer leaf materials are different and the resonant frequencies are different. as just a rough calculation, the inner walls to the drywall on the exterior is over 20db greater than the exterior wall itself on 8" and 23db on 12". and your exterior wall calculates to around 40db TL. of course doing a proper sound level test on the existing structure would make all the difference...

Ted White3 June 2011#18
To overcome a triple leaf issue, the extended air cavity is certainly a good idea. Similarly, having the extreme outer leaves as massive and as damped as possible will also work. The larger the cavity and the more mass the extreme outer leaves have, the less severe the effects of the dual air cavities (triple leaf) will be.
