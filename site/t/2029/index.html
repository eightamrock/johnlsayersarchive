knightfly26 July 2004#6
Everyone has given good advice so far - have him use lots of masking tape markers, and mark positions of EVERYTHING in the construction - studs, joists, conduits, etc - no wall that intersects with another wall or ceiling should have ANY wallboard surface in hard contact with its intersecting surface, nor should any piece of RC come closer than an inch of the intersection. Mark the locations of all the strips of RC and run them straight and true end to end, so that you don't miss with any screws; mark stud locations so you do NOT put any screws through the wallboard and RC and into a stud, just in case the screws might be long enough to touch the stud. Leave your 1/4" gap all around the perimeter of each wall surface (not between sheets of wallboard on a given wall, though) - use spacers to keep this gap. Caulk these perimeter gaps with acoustic rated caulk/sealant. \ If in doubt about ceiling support, get specific information from the manufacturer of the actual RC you're using regarding spacing of the channels, for the EXACT amount of panel weight you'll be using. RC is for all intents and purposes a "spring" - and just like a spring, it needs to NOT be at either end of its "travel" if it is to act as a spring. The manufacturer will know their specific product's characteristics better than anyone, so their spacing recommendations should be taken over anything you read in the USG manual - USG, as I understand it, no longer makes RC; and those companies who do, may have changed things like gauge, perforation ratio of the resilient leg, etc - Use proper screws for each application - screws for wood when attaching RC to wooden framing, screws for steel when attaching wallboard to RC or to steel studs, etc - for all but top layers, you can use half as many screws (twice as far apart) as standard, then the top layer gets a full schedule. Longer screws for successive layers, always at least 3/8" longer than total material thickness, 1/2" longer is better (but watch placement, as I mentioned above) Do NOT, repeat NOT, let your friend put up a course of wallboard and THEN use RC and more wallboard - I've talked to a lot of experienced contractors who've claimed RC didn't work, and without exception found that they had NO IDEA of the physics involved and had put the stuff in the wrong order. Hope that helps, if your guy has any more questions or doubts now's the time to clear them up, not AFTER... Steve

knightfly26 July 2004#14
Andrew, good call on placing the shims coincident with studs, thanks - also, I forgot to mention marking where each and every screw goes - again, seems too anal - but you'll reconsider the first time you try to put a second layer's screw through the head of an under layer's screw, and only end up tearing the crap out of your wall - Stone-man - 1.75" sound more like it; still, I'm wondering if you could stand to put up maybe a 1.75" steel stud frame (spaced maybe an inch from the existing) and a layer or two of wallboard there - no RC needed, because of the separation between outer and inner wall, and better isolation. When using NON-load-bearing, light gauge steel studs OR RC, the flanking problem caused by fasteners pretty much goes away because it's absorbed mostly by the insulation - but if fastening straight to wood studs on BOTH sides of a SINGLE frame, flanking from fasteners can cause as much as maybe 10 dB loss in isolation. Let me know if you can consider the second frame idea, or if you think that wall is already "good enough" - if that's the case, then maybe just another layer of wallboard. Keep in mind, though, that a completely solid heavy wall like that will also increase the need for more bass trapping once the room's complete... Steve

knightfly26 July 2004#16
In that case I'd just add a layer of drywall - you could probably just use panel adhesive, and just strip-glue every 16" (like stud spacing) - this will avoid the new panel changing the coincidence frequency of the old by becoming "one piece"; if you just glue strips on spacing similar to studs, you maintain separate coincidence frequency for each panel and the wall has better isolation. Putting another layer on RC, with only 1/2" air gap, would give you a mass-air-mass resonance of around 200 hZ - this could become a real pain to fix with treatment later. The mass of the blocks and everything else should give you pretty good isolation as is, probably around STC 55 or so... Steve

knightfly27 July 2004#20
In this case, that makes the most sense. When you do, assuming you don't have ANY wetness in that area, I would put the new layer resting on 1/4" thick spacers , do NOT spread glue on the entire panel but only 2-3" wide stripes running the same as studs (2" stripes if you use 16" centers, 3" if you use 24" centers) - spread the glue stripes on the existing wall vertically, set the spacers directly under each stripe, then screw the new panels onto the old using Type G, or gypsum, laminating screws to hold the new panel against the old til the glue sets - after that, you can mud over the laminating screws, caulk around the new panel, and finish (tape and texture, etc) This will get you better isolation than full surface glue... Steve
