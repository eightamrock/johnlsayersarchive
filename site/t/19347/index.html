That's fine. This is not intended to be a recording studio, just an isolation room for drums, so the low ceiling won't be too much of an issue. And as folks sometimes say around here: a room that isn't ideal is still a hell of a lot better than no room at all! On the building and your plan: OK, you have some good things going for you, and some not so good things:
The garage is built with 140mm solid concrete blocks and rendered on the outside, the floor is concrete slab
That's the "good part"! Lots of mass there, in both the floor and walls, and the floor is "slab on grade", so that's excellent.
and the roof is felted with roof tiles.
That's the not so good part. More on that later, but this is the weak link, and will be the source of your nightmares in coming weeks.... :) But there are ways of dealing with it...
We managed to borrow a sound meter from my sons teacher and when on his drums it reads about 125db
:shock: That's rather on the high side! Not impossible, but he must have been bashing the hell out of those drums to produce 125 dB. You did measure with "C" rating and "slow" response, right? Also, at those levels, I hope you were all wearing good hearing protection. At 125 dB, you suffer irreversible hearing loss after about 3 1/2" minutes of exposure. 125 dB is also beyond the threshold of pain: that must have been hurting your ears physically. The other possibility is that meter you used is not calibrated or not good quality: There's a lot of cheap and useless Chinese junk on the market right now: those are the meters you can pick up for around US$ 50 or less on e-bay and Amazon, new. A good meter will cost around US$ 100 or so, new. Brands like Extech, Galaxy and suchlike. One other possibility is that you were not using the meter correctly: Measurements should be taken roughly at chest height, and about three feet (one meter) away from source, with the meter held out away from your body, and the mic angled upwards at about 45° or so. I'm not saying that your son didn't really produce 125 dB: just that such a level is about 10 times higher than you'd normally expect for drums played in typical manner. 125 dB is about the same level as putting your head about a foot away from a pneumatic hammer going flat out....
the average reading of our environment was between 50db and 75db as we live next to quite a busy road in the daytime,
Where did you take that reading? Inside the room, our outside? It would be helpful to have a reading of the drums measured at the location of your neighbours house.

as he is only having the back of garage i was going to partition it using a double stud or maybe a staggered stud wall this is what will have the doorway in
Isolation is an "all or nothing" system. It is only as good as the weakest path out of the room. If you do your floor and three walls to the level where they give you 50 dB of isolation, but the other wall only gives you 40 and the ceiling only gives you 30, then your sum total isolation is 30. Implying that you wasted a lot of time and money getting your good walls up to 50 ... Think of it like a fish tank: If you put glass on only three sides, but they decide to use thin plywood on the back, and cardboard on the bottom, how well will that hold water? :) Even though the glass will work fine, the other materials wont: water will leak out through those, splashing all over the place. Same thing applies with your room: If you want to stop sound at a certain level of isolation, then ALL the walls AND the ceiling, AND the door(s) AND the window(s) AND the HVAC system AND the electrical system must ALL be done to the same level. In other words, you'll probably have to beef up that forth wall a bit more to get the high levels of isolation you need, and you will DEFINITELY need to do something about the ceiling/roof.
The ceiling, as it is only 2.1m high i was going to put the new joists in-between as to retain a bit of hight but still decoupling from existing structure.
Great, but what are you going to do about the ROOF? With your current plan, the roof is your outer leaf, yet it has low mass, and is not sealed: that's not a very god outer leaf. You won't get good isolation out of that. To fix that, you have two options: 1) Beef up the mass of the roof, and seal the entire thing air tight. That's probably not an attractive offer, and possibly not even a legal alternative. Roof decks usually are required to be ventilated underneath, to prevent overheating, the formation of condensation, and other nasties. Sealing up the roof space defeats that, which is bad in may ways. So you might not want to go this way. 2) Don't touch the roof: leave it like it is, and instead go for the dreaded "three-leaf" system. In other words, slap a couple of layers of drywall on the existing joists, carefully sealed and with GG, to act as your "middle-leaf", then build your complete "room-in-a-room" below that. I would go for option #2. However, that does mean that you lose quite a bit of ceiling height.
As for insulation i was going to get acoustic rockwool rwa45 slabs 100mm or 75mm not sure yet.
The more, the merrier! :)
Does it matter what sort of insulation you use ?
As long as it is open-cell, and its density is roughly in the "good" range for that type of insulation, then you are good. If you choose fiberglass insulation, then the density needs to be around 30 kg/m3, and if you use mineral wool the it needs to be around 50 kg/m3.
Does it aid in soundproofing or is it purely for insulating the room ?
Ohhh yes! It does aid, and big time! Basically your "room-in-a-room" plan creates something called a fully-decoupled two-leaf MSM system. That's a resonant system, where MSM stands for "Mass - Spring - Mass". The layers on each side of the wall are the Mass (in your case, your outer-leaf mass is the concrete walls, and the inner-leaf mass is the drywall). The spring in the middle is the air. You don't normally think of air as being "springy", but as far as sound waves see it, it sure is very springy. So you have a resonant system, which works exactly the same as a weight bobbing up and down on a spring: it oscillates at its natural frequency. You "tune" your wall to the lowest frequency you can get by using the correct mass on each leaf, and the correct size of the air gap. However, the air in that gap can also resonate in many other ways, and you do not want that to happen, since resonance is a very efficient method for transferring energy. To stop the air from doing all its resonant things, you "damp" it by putting the insulation in there. That's sort of like taking your weight that is freely bouncing up and down on its spring, and instead of letting it do that in air, let it do that in a tub of molasses... The molasses heavily damps the oscillation, to the point where it just doesn't happen. Insulation is to sound waves as molasses is to a bouncing weight. So yes, you totally, absolutely and definitely need to fill the air cavity with insulation. Oh yes, and it also has good thermal properties... :)
I think my biggest problem is going to be the ceiling, i was thinking of plaster boarding the existing joists first and then build rwr but as i am going to put the new joists in-between to keep some hight I’m unsure what to do ?
Your thinking is spot on. You have the right idea. Yes, it will be a 3-leaf system to a certain extent, but it is still your best option. Unfortunately, it does mean losing head room. However, if you build the ceiling "inside out", then you won't loose any additional head room to the acoustic treatment that your room will need, so from that point of view you gain space up there. Yes, the final outcome will be a low finished surface, but you don't have many other options, realistically, and as you say, this is just a practice room for drums, not Abbey Road! :) - Stuart -

I would suggest that it is waaaaay to early to make a start! You should get your entire design fully complete before you lift a hammer or even buy a bag of nails and some 2x4's. There's a huge amount of stuff that goes into a design, even though yours is just a very simple room. For example: doors are critical for isolation, so you need to have those designed properly, including the framing, double perimeter seals, heavy-duty hinges (to handle the extra weight), and the automatic door closer. Windows are critical too (if you have any, of course!). Your electrical system is also very important: you cannot just punch holes in your new inner leaf to put in plugs, switches and light fittings! Ventilation is another biggie: the room has to be sealed in order to be soundproof, but ventilation implies making big holes in the room to get air in and out. So you need to design the silencers to go on those holes, so that sound cannot escape. Etc. If you don't have all of that lined up and fully designed BEFORE you start building, then you are in for a major disaster. Not trying to scare you! Just pointing out the way it always, invariably goes with builds that are not designed properly: check out a few threads on the forum, and you'll see that those who are not prepared either end up with lousy rooms that don't isolate and sound awful, or they end up having to tear things down and re-build them the right way. And there's also a rather large number of build threads that just sort of come to an inconclusive halt after the builder figured out that his build had gone wrong. We never hear about those guys again, but it doesn't take much imagination to figure out why their build threads are abandoned... So please take the time to learn SketchUp (it isn't that hard... really! I promise! :) ), and do the design properly, taking into account all the aspects. Post your design here as you move forward, so we can comment on it and perhaps suggest ways of improving it, or of doing things another way to make it easier / cheaper / better. - Stuart -

Soundman20209 May 2014#9
It's looking pretty good, Barry. Nice work! :thu: It pays to learn SketchUp. :) Just a couple of minor issues: 1- Use double top plates on your stud framing, not just single. For many reasons. 2- Install the ceiling drywall so that it goes all the way to the top plates, with the wall drywall rising to just underneath it 3- You'll need nailing strips and cross bracing on the ceiling framing. Right now, you have no place to nail the ends of the ceiling drywall, where they meet the top plates. 4- Use triple studs on each side of the doorway rough openings, instead of just double studs. The doors will be VERY heavy, so they need the extra support. You don't want the framing sagging and bending as the doors open and close. 5- Fix your door headers: They must be supported with jack studs, and have cripple studs above (or solid header above). 6- Have you considered doing your ceiling "inside out"? It might make sense in your case. Apart from that you seem to be on the right track! :) - Stuart -

Soundman20201 July 2014#15
I would have to cut plasterboard into pieces as i couldn't get full size sheets up in-between the joists to fit on top ?
Careful there! You might not be able to do that! Your roof deck needs ventilation from below, for a couple of reasons (heat, condensation, etc.), and that is normally accomplished with vents under the eaves, and either a ridge vent or gable end vents. You cannot block those. If there are no eave vents but there ARE ridge vents or gable vents, then the airflow is probably intended to come from the room itself. So before you go sealing off any of that, you should first look into the the roof deck ventilation issue: Only if you are 100% certain that it is done correctly, only then should you proceed with your plan to create the leaf in the manner you are thinking of. - Stuart -
