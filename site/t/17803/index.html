Reed Black14 September 2012#2
Well, here's my first idea (not always the best). :roll: First of all, forgive my use of imperial measurements, but it's just what I'm used to. Whatever the equivalent product is in metric will do you fine, even if it differs a little in length or width. For densities, though, use similar. You actually have a decent room proportion going on, with reasonable modal distribution. That's a good start. But it's not the "reflections from the walls" that are your biggest problem -- it's the bass response, which is always extremely distorted in smaller rooms. I don't think the rockwool on the ceiling (or your 8" traps) is doing you much good -- it's not dense enough, and in the case of the ceiling, it has no air-gap behind it. Instead, use Owens-Corning 703 or equivelant fiberglass (3lbs/ft3--what's the conversion?) wrap it in cloth and mount it in a 1x lumber wood frame with at least a couple inches of air-gap behind the back of the fiberglass. The more air gap, the lower the bass absorber will go in Hertz (as well as height!). We want the former and not the latter. So as much as you can spare up to 7.5" would make sense to me. I wouldn't do more than that because you want to walk around comfortably without ducking! Additionally, that'll take care of the quick high-end reflections down from the ceiling just as well as the rockwool was, while being much more broadband, and getting to the lows that distort your room response the most. The bookshelves might be another good candidate for 703 panel fittings--use the cubbies and shelves as pre-made frames--though those boxes are gonna be tuned to particular frequencies (bad), and they'll be more or less subdivisions of each other (also bad), so I'd fill them with rockwool that's 50 kg/m^3 (even the stuff from your ceiling if it's that density!) in order to damp that resonance as much as you can. Then close off each cabinet with pieces of the rigid fiberglass cut to fit across the surface (after being wrapped in cloth). Do the same with the space under each desk where your feet would go. Do one of the wall with the door too in the air-gap fiberglass panelling I recommended for the ceiling. Make sure to plan properly so the door can still swing open! Then you'll have both a decent amount of bass trapping and three absorptive boundaries and corners facing three "live" boundaries. That would probably make for a decent amount of reflectivity in the mid- and high-end to sound reasonably live, but would prevent rays from bouncing back-and-forth more than once. and causing too much blurring of the image. The panelling on the ceiling and wall should go all the way to the corners, too, to involve as much of the other boundaries as possible. If you want to go extra fancy, you make more 703 panels that straddle the ceiling and wall corners the way your rockwool boxes do--but faced with a layer of the heavier, semi-rigid fiberglass instead of low-density rockwool stuff. The paneling on the ceiling would also be a decent way to mount lighting, though it might be getting too close to head-height to be comfortable. You also probably want to orient the drumset along the long axis of the room. However, the overheads might sound asymmetrical... in which case, you'd probably have to build a panel on the "left" wall (facing the bookshelves), and of decent depth, to balance out the image. Regarding the glass, don't sweat it yet. You may want to build a standing gobo-type absorber in the future to set in front of the first reflection point on the window wall when you're recording, remove to look outside when you're just practicing... but it's not low hanging fruit, yet. The other stuff is more important, and easier. Anyway, that's my unscientific, unthought-out analysis. Others?

Soundman202026 September 2012#14
In the help file I downloaded from the REW website it said I needed a SPL Meter.
That's only if you want to calibrate REW so that it gives accurate absolute level readings. You do not need to do that if you just want to analyze the acoustics of the room, since you only need relative level readings, not absolute ones. Some meters also do have a direct out for the mic, which is good for use as a measurement mic in some applications. But that does not apply to you. So like Reed said, you can just use your SM81 connected directly as the input channel to REW. That's all you need. The sound level meter is very useful for measuring and calibrating your room, but you don't need it for the REW analysis. - Stuart -

Unfortunately, I'm a Mac user as well, and never had much luck with REW. I bought a very expensive program, SpectraFoo, but because of it's price-tag I don't think it's appropriate for you. However, with REW, try just using your internal sound processing system instead of your outboard unit and you should be fine (headphone port and mic "in"). Don't obsess over the possible colorations of your onboard converters and whatnot; I know the REW manual makes a big deal out of it, but any aberrations in your testing equipment pale in comparison with room coloration, which commonly consists of ≈30 dB swings across the frequency spectrum! :twisted: Stuart, or others: Any other ideas for us Mac users? Take special interest and care with your measurements below 500 Hz. That's what's most important. R.

try just using your internal sound processing system instead of your outboard unit and you should be fine
That's probably OK, as long as you always use the same system for ALL your measurements, both now and in the future. I think I've said this before; it's not so much the absolute values you are looking for with the treatment analysis, but rather the changes that you achieve with each round of treatment: For isolation testing, you most definitely need something that accurately measured objective, absolute values, but for treatment measurement, you can probably get by on something that isn't calibrated to provide absolute readings. It's the relative changes that are important, so you can see what is working and what isn't at each stage. The problem comes if you change your system: If you ever switch from the cheap two-dollar Mac pre-amp and five-dollar Mac digitizer circuit, to a professional XX-hundred dollar pre-amp and thousand dollar interface, then you can no longer use ANY of the previous readings for comparison, since you have made a major change in the signal chain. I have no idea what claims Apple makes for the response curves of their "mic in" connector, but I'm pretty sure that it doesn't get very close to what even a semi-decent piece of pro equipment offers! :) One thing you could do, is to test it: Send some full-spectrum pink noise out of a good pro interface, into the mic connector on the Mac, and see what you get. Might be interesting to see just how good/bad it is. I suspect that it might be fine in the mid range, and not so fine on the lows and highs. But anyway, the point is that as long as you don't change your system while the studio is being built, and always use it in the exact same way, with the exact same software settings, then it might be OK.
