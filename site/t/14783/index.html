Been there done that and don't care for it. Thanks in advance for suggestions. Sincerely, Dean Mitchell

Soundman20209 September 2010#4
Really, I have treated the room like your link shows before. It's way too dead.
Then you didn't do it right! :) Your room right now is a resonant cave, with modal issues at several frequencies in the kick, tom and snare range (but with large gaps in the spread at the low end, due to the tiny dimensions), and specular issues up where the cymbals live (and the snare attack). Plus, the room has a REALLY low ceiling that will splatter your overheads with major comb filtering artefacts, making your cymbals sound tinny and harsh. The room ratio is OK, almost right on Louden's best ratio, but that's about the only good thing about the room: the walls look to be parallel so you must be having major issues with flutter echo, too, and it is VERY small. At a guess, I'd say its really, really hard to track cleanly in that room right now. The recording you provided isn't much use as a sample, since it is purely kick and snare (no toms, hat or cymbals). A recording of the full kit, played both fast and slow, loud and soft, would help to get a better idea of what is going on in there. But even from that recording you already provided the problems are grossly evident: the long clangy tails on the snare, the dullness of the kick attack, and the general lack of clarity already indicate what huge issues you have with that room! (Of course, some of that might just be the poor mic placement technique). Personally, I'd follow Ro's advice for the basic modal treatment, then put full floor-to-ceiling angled broadband slot walls on at least two of the side walls to keep it nice and live, and maybe some checkerboarded 703 panels on parts of the other two walls, to deal with the flutter. And a thick layer of 703 on the ceiling, directly above the kit, to make it possible to use overheads without suffering too badly from comb filtering. That would help a lot, but that room is never going to be really good for drums: it is way too small. - Stuart -

You came for advice (or maybe not) on how to make your room sound the best it possibly can. You got it. You didn't like it. That's YOUR problem, not MINE, so I won't be missing any sleep over it. :) The only thing you can do to make your room more "live" than it already is ( :shock: ), is to coat all the walls with a thick layer of extremely dense concrete all around, while trying to make the shape of the room as spherical as possible. Then seal the surface with paint, for that final touch of extreme brightness. Then move your drums as close as possible to the geometric center of the room, place all your mics up against the walls at random locations, and enjoy! :lol: (Maybe you could also add glue some carpet on the ceiling, and egg crates on the walls, for the final edge.) That's all the advice you'll get from me. If you prefer the acoustic advice you get at Gearslutz ( :shock: :!: :? ), then go for it! I'm sure yo will undoubtedly find many like-minded folks over there, who will gush ecstatically over your drum sounds. But over here, most of us prefer the professional approach, the one that real studios use, to capture the kind of true drum sounds that you hear on actual, real albums that people actually go out and buy, and listen to, and love... Bye. Have fun in your cave... - Stuart -

John Sayers10 September 2010#10
Dean - unfortunately drywall is not a very good reflector - I'd line the room in angled timber - by angled I mean break the wall into sections with angular shapes on the walls and ceiling and in the corners - have no absorption in the room at all just shiny wood surfaces - add some tile/stone surfaces if you have it. That will extend the reverbtime in the highs as you suggested.
