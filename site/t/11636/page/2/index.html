Soundman202023 March 2009#28
the interior walls & ceiling won't be attached to the exterior structure. is floating the wrong word to use?
Yup! :) Like NativeLuv said, what you are doing is "decoupling" your innear laf from your outer leaf, which is most definitely a very good thing! So that's fine, and don't worry about my comment. "Floating" is something else, which can also be good, but is really hard to do right. Floating your walls means that you build your entire inner leaf on top of some kind of resilient support, such as neoprene or EPDM or something like that, which acts as a spring, so that the wall does not transmit sound to the floor. It's not a bad thing, but getting it right is not easy, as it requires that you must calculate the final mass of your walls and ceiling accurately in advance, then figure out what type of material you can use to float that mass, how much of it you need, and where to place it so that the entire wall floats evenly, compressing the resilient material perfectly to the middle of the range where it will act as a spring. If you put too much mass on it, then it "bottoms out" and flanks instead of floating. If you put too little mass on it, then it doesn't compress enough to float, and once again it flanks instead. If you get it right, its a good thing. But getting it right ain't easy, and getting it wrong means that you wasted a lot of time, effort and money for nothing. You'd normally only try to float your walls if you needed extreme isolation, beyond what most people need, but that doesn't seem to be the case with your build. So you are "decoupling", not "floating", and that's perfect! I just got a bit concerned when I saw your "floating" comment, and wondered if you were doing it right, but you are not even trying to do it at all, and that's fine too! - Stuart -

Sandersd13 April 2009#45
Just bought a bunch of Ultratouch and a box of 6" impaling clips. Wow, that stuff is tough - almost impossible to cut. A razor blade just skims over the surface. I ended up using an 8" carving knife and a carbide sharpener that had to be used before every cut. Buy the special knife they sell for cutting it. And forget impaling it - it's like trying to poke a hole in leather. They ought to ship a bunch over to Iraq to replace the kevlar vests. I had to wear a dust mask and goggles to protect my eyes from all the cotton dust. I didn't the first time and it felt like sand in my eyes towards evening. But it's not itchy on your skin. Next time I'll wear long sleeves and use mineral wool. It''s much easier to fab.

ideaofnorth12 May 2009#49
Another update. So, we finished applying ultratouch to the interior walls, and built 4 boxes for the ceiling. The ultratouch was tough to get to stay up. I think it might be easier with 16" on center walls, but 24" was just too wide to get the kind of compression you need to keep it in place. we tried the little metal insulation rods and even just nailing the top of the batt to the bottom of the top stud. i ended up using some roofing nails with a little plastic washer through the batts to hold them up, upholstery style. we measured and figured on about .25" of the nail going into the drywall which is 2 layers of 5/8" with green glue. I did get an electric carving knife, but found just tearing it by hand, phone book style, worked the best and most efficiently. It will compress into spaces and neaten up pretty well. We also ran electric from the main panel in the house to a little subpanel in the studio. We ran one loop circuit of regular outlets for lights & stuff, and then ran around 8 individual breakers to individual isolated ground outlet for instruments & recording gear. Those isolated grounds run to a star grounding situation behind the garage. So, I've been rushing to finish because I had a jazz session (Jeff Platz quartet featuring the amazing Daniel Carter) booked for a couple weekends ago. We made the deadline, and I was really pleased with how the room sounded. Not overly dead, actually, I think because of the exposed sections of ceiling and concrete floor. Room has a very warm sound, and most importantly the band felt really comfortable in there. An added bonus was how well the isolated ground worked. Jeff uses a 70s silver face deluxe reverb that we've had RF and noise problems with at other places. It was super quiet and clean in the new space, sounded incredible. This inside out design, Rod's book and of course all the great advice I've received here really, REALLY has paid off. We're on a fairly busy street, and during the session we had some incredibly quiet moments (solo clarinet, etc), and you could've heard a pin drop. No noise from outside. Conversely, I've had someone beating on a drumset and from the outside it really sounds like someone watching tv, very little bleed at all. The weak spot right now is the doorway, but we're planning on beefing up both doors with an inset layer of plywood w/green glue, and another stop with trunk rubber gasketing as in Rod's book. My current to-do list: - Put up fabric (Osnaburg I've decided on) and trim over the Ultratouch. - Paint the ceiling. - Buy & install the minisplit. - Build and install the window plugs (they're covered in 2 layers of drywall for now). - Do some testing and see what's happening frequency-wise. But the nice thing is the room is operational for the time being. Here's an mp3 of one of the takes from that session, if anyone's curious how it's sounding: http://www.peapodrecordings.com/shared/16_IMPROV_V6.mp3
