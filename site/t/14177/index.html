Soundman202012 March 2010#6
How will putting a solid top on them help with flutter echo?
You did say they are angled already, so putting a hard top on will be reflecting things at angles, not directly back the way things came. It's one trick that John uses regularly in his clouds, to help deal with flutter. As long as you have a reasonable angle on the cloud it will work fine.
Won't that just create other flutter echoes from the top of the cloud's hard reflective surface to the ceiling's hard reflective surface
You only get flutter between parallel surfaces, so the key here is the angle. If you don't tilt them, then sure, you'll still have flutter, but with slightly different characteristics: But if you do angle them enough, then your flutter is gone.
Are you saying the flutter echoes pass through the 4" 703, hit the ceiling, and then pass back through the 4" 703 and are still audible?
Yup! Each passage through will, of course, remove some energy, but not all of it: things are still bouncing around in there. But if you put a hard top and tilt it, then ALL of the energy is now going in a different direction, after the very first bounce, and won't be back. Search the forum for "cloud" and "angle"...
I guess it will make more sense to discuss this once I'm sure of the ceiling height, huh?
Yup! :) - Stuart -

Soundman202013 March 2010#15
and also make a few quick RTA measurements of the room. ... should I consider moving the reference speaker (Dynaudio AIR15) to the location of the instruments and get some RTA readings in that fashion?
Put your speaker right on the floor in one corner, as tight into the corner as you can get. That way it will probably excite all the modes associated with all three of those surfaces (wall, wall, floor). Since you also suspect your ceiling is a problem, try to figure out a way to repeat your reading with the speaker stuck up in the wall-wall-ceiling corner. You do this because modes terminate on walls, and at least three walls meet in corners, so you should be able to excite the modes for all three at once. If you were to put your measurement mic in the diametrically opposite corner, you'll have the best chance of picking up all those modes. Put a better idea is to put the mic where you normally listen, since that's the spot where ... well ... where you listen! That's what you want to analyze. - Stuart -

Soundman202017 March 2010#21
Any suggestions on the placement of the false wall knowing the hard dimensions of the room and seeing the RTA response?
One way of figuring it out would be to look for a known good room ratio that is close to what you already have, then work out how much you'd need to adjust one wall to get to that ratio. Tat will help with your mode spread. You could also then splay (angle) that new wall to deal with flutter echo. How much to angle it? Good question. In a control room, walls are splayed 6 degrees on each side, for a total of 12 degrees differential, but that is in order to get a good reflection free zone, as well as helping with flutter. So 12 degrees would work, but something less might work too. Perhaps one of the acoustic experts can help you out with the minimum angle that you'd need to splay.
Will one false wall really start to tame some of those peaks and nulls (with appropriate broadband absorption)?
The combination of both will do good things. Based on your new (accurate!) dimensions, your major issues should be at 29.7 Hz, 42.1, 49.1, 57.4, 59.5, 66.5, 77.1, 82.7, and 89.2. But that's only up to 90 Hz., where your room modes stop being the dominant factor. Beyond that there are still multiple recurring pairs of modes all the way up the scale.
