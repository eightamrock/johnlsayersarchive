Hello autophase, I am no expert here, but I have worked through the same issue of having a small space that I needed to work in. I designed something for you similar to what I built in my home. I am getting great results in my room and it was cheap enough that I could continue to save money for when I begin building my garage out into a small studio. The design I did for you includes superchunks in the front corners, a super superchunk in the rear, 2'x4'x4" panel absorbers on the walls spaced symmetrically 2" off the walls, and a 4'x4'x4" panel absorber spaced 2" off the front wall. I also suggest packing insulation into the cavity of the window and then drywalling over it. I left the blinds up between the insulation and the window and from the outside it still looks like a normal window. Like I said, it's basically my room but a bit smaller and I love it for what it is. I think this is a very good starting place for you and should be able to be accomplished for around $1000. The only other issue that you will need to work through is the first reflection points on the ceiling. With a height of only 7'5" it will make you feel very cramped by hanging a cloud but if you want to really use this room to mix I highly suggest it. If you are interested, I will take a few pictures of my room for you when I get home later... If you have the money and time, you can go with what you initially hinted at with the resonators and other more laborious and costly solutions. For reference, check out John's bedroom design at http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/download/file.php?id=5787 and Kendale's build at http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?f=10&t=4091. Kendale's is a great design for a room in a home but he had A LOT more space than you do. He does document construction of building his resonators very well for you though. Hopefully this well get you started or one of the experts on here can step in and correct me if I was wrong about anything or just add to what I've said.

Soundman20209 September 2010#9
It depends what you are trying to do! For the same thickness of absorption, leaving an air gap behind the panel extends the range down to lower frequencies, and increases effectiveness. Putting it right up against the wall tends to limit it more to higher frequencies. There is also the issue of angle of incidence: When a sound wave hits the panel "head on", then it "sees" the thickness of the panel, but when it hits at an angle (as most waves do), then it sees a greater thickness. Absorption works on the velocity component of a sound wave, not the pressure component, and right at the wall velocity is zero, for waves arriving at a high angle of incidence. So right at the wall, the absorption is doing nothing at all for waves arriving head-on. But for waves arriving at non-normal angles, there is some velocity component right at the wall boundary, and the absorption at the boundary is doing something for those waves. So if you leave a gap, you are moving the absorption further away from the zone where normal waves aren't affected (only non-normal waves), and more into the zone where all waves are affected by it. But you could get the same effect by just making the panel thicker. But that would be more expensive... It gets rather complex... Basic rule of thumb: leaving a gap behind the panel makes it more effective at lower frequencies and for higher angles of incidence. The bigger the gap, the lower it goes. But if you are leaving a really large gap, then you might as well fill part of it with more absorption, to increase the effectiveness even more. I guess that probably confused yo more than helped you! It even confused me, just writing it.... :) :shock:

tgauge13 September 2010#19
if you are going to wrap the entire abosrbers, make sure that it is no thicker than 3.5 mil (i believe that's what is suggested) and realize that this is going to reflect some higher frequencies that are not able to pass that plastic. You really have to experiment, some people wrap the entire absorber to reflect or "keep" the highs so that they don't have too dead of a room and some people cover just the back to let the highs get absorbed. I personally only covered the backs of my clouds because my ventilation system hits them and I didn't want anything flying down onto me. It's really a trial and error to see what you like. Also, when choosing fabric remember it needs to be breathable but most importantly it needs to be fire rated for your safety.
