len-morgan14 April 2006#26
What seems to have gotten lost in this discussion is what constitutes a "leaf." It has nothing to do with "seals." A leaf, plain and simple is a mass (as in mass-air-mass). It doesn't matter if it's one layer of drywall or two layers - unless you inadvertantly got a squirrel (sp?) between the sheets of drywall, you get a thicker (more massive) leaf with two layers, not two leaves. When you introduce an air gap (like the studs) between the two masses, you now have two leaves. The drawings that Kendale posted show this quite clearly. If you read Paul Woodlock's build diary, you will find that he discovered the third leaf effect in his own "bomb shelter" construction after he put up (and sealed) his two doors leading to the house. As I recall, he had turned up the boom box in the studio and then went into the house to see how well the doors sealed. There was a piece of plywood or MDF or something in the house which he picked up to take back into the studio and as he approached the closed door (which was silent), all of the sudden he could hear the music inside quite clearly. All he had was a flat piece of wood in his hands and that "third leaf" destroyed his isolation. It wasn't a "sealed" leaf. Just a piece of wood. As I was advised early on in my studio build, there <b>IS</b> a point where the air gap is so large that third leaf (or quad leaf in my case) is not an issue. But my air gap was a hallway between two free standing rooms and was over 8'. Unless you want to loose 8' or so around the entire structure (including the ceiling) you will either have to go the "bull and hammer" route OR sacrifice isolation. Since you mentioned that you are going to have drums and neighbors within 25', you would be better off to error on the side of caution, proven practice, and the advice of experts (like Rod, not me) and build it "right." len

sharward14 April 2006#28
Yes, insulation counts as "air" in the Mass-Air-Mass equation, provided air can pass through it. Examples of insulation that does not count as mass are closed cell foam, kraft paper backed batts (common mistake!*), and insulation that is packed in so tightly that it forms a bridge between the leaves (ideally, you want slight pressure on both leaves to help dampen the leaves, but not so much that it becomes a path to transfer vibration from one to the other). *Actually, I think kraft paper backed batts are semi OK if the paper is against one of the leaves... --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward14 April 2006#32
Remember -- wider air gaps give you better isolation, especially for lower frequencies which are the most troublesome. I have heard that 12" is a good number in that you get a lot of benefit with relatively little lost square footage. Remember that the air gap is measured from the backs of the wall surfaces, not the studs... So you can achieve a 12" air gap using 2x4s that are 5 inches apart (3.5 + 5 +3.5 = 12).

apexer25 April 2006#34
UPDATE: Removing the drywall was not hard at all!! I even managed to remove it in 8'X16" sections that will fit perfectly inbetween the studs after I remove the insulation to beef up the outside leaf. The best method I found was to 1. find the studs with a studfinder, and mark them 2. with a large drill bit drill until you hit the stud, then angle and push towards the piece of drywall you are trying to remove until it slips off the edge of the stud. Do this in 3 or 4 spots vertically up to 8 ft. 3. using a long straight edge, connect the dots, and draw a line with a pencil. 4. Using a jig saw, start sawing from the bottom right hole up to the top right, and across to the left stud, using the header as a guide. 5. while keeping some pressure on the piece of drywall so that it does not cave in on you, do the same thing on the left with the saw. 6. cut from the bottom holes down to the floor using either the jig saw, or a drywall hand saw. 7. pull the section out, and lay it on the floor. CAUTION: shut off all breakers, and try to use short jig saw blads, and drill bits. I did actually hit a wire with the drill, whoops. I will be able to fix it when I get all the drywall out. Does that make sense? Check out the pictures.
