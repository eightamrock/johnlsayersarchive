Soundman20206 March 2011#12
Thanks for fixing that, Scott, and welcome! First, a comment on something you said:
That west side could use some beefing up sound-wise.
Unfortunately, you can't beef up only one side: Sound is something like water: It spills out all over, even if the hole is on only one side. So beefing up just one side of the room won't work, as the sound will simply take the easy path out, through the other five sides. And since sound waves bend around objects that are smaller than the wavelength, low frequency sound escaping form the north, south, east and through the roof will simply bend around your entire house and be easily heard on the west. Plus, sound with shorter wavelengths might end up bouncing off other buildings, cars, trees, etc. on those sides, thus also being audible on the west. In other words, isolation a room is an "all or nothing" proposition: You have to do all five sides (including windows, doors and HVAC) to the same level of isolation. Anything less won't help, and you'll spend a lot of money for minimal results.
If I could make it soundproofed enough for my personal drum and guitar amp recording usage that would be great. Also open to electronic drums if soundproofing is too difficult and/or costly.
It might be possible to isolate your guitar amp enough, but unlikely that you'll be able to ply acoustic drums in there on that budget. Now for your questions:
1) Is ventilation going to be an issue given the construction type and living in the pacific NW?
Ventilating is always an issue in a studio. In order to isolate, you have to seal the room air tight, twice. Each leaf of yoru wall must be hermetically sealed. So if you plan to breath inside ( :shock: ) then you do need to think about HVAC. And of course HVAC means poking large holes in your sealed structure, which brings another series of treatment requirements, but it can be done.
2) Is anyone willing to help design for a fee?
It all depends: How much money do you have? :) Just kidding. It doesn't look like you have enough budget to pay a professional designer like John for his work and also still have enough money left over to actually build it. That sort of is the purpose of this forum: it is for folks who don't have enough money for that and are willing to go to the hassle of learning how to do the design work themselves. Of course, that means taking the time to learn the basics of acoustics, learn SketchUp, create their own designs and post them here for others to take a look at and offer suggestions for improvement. So if you have the time and the willingness to learn all this stuff and do the work yourself, then you don't really need to pay a designer. If you don't have the time or the inclination, then you might have to pay for that service, and there are a few people here who could help you with that.
3) Assuming I have the help ready to go and access to materials, how quickly can I get something properly designed and installed realistically?
Design could take a few days to a few weeks, or even months, depending on how complex it needs to be and how much of that you are going to do yourself. Construction could take a few more weeks to a few months, or even years, depending on how complex it is, how many people are working at once, their skills, the materials, how much money you have available at each point, and many other things. I guess that answer isn't much help, is it? :)
4) How should I handle ceiling?
Depends on what you want to do!
5) Should I do anything with the floor?
you said "Floor is concrete", so that's it: you are already done with the floor. Nothing else needed. Concrete makes a great acoustic floor. You might want to stain it for aesthetic reasons, but you don't have enough budget to do much else with it, and bare concrete is good just like it is.
6) I'm thinking just one door for entrance. Options?
That can work, but once again it all depends on the overall design, your goals, and other things. A studio isn't a bunch of independent things attached to each other: it is a system. Each part affects all the other parts, and they all work together in unison to achieve your goal. So it might be possible to go with only one door, depending on yoru isolation needs, but it is far more common to have two "back to back" doors, as Brien mentioned.
7) Is there anyone locally here in Portland who would supervise for a modest fee?
Like Brien said: You don't necessarily need someone locally. If you post your detailed designs here before you start, plus plenty of photos of what you are doing at each stage of the build, then we can "supervise" form where we are, spread all around the planet. The internet is a wonderful tool!
8 ) Would install leave any marks?
On you, on the house, or on your bank account? :) Seriously, if "not damaging the existing structure at all" is one of your goals, then that can be considered in your design goals. It MIGHT be possible to minimize marking existing structures, but it would most likely make things more difficult, more expensive, and less effective at isolating.
9) Is soffet mounting speakers too difficult and costly for a first time non-permanent project?
It isn't all that hard to soffit mount, and you can make a "modular" set up for that, if you design and build carefully, so that it can be dissembled and taken out easily. Once again, that adds to the design complexity, and the cost, but the benefits of soffit mounting are well worthwhile. - Stuart -

one thought then, if isolation isn't the priority - maybe do a room within a room as if finishing the garage - a set of 2x4 walls on the inside all around make the window and garage door openings framed then add another set of framing there that could be removed later. use pink insulation between the walls. seal the exterior walls and roof. over the window put in a 3/4" plywood and 2x drywall plug (you'll build something similar inside). on the ceiling - you might sister up the joists w 2x6 and using rc-2 or isolation clips and hat channel/furring strips put in your ceiling. i'd think 2x 5/8" drywall all around, or 1/2" x2 all around if thats more in the budget, worst case, one layer of 5/8". on the garage door - take it down along with all the brackets and store it all. build an exterior wall within that frame and put in a decent door (or not) and paint it to mimic the garage door. for the floor - build some isolation pads for the drums, amps, etc. for treatments - you're renting - plan on taking it with you so build treatments as modular components - soffit boxes etc as desired. worst case - start with plan old cloth covered absorption scattered around and some reasonable symmetry where you plan to mix. build up later when you have more budget. maybe if the isolation is handled as a "upgrade" to a finished garage, you could get the landlord to chip in some $? this could help offset some of the money for additional treatments.
