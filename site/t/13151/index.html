cporro10 June 2009#2
1. Can I put another layer of 1/2 drywall on the 1/2 drywall already there between the joists or I MUST put 5/8 drywall"
it's just about mass. 5/8th is more massive so that's why it's being used. more mass same amount of work. what you do need to think about is the load you are adding. drywall is heavy and you don't want to overload the ceiling. try and find someone who knows about structural engineering.
2. Is there a good alternative to acoustic caulk as aparently it is such a mess to work with?
all caulk is a mess imho. the idea with acoustic caulk is that it maintains the seal. this seal is very important.
3. Is it good to use drywall screws for both layers of drywall or not?
i guess it would be ok so long as there is something on the other side to support the drywall. for me i can't do it because i'll screw into the roof or siding. it takes a while to understand this stuff. it may be a good idea to spend more time studying the concepts and doing the construction later. save money and time.

You didn't mention what that floor is made of, but assuming that what you are adding (2 layers of drywall) is going to double the mass of that floor, then all that will get you is roughly 6 dB of extra isolation, additional to what you had before. Is it worth the effort?
1. Can I put another layer of 1/2 drywall on the 1/2 drywall already there between the joists or I MUST put 5/8 drywall"
You can if you want, but like cporro said, more mass will get you more isolation. It would have been better to go with two layers of 5/8, but you already have that layer of 1/2 up there, so the best you can do is to add a layer of 5/8 now. But once again, are you sure that the limited additional isolation is worthwhile? I don't want to sound like a wet blanket, but unless you can put a proper ceiling in there and decouple it in some way, you are stuck with mass law, and mass law isn't very encouraging. Mass law says that each time you double the mass, you get another 6 dB of isolation. You don't say how much isolation you have at present or how much more you need, but 6 dB isn't much. Is 6 dB of additional isolation good enough for your needs? And I'm still assuming that a total of 1 1/8" of drywall is enough to double the mass that was up there already: If not, then it won't even be 6 dB. Also (and once again I'm just echoing and emphasizing what cporro already said): Are you certain that the structure can handle all that extra mass? From your dimensions, that's something like 140 square feet of drywall you are adding up there. As near as I can figure it, 140 sq.ft of 1/2" is about 300 lbs (roughly), plus 140 sq.ft. of 5/8" is roughly 400 lbs. That's a total of 700 lbs of mass that you are adding to your floor, not including the caulk, insulation, cloth, trim, and other stuff. Is the floor capable of safely supporting that? Did you check with a structural engineer? If not, then maybe you should. You might be fine, but you also might not. Only a structural engineer can tell you for sure. - Stuart -

Hate to tell you this - but that IS a wood girder (a Beam is technically steel) it is a structural member that carries transversly loaded floor joist . In the case you describe - with the joists being installed in 2 pieces (joints breaking over the girder) the load is actually 1/2 of the entire floor load being carried by the member - and typically (although i am not there I can picture the construction) there is a wall installed directly above the girder - this is a bearing wall - the joist for the floor above that would typically bear on that wall - (although it is always possible that a post is installed directly above that post to transmit the load above) and the girder is now carrying 1/2 of the upper floor as well PLUS the ceiling load above that (I am assuming that your landlord's apartment is on the top floor). Removal of that member without having this analyzed by a structural engineer is insane - the 2x6 member is not capable of carrying the load of a post - it will fail (eventually) under bending if not under compression - but it will eventually fail. There is a reason builders install posts where they do - it is all determined by math due to load - for your landlord to do what he has done is not only wrong in principle - it is a flagrant code violation - and if there was ever the catastrophe X is referring to - he could face criminal charges if anyone were hurt or killed. Put the column back until you can have this assessed by a real structural engineer - someone who can provide you with a signed and sealed stamped letter certifying that this is structurally acceptable. The cost would only be 2 or 3 hundred dollars for a simple field visit and letter. Rod
