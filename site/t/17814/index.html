Hi "somalade", and Welcome! :) You have an "interesting" shaped space there. But it does seem to be big enough that you should be able to get it sounding nice.
• Floor is timber construction and is carpeted.
You say that you don't want to change that, but you should be aware that the carpet is probably a large part of the problem with your room acoustics. Carpet is selectively absorbent, in a random and unknown way. But worse than that, it does exactly the opposite of what you need for a small room. Carpet absorbs high frequencies very well, mids to a certain extent, and lows not at all. But you need the reverse: You need to absorb huge amounts of low frequencies, quite a bit of mids, and no highs. So the carpet is fighting your efforts, and reversing the effect of other treatment. You will therefore need to treat the room a whole lot more if you insist on keeping your carpet, and some of that treatment might be rather unusual, as it will have to counteract what the carpet is doing.
I need to try and achieve a balance between the ideal acoustics for a control room and a live room but would prefer to err on the control room side so I can create translatable mixes.
Have you considered make the acoustics variable? There are several things you could do to make it possible to change the room from a more neutral environment for critical listening / mixing / mastering, to a more live or even dead sound for tracking.
• My monitors are Mackie HR824s. I currently have these sitting on my desk but would prefer to move them a bit further away (and wider apart). I understand these shouldn’t be soffit mounted, so am looking at building some stands out of 100mm di. PVC pipe filled with sand.
Definitely! That was one thing I noticed in the photo that needs fixing. Those are fairly big monitors, and it definitely looks like you have your head too close to them. Even though "near field" and "far field" are much more marketing hype than real definable technical terms, there still is the issue that you need a certain amount of distance between the speaker and your head for the sound emitted by the tweeter and the sound emitted by the woofer to merge into a more or less even sound field. To me, it looks like your head is not far enough away for that to be the case. I tried to find a recommendation from Mackie for that, but came up empty. But you'd probably need at least 5 or 6 feet between your ears and the speakers. So massively heavy stands right up against the front wall seems like the way to go.

OK, I think you have me convinced that it would be worth it removing the carpet.... part of the reason for keeping it was so allow me to keep the ceiling as is without adding absorption.
One of the reasons why the vast majority of pro studios have hard floors and soft ceilings instead of the other way around, is psycho-acoustics: the way we hear, and the way our ears and brains interpret sounds. We are accustomed to always be the same height above the floor, our entire lives, simply due to how tall we are. Our brains are well adapted to dealing with reflections coming off the floor, but not so well adapted to reflections coming off the ceiling, since ceiling height changes everywhere we go. So to our brain, everything sounds "right" when the floor is hard and the ceiling soft.
So, basically wanting advice on where to start with planning the acoustic treatment (assuming the fundamental concept is OK)... Am thinking I should buy some rigid fibre glass, make some corner bass traps, measure the impact and go from there?
There's a sort of "standard" method for treating rooms, which goes something like this: First, set up your speaker geometry correctly, such that the speakers are either flush mounted in soffits (best choice), or up tight against the front wall (2nd best), or at least 1.5m away from any wall (3rd best, but really hard to do in most rooms). The speakers should be mounted on massive, heavy, solid stands (never on teh console bridge, shleves or the desk), and should be decoupled with a suitable rubber pad (Sorbothane, neoprene, EPDM, etc), such that the acoustic axes ends up being 1.2m above the floor. Set up your chair so that your ears will be 38% back in the room (38% of the distance between the front and rear walls). Set up your speakers about 1 to 2 m apart, but also well away form the side walls, and angle them inwards at 30°, such that they are aimed at your ears. The acoustic axes will intersect a few inches behind the back of your head. Now set up thick absorption (4") on all of your first reflection points (both side walls plus the ceiling), as well as between the speaker and the front wall. Put thick absorption on the rear wall (6", spaced 6" away), and build large superchunk bass traps in as many of the corners of the room as you can. Now measure the room response to see how it is doing, in order to decide on the next round of treatment. Of course, you should measure the room response before you start installing any treatment, so that you have a baseline against which you can compare later measurements, to see what changed. That's the basic idea. - Stuart -
