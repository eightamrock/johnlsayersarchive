sharward16 September 2006#6
Perhaps a construction expert will disagree or clarify this, but here is my understanding. Each layer of overhead drywall should be installed on wood joists/trusses using ring shank nails or course threaded drywall screws (I like screws better), or on steel joists/channel using fine threaded drywall screws. Minimum screw penetration in wood studs is 3/4" of full diameter and minimum screw penetration in steel studs is 1/4" of full diameter. I'm not sure of the minimum penetration for ceilings screws, but Rod likes 1 3/8" as a bare minimum. It is not appropriate to attach one layer of drywall using the above method and then to attach a second layer of drywall to the first layer using adhesive. This does two things: increases the load on the screws of the first layer (not safe); and changes the resonant properties of the drywall (in a "not good way" either) by joining them into a single, thicker panel. The only way I see making it work safely is to double the number of fasteners in the first layer... But then where is the time or cost savings? :roll: I'm also pretty sure that you need at least two edges of a panel to be attached to studs or joists -- that using a construction adhesive along the edge to keep it from "flapping" is not appropriate. In other words, the subsequent layers are fastened in exactly the same fashion as the first layer, with one axis' edges always lining up over a stud/joist/truss/channel. I also recall reading somewhere that drywall does provide some level of shear protection on walls, so to say that drywall is not considered "structural" like plywood may not be completely accurate -- or at least there's more to that story than can be covered here. I know there are building codes that govern this, and although I'm reasonably sure the above accurately covers aspects of them, always consult your local building department to be sure you're in compliance (a.k.a., my standard disclaimer ;-)). For my outer leaf ceiling, I scrapped my plan to tear down the existing ceiling and hat channel and put up new, stronger joists able to support 3 or 4 layers of drywall, and instead installed one extra layer of drywall on the existing ceiling using a Green Glue sandwich. However, in doing so I decided against installing the second layer onto the existing hat channel, as that would have doubled the load on that channel, and especially the 24" o.c. screws that hold the hat channel up! :shock: So, with my building department's approval, I fastened the second layer directly into the bottom chord of the trusses. Most of this is already in the reference section and/or on other threads, hopefully an easier read for Danish friend AND for the shear joy of typing. :twisted: Applying Green Glue, acoustic caulk/sealant & drywall mud with tape (joint compound): 1st layer of drywall: 1. Mud and tape all seams of drywall pieces, that is where two pieces meet that you're putting on the same wall, same layer. Drywall (in US at least) is tapered on edges for tape and mud, have to fill in the tapered portion with something or you'll have a small AIR pocket in middle of drywall. 2. On perimeter of drywall (all corners to other walls, ceiling & floor) use backer rod and acoustic sealant. Some skip the backer rod which is not recommended. 2nd layer of drywall: 1. Apply green glue to back of drywall, put it on wall against 1st layer of drywall, make sure to get it fully fastened within 15 Min's of applying green glue (something I almost screwed up, had to run like crazy when I encountered a problem). Fastening the 2nd layer of drywall will squish , spread the green glue between the two layers evenly. 2. repeat same steps for 1st layer of drywall. Finished corners: you can rake back the caulk enough so that you can apply mud and tape in order to make it look like a regular corner of wall when painted... it'll be a pretty corner depending on your drywall skills. ALSO see reference section for more details on how to drywall, caulk etc... AND www.greengluecompany.com Marc
