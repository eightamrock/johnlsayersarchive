The carport and permitted basement areas are about another 1000 sq ft, but only have 7.5 ft ceilings.
OK, so that's clearly the big issue you are concerned about. 1000 sq ft is great, but 7.5 feet is not much to work with, and as you certainly know by know, sound needs height to sound good. Most instruments need space, but drums, especially, need height. Drums is an unusual case, since you need to have mics overhead. With a low ceiling, the mics are practically at the ceiling, or real close, and therefore picking up major reflections, with very short time delays, which implies comb filtering, phase cancellation, and all those other nasty things. There isn't much you an do about that with treatment, either. You can't make the ceiling "go away" enough to be useful with such a low ceiling. So I'd say that "respectable sounding acoustic drums" is probably not something you'll be able to do in there. However, vocals is a possibility, and so are other instruments. As you say, it won't be world class, but it also does not have to be terrible.
The floors are concrete throughout.
That's good. But speaking of floors, have you considered the possibility of going DOWN? Digging your floor down 2 or 3 feet deeper would make a world of difference! Yeah, it sounds scary, and it sure wont be cheap, but it really might be worth your while talking to a structural engineer and an inspector, to see if this is physically feasible, and legally possible. There's a good chance that you would be able to dig out at least a section of that 1000 sq ft, drop it down a good bit, pour a new floor (with footings, obviously) and tie that all in to the rest of the building. It's worth a try: the worst they can say is "no way", but if they say "yes" then you have a really good chance of meeting ALL your goals, including the drums.
I am hoping that isolation won't be a huge issue.
The best way to find out if your hopes are realistic, is to get yourself a sound level meter and measure how loud you are. In other words, in a typical recording session that you do today, take readings of just how loud the instruments are (set the meter to "C" weighting, slow response): Then check your ambient levels in the neighborhood at 3 AM (or whatever time of day is quietest), and also check your municipal regulations on noise. Subtract the smallest number (ambient or legal limit) from the biggest number (in your recording session), and the result is your answer: how much isolation you need: Based on that, you can check the literature to see what types of materials and techniques will get you that number, then choose one that fits your budget, skill set and building code.
Currently no heat in the basement, or air flow other than that provided by the vents/windows.
HVAC is a large and very important part of studios, and often overlooked. Basically, your studio will involve a double-hermetic seal all around you, wrapped in multiple layers of very thick, very efficient insulation. Think of it like two very thick, sealed plastic bags, one inside the other, each wrapped in thick blankets... Obviously, it will get very unpleasant in there, very fast. HVAC is a must. So you need to figure that into your budget and plans too: either a ducted system with an air handler somewhere, or a mini-split unit for each room, plus ducting for fresh air and stale air. In either case, you'll also need silencer boxes.

There's a mix of that. Some start out trying to go the DIY route, then realize they are out of their depth and hire a designer. The trouble is, many of the folks who do that, then stop posting on their threads, so you never know how it turned out! Some do, though, and that's great. But most of the folks here who have successfully completed their studios, or are in the process of doing so, have taken the time to learn the basics of acoustics, and the basics of studio design, then do it themselves, with the help of the other forum members. I really can't give you a number, and say that zz.z% hire designers and yy.y% go totally DIY. It would be interesting to have that statistic!
I'm handy enough for most "home improvement" tasks, but I'm certainly no expert on acoustics, or the design of acoustic spaces.
It's not that hard to learn, although it does take time. Figure on a couple of months, at least, to learn enough to get a good start on your design, then a couple more to actually do the design, and iron out the kinks in it. You probably can do it, if you really want to, and if you have the time. But for some builders "time" is the issue, and they prefer to hire someone to do the design for them, so they can shorten the cycle a bit. There's also the issue of money: obviously, designers don't work for free! So part of your budget will have to go to his fees, but really, that is money very well spent. There have been a few cases, right here on the forum, of people who tried to save on designers fees, did something silly, and then wasted a lot of time and money undoing it and re-doing it. Probably cost them more in wasted materials and labor than the designer's fee. Plus, a good designer can save you money by optimizing the design, and not over-designing or under-designing. Over designing means you buy and install more materials than you really needed, so you wasted money. Under-designing means you didn't put enough in, so you have to go out and buy more (after the place is finished, and didn't meet your goals), then modify it. Both of those are big money wasters. So purely from that point of view, paying someone to design it for you is money well spent, and might actually save you money in the long run.
Clearly this is a forum which is dedicated largely to spreading critical information regarding *how to build a studio yourself*...so I'm wondering how people feel there is still real value in hiring professional consultants and designers
Personally, I think there is value in hiring someone, if you realize that you are totally out of your depth and unlikely to be able to learn enough, soon enough. On the other hand, if you have time and are reasonably smart, then most people probably can figure it out. But that goes back to the "time" issue... Time to learn, time to design, time to check the design, time to compare it with other designs, etc.
I think the other threshold issue I need to tackle is related to the HVAC--or more generally, ANY necessary action item that will require a permit.
That's smart: Any job you don't know hoe to do, or don't feel comfortable doing, or lack the qualifications to do, should be farmed out, most definitely. So things like structural issues, HVAC, electrical, glazing, pouring concrete, and other similar things might well need professional input. But you might still be able to do the basic design for those things yourself, and then pay the expert to take a look at that and correct your errors. It all depends on how involved you want to be. So I guess what I'm saying here, is that everyone is different: some can handle everything themselves, some can handle part of it, some just want to hire a guy to do it all. The forum is for all three, but mostly for the first two: There's a huge amount of very solid information on this forum, and a lot of good people who can help, including some world-class designers, such as John himself (obviously!). So here on the forum, you'll should (hopefully) get the same level of advice and knowledge as you would from a designer, and you get it for free! But you have to do most of the drudgery work yourself, and you might not get the answer you need, when you need it, since the moderators and members here are all volunteers, and help out when they can. Anyway, check your PMs, and let me know if you got that. :) - Stuart -
