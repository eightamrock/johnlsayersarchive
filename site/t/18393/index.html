Johnnie26 June 2013#7
Hey JRH! You take criticism well! Good job. I see what your intent was, and am glad you can see how it came across. It sounds like you definitely have enough budget to get something functional working, and you've already mentioned that you are thinking of staging the design. Let's lower the scope a bit and revise to a goal achievable with the current budget. If you've got 20k available, you've definitely got enough to do a reasonable control room and recording area with iso booths. I'm thinking is that the plan is a little complex at the moment. I'm definitely NOT a professional designer, but I have recorded in a number of different studios, and I remember some components that made things awkward, so I'm looking at it from that perspective. I'm a big believer in keeping pathways for access to rooms simple. It's also awfully nice to have a place for people to congregate that ISN"T the control room. You've got an entry to the building in the upper left hand corner. I don't think you want your entry to go into a live recording room. That seems likely to end up with an eventual intrusion into a recording. It's also going to be tough if there's no access to the control room except by going into the recording areas. It's probably also not up to code. Most places I can think of are going to require at least 2 entries and exits. That ended up being an issue with my own initial plan. What if you come into a small lobby/gathering area in that upper left hand corner. (sort the second exit out later) What if you Re-orient your control room plan so that it's symmetrically reversed. It's up on top instead of being on the bottom. This would give you a traffic pattern where people come in, enter a lobby area, and then can enter the control room. They can also step out of the control room into a lobby. It's also nice to be able to get to recording areas without passing through the (often crowded) control room. (the goal here would be that while you're "staging", you wouldn't have people walking through unbuilt areas. They'd enter right into function spaces, and you could just expand into the unbuilt stuff later. Keep your centralized control room plan, but I'd reduce the doors into/out of it. Right now your control room is your major center of traffic flow, and I don't think that's really necessary. It seems more logical to me to have the doors on the iso booths open into the live room, rather than daisy chain back into the control room. If I'm a singer having just finished a take and wanting to relax with a musician, it's nicer to just step directly into the live room. It'd feel frustrating to have to do a big U traffic pattern back to the control room, and then back to the live room. It'd make me feel that much more disconnected from my band mates. So along the top, we'd have lobby on the upper left, control room on upper middle, now place your current bottom left corner into the new upper right corner. Treat your "piano room" as the primary recording area until you can afford to do the whole space. I don't think you need so many entrances and exits into that recording area. As an example, the current piano room has 2 separate double door entries to the eventual drum room. I'm willing to bet that only one is really going to be used much. Why not just do one, and if you want the extra visibility, put a window in on the other space? Also, do you really need so many different individual booths? I note that all your doors are single doors and all your walls are single walls. If you want isolation, you're going to need to plan on decoupling those spaces from each other, which is going to mean double walls/ceiling with air gaps. If you want single doors, they'll need to be specially designed for that... might be easier to double the doors though they'll still need the usual stuff (closing mechanisms, air seals, etc). Also, have you looked at the sizing on your booths once you do that? are they still ok? Do you really need so many booths? As a quick caution, I haven't really thought about what those changes would do to your eventual other half of the building, so it's good to think through that bit too, even if it's not happening right now. It does look there's some room to spare there though :)

HI there JRH, and welcome! I just wanted to add a bit more detail to what John and Johnnie already said: To give you a rough idea of what it would actually cost to get this built by a contractor (not including the actual design, permits, etc.), call up a couple of local contractors and ask them for their standard basic flat rate per square foot for converting a garage into a home theater or living room. Add about 20% to that, and you have a realistic rough idea of the real costs involved, in YOUR area, which might be very different from what it costs someone else who lives in a different area. That's the best way of getting a handle on costs. Based on that, and your available budget, you can figure out how many square feet you can afford to build right now, and how much will have to wait for later, as funds allow. OK, so that's the contractor's rate. If you plan to do all the work yourself, and already have all the tools you'll need (and the expertise to use them), then of course it will be quite a bit less expensive, since a very large a chunk of the cost of hiring a contractor is labor, and another chunk is his markup on materials. So your dollar goes a lot further if you do the build yourself. But the build also goes a LOT slower.... So based on all of the above, you can estimate how many square feet you can reasonably expect to do, then come up with a decision on which room is most important and concentrate on building that. However, that's the build: Regardless of which order you build the studio rooms, you MUST have everything fully designed up front, before you even get your first 2x4 delivered. The entire plan must be totally complete, since all the parts of a studio affect each other, and changing an apparently minor thing in one place really can turn out to have major implications in another place. Believe me, when I'm designing studios, I spend most of my time doing just that: juggling the details of all the rooms at once, to try to come up with an optimal compromise. So I urge you not to make the mistake of designing and building just one room, then trying to design the others around it later. You have a very nice sized space to work with, and a good base, but there's an awful lot of things that need to be taken into account when designing your studio, so I'd suggest that you should take the decision now on if you are going to do it all yourself (design included), or if you are going to hire people to help you. Designing and building yourself is very rewarding, and you can save a lot of money like that, but it is also very slow. Just learning enough about acoustics and design can take you a couple of months (realistically) then actually doing the design can take you a couple more (at least). Then the build process can take you many, many more months on top of that. If you take a look at threads here on the board by people who have already finished their studios then you'll get a better feeling for what is involved, how long it takes, and what you can realistically expect as the out come, form both the DIY approach and also from the contractor approach. One more thing that's very important: Designing and building a studio is nothing at all like designing and building a house. Same materials, same tools, but very different techniques, and many "gotchas" hiding in unexpected places. Many simple procedures that the contractor is used to doing for houses, shops, offices, etc. simply cannot be used for studio construction, so it is important to find a contractor who has build studios before (successfully!), or one who is very willing to learn to do things differently. One other piece of advice: don't rush! It takes time to get it right, and getting it wrong can be frustrating, not too mention expensive. As you work your way around the various build threads on the forum you'll mind more than a few by people who rushed ahead before they were ready, or got contractors who didn't know what they were doing, and then had to stop, dismantle things, take several steps back, and re-do things the right way. Some didn't bother taking advice from the experts here, and curiously their threads sort of fade away long before the place was finished. On the other hand, those who did take the advice generally ended up completing their studios, very successfully. So that's probably the first big decision you need to make: design and build the entire place yourself, or hire someone to do it for you? Or at least to do parts of it for you (the parts that you don't feel confident doing yourself, or don't have the time or expertise to do yourself). That said, I agree with Johnnie that your budget can probably be stretched to do the control room, and maybe a booth or two, but a lot depends on how much isolation you need. That's generally the very first technical question that needs to be answered in numbers: "How loud are you (in decibels)?" and "How quite do you need that to be on the other side?". A very large part of the entire design depends on answering those two questions accurately. The materials and the construction techniques for building the rooms depend on those numbers, to a very large extent. On your layout: I agree with Johnnie here too: the basic concept is OK; but there's a lot that can be done to improve it, from the practical point of view (not just acoustics). For example, I would switch around "booth 2" with the machine room, so you can have a direct line of sight view into the booth: you don't need to make visual contact with the equipment racks, but you very likely do need visual contact with the talent you are recording! Same for booth 3: move it forwards to some place where you can get sight lines. I would also not have one booth leading into another booth: that's a nightmare, both acoustically and practically. Your CR shape is also questionable: there are better ways to shape it, for good acoustics. It's not bad, but could be improved. My other comment is on Doors. You are spending a large fortune on those! As Johnnie pointed out, each door you show there should actually be two doors, back to back, in order to have decent isolation between rooms. Considering that each door will cost you a few hundred dollars to build yourself (or a thousand or so to buy), you are blowing your entire budget on doors! I count 25 doors on your diagram, meaning there will be 50 in total. Even if you can do those for just 200 bucks each (VERY unlikely: just the seals, hinges and closers will cost that), then you'd be spending 10 grand on them. So I would greatly reduce the number of doors. you don't need that many! The other issue with doors is that you are showing several double-doors that meet each other in the middle: that's impractical for a studio, as there is no reliable way to get them to seal properly against each other in the middle, unless you add a center stile: But if you do that, then you defeat the purpose of the double door anyway... So I would definitely get rid of all of those, and replace them with single doors. Make them wider if you need to get equipment in and outs, but don't do double doors. You'll regret it if you do. (That said, it is possible to do double doors for a studio: just really, really hard to get right, and really expensive....)

That isn't a lot, unless you are doing everything yourself (design and construction). Well, you're gonna kick yourself here, but when you do get it into 3D, that's when you'll realize that you have to "un-commit" several things an re-do them. It really is amazing how many things just leap out at you in 3D that you never even notices in 2D drawings. Plus, when you get down to planning the actual acoustic layout and treatment of the rooms, 3D is an absolute necessity: Sound moves in 3D, not 2D... :) So I'd suggest putting it into 3D already, even though it will take some time and then be slower to change things, but it really is worthwhile doing that, as it gives you an entirely different perspective on things. - Stuart -
