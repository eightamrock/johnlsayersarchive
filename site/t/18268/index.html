I’d obviously like it sound proofed (as well as insulated etc) and will look to do the basics in this sense, however how far do I need to go?
That depends on two things, and you need to put numbers to them. Actually four things: 1) How loud are you? Measure the level of a typical loud session, using a proper sound level meter. 2) How quiet do you need to be? Measure the typical ambient level outside, at the time of day you would normally work in the studio. 3) How loud are things around you? Worst case: Thunder, rain, wind, helicopters, aircraft, cars, trains, TV, radio, animals, etc. They all make noises that you do not want to get inside your studio, so try to measure how loud they can be, peak. 4) How quiet do you want it to be inside your studio? Pick a nice quiet time of day inside a nice quiet room some place, that you find to be at a good level where you can work well. Measure that level. Measure all of the above with "C" weighting and "Slow" response on the meter. Now find the biggest of those four numbers, and the smallest. Subtract the smallest form the biggest. The difference is how much isolation ("soundproofing") you need. This is the only reliable way of determining your isolation needs: guessing is not the solution!
to ensure an accurate/flat response in the room itself.
Don't set your goals too ambitiously! "accurate" response is do-able and realistic, but "flat response" not so much. You need a pretty large room, and good treatment to be able to get truly flat response. A realistic goal for a typical home studio in a small room is more like "+/- 10dB, across the normal range of the speakers". A more ambitious (but possibly still attainable) goal would be "+/- 6 dB, from 40 Hz to 20 kHz". The smaller the room, the harder it is to get "flat" response, simply due to the nature of the room.
The big question is, am I ever going to get a good mixing environment in such a space?
You sure can! John has designed some pretty small studios that turned out great. Yours won't be the next Abbey Road, of course, but it can still be good. And if your only choices are "reasonably good but not fantastic" and "no studio at all", I reckon I'd go with Option A every time! :)

I've actually been looking at both Adam's and Genelics. I'm not sure i'll be able to afford the sub straight away, a quick question. Would you softfit the sub as well? I take it you would?
some people suggest soffit-mounting subs, but I'm not too convinced of the benefits of doing that, for a couple of reasons. One of those is that subs, by nature, are omnidirectional, so trying to "direct" that means that all you accomplish is reducing the level by 6dB :shock: Half of the power stays behind the soffit, instead of going into the room. A soffit would have to be huge to be able to actually "focus" such long waves. Another reason is simply that most good subs are ported, and often that is at the rear, or underneath. So soffit mounting means that you are blocking the port form the room. And finally, you can often improve the bass response of the room by moving the sub around a bit, to find the position where it causes the least problems and has the smoothest response, but you can't do that if it is soffited. So I'm not a big fan of soffit mounting subs. On the other hand, I'm a MAJOR fan of soffit mounting the mains! :) Before you decide on your speakers, go find some dealer that has both Adams and Genelecs, and a good show room where you can listen to both in a decent environment. Take along some of your own music, and some commercial CD's that you know really well, and had listened to in many different places, and do a comparison. The big advantage that you'll find on the Adam A7X or A8X is the high end: it is smooth, has fantastic off-axis response, and extends way up beyond anything else, to something amazing like 35 kHz or so. That means that the tweeters are not struggling in the least at 20 Khz, which is generally considered the upper limit of human hearing. Most lesser speakers can get to 20 k, but they really have to struggle to do that. Think of it like taking your average family sedan to the race track: The specs probably say that it has a top speed of 120 MPH, and it probably actually is able to hit 120 MPH if you really push it, but it will be screaming and groaning and shaking and shuddering and totally stressed out to do that, and won't do it well. But try that in a Ferrari or Lamborghini, and 120 MPH is a piece of cake: it can do that in its sleep, with one wheel and two cylinders tied behind its back! Simply because it is designed to go so much faster. Same with the speakers: the Adams can get to 20 kHz while taking a nap, since they are design to go some much higher, but most normal tweeters are having a hard time reproducing 20 kHz, since that is pretty much their limit. OK, please excuse the shameless rant in favor of Adams, but I just love these things! Those ART tweeters are just so damn impressive that it's hard to ignore their advantages. Streets ahead of most of their competitors.

Soundman20207 April 2013#7
In respect of going deeper and giving myself more height, will i mess up my ratios as i can't go sufficetly wide enough for such height?
Not really: sound waves don't have a sense of direction: they don't know "up" from "left", so acoustically speaking you can swap the "height" dimension in calculating your ratio with the "width" dimension. You can't really do the same with "length", though, for a different reason: you need the back wall as far away as possible from your head and the speakers, to get the longest possible time delay between direct sound and reflected sound, hitting your ears. This is a psycho-acoustic effect (related to the way our brains perceive sound), not a purely acoustic effect (the way sound interacts with the room), so it's not quite he same. But swapping "width" for "height" in the room ratio equations is perfectly valid, as long as the room isn't so narrow that it starts causing other psycho-acoustic effects, or makes it impossible to position the speakers correctly. In your case, that shouldn't be a problem, since you should be able to get at least 2m of width. - Stuart -

Soundman202022 April 2013#9
In regards to bass traps, how do peopel feel about this design
That's a basic superchunk. Very effective down to low frequencies, provided that you have enough of them, in the right locations, and make them big enough. Ideal is about 36" across the front face (the diagonal part, facing the room), but never less than about 24". If you look around this forum, there are LOTS of designs and examples of DIY acoustic treatment: that's what the forum is all about! :) However, you seem to be jumping the gun somewhat! :shock: Acoustic treatment is a long way down the line for you at this point! You should be thinking about the actual design of the studio building right now: isolation shell, size, shape, ratio, volume, target isolation, materials and techniques, etc. All of that will have an influence on how the studio needs to be treated in the end. There's not much point in planning the treatment when you don't even have the basic concept in place yet. Studio design is a process, where you should follow a series of steps that will lead you to the right decisions and best design for the available space. Trying to jump forward is not going to save you any time (or money) later, as you'll only need to re-do that part of the design anyway, when the time comes. I would suggest that it would be better to start put by determining what your isolation requirements are, in decibels, then look at techniques and materials that will get you to that level, on your budget, then take those into account in designing your basic building shell. With that in place, you can then decide on the shape and dimensions of the actual room interior. And based on THAT you can start figuring out treatment. - Stuart -
