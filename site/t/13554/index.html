Hi Ducharme. A couple of comments that might be worth something:
I will be installing the studs for the outside wall in the next couple of weeks if all goes well.
I'm wondering why you need studs on your outer wall? From the diagram, you seem to have a rather thick brick or concrete wall around at least two sides of the room: Or is that large grey "L" shaped block something else? I'm just assuming that it is concrete. If it is, then you don't really need to do anything more to it, probably. Putting studs and drywall up against it won't do much for you, except eat into your budget! (If it isn't concrete, then ignore my remark...)
The main untreated live room has the windows heavily closed and insulated for jam sessions and tracking we can't get away with loud stuff past about 10pm.
What kind of instruments are you playing in there? A drum kit will put out 115 dB all by itself without much trouble. Add in a loud bass player, extreme electric guitar, etc., and you could be approaching 120 dB. Or do you play quieter instruments?
As for the studio I think I mix at about 80db, but I plan to get a DB meter and keep it on from now on.
You might be surprised at just how loud you are when you mix! I was, teh first time I measured. Your pair of JBL LSR4328's can put out 115 dB peak between them, and their sensitivity is listed at 96 dB, so you might find that you are mixing at a higher level than you imagine. 80 dB isn't all that loud for a CR. Once you get your meter, you might find that you are closer to 90 dB.
I believe that soffit mounting my JBL LSR4328 monitors with Johns method with hard surface on top and hangers below should help in a small space like mine.
Yep! I'm a fan of soffit mounting. Some others here aren't, but I think it's a great idea. However, you need the "hard surface" all around the speaker, not just "on top" of it. (I think that's what you meant to say, but its a bit ambiguous, so I'm just making sure.) The hard surface (baffle) needs to be rigid and massive (as you already know if you have been getting into Tom Hidley's designs!) and is supposed to simulate an infinite baffle. So it needs to be as large as possible. In John's designs, the hangers normally go BEHIND the lower portion of the baffle, but the baffle is still there, nearly down to the floor. Also, from your diagram it seems that your speakers are not centered in their baffles: they should be. You want roughly the same amount of "hard surface" on each side of your speaker, and it doesn't look like that from your diagram. You can move them a bit further out (towards the walls). That will also help with your stereo image. If that messes up your axis intersection point, then you can angle the soffits and speakers so that the axes still meet in the right place. I didn't measure it, but to me it seems that your ears are a bit further back than the 38% mark, and maybe close to the 50% mark? Or is that just my fading eyesight playing tricks on me again?
I need to have the door on the back wall and the side door to the vocal booth.
I don't see the rear door as being a major issue, except that it takes up the space where you could have nice comfy couch to snooze on! :) And it puts a hard reflective surface right behind the mix position, but that can be treated. However, your door to the booth might be an issue: It seems to be right at the first reflection point for your right speaker. Any chance you can move it rearward a couple of feet?
Both leafs will have two layers of sheetrock 1/2 and I may use sheetblock in between them
Save your money and forget the sheetblock: Go with 2 layers of 5/8" sheetrock instead: Mass is mass, and the sound waves really don't care how much you paid for the mass, so just put the cheapest mass in that you can get (within reason!). It doesn't come much cheaper than plain old sheetrock, and 5/8 beats 1/2.
The outside leaf will have studs and insulation along the basement cement walls
So the walls are cement! Then I don't see why you need those studs. If it is just to keep the 703 in place, then there are other, cheaper ways of doing that... :)
The inside leaf is inside out and supports the ceiling on the walls with about 1" clearance to the outside leaf ceiling.
1 inch isn't much if you need good isolation! Any chance you can get yourself a bigger air gap up there? The normal recommendation is at least 4 inches between facing inner-leaf and outer-leaf surfaces. Reducing the air gap reduces isolation. What is the existing ceiling made of ? Is that just the floor of a room above? How much isolation do you need to that room? When you say 1 inch, do you mean from the top of the sheetrock on your inside-out ceiling, up the the bottom of the joists on the floor above? If so then ignore the above comments: you are OK as your air gap is the joist space in that case, which is going to be much more than 4 inches anyway, I expect.
Question 2: I planned to use slats on the vocal booth door and the same on the other side, is this ok or should it just be broadband absorption?
If it really is at the FRP, then absorption, at least for the guilty section.
Question 3: Hangers or super chucks for the back wall.
Yes! :) John usually recommends hangers, I think. But on the other hand, superchunks are easy to build, and also work well. If you have the time, I'd go with hangers.
Question 5: The soffit design, I have seen several soffit designs that have a smaller footprint on the floor than the ceiling and give the speakers a slight angle. Would that help to raise the speakers higher and not conflict with a pair of large computer screens on a small desk I have to plan for?
Yep! You can raise your speakers if you need to, then tilt them forwards (and the entire baffle along with them), so that the acoustic axes still meet just behind your ears, which should be somewhere around the 38% mark. You can tilt them down by as much as 10 degrees (some say 15) if you need to, but watch out! You might end up creating new reflections on your desk surface if you go too high and tilt too much... Good luck with your build! - Stuart -

The L shaped gray area is the outside cement basement walls.
Cool! Then you only need to add an inner leaf!
In this case is the 38% point measurement from the inside leaf or from the center of the soffit wall?
Inner leaf surface (unless the center section of the soffit is going to be reflective, solid and massive). But the 38% "rule" is just a guideline anyway: it's not carved in stone. From what I've read, many engineers prefer a position a bit closer to the front wall. And in any event, just moving your head a bit in a small room will take you across several percentage points! 38% gets you in the ballpark, but then your ears will find the perfect sweet spot. So I wouldn't sweat it too much. Also, don't forget that it is your EARS that are supposed to be at 38%, and NOT the intersection point. For most room / speaker geometry layouts, the intersection of the speaker axes will be at a point about 15 - 20 cm or so behind your ears (unless you have a very unusual shaped head!). But once again, 38% is just a starting point.
If I built a door that had almost no gap and cloth covered 703 (possibly mineral wool would be more absorption) would that work or is the fact that it is a door instead of 2 layers of sheetrock and absorption?
The issue is just the reflective surface, no matter what it is made of. As long as you can get the actual reflection point (and a good margin around it) covered with a few inches deep of something like 703, that shoudl do the trick. I guess you know the "mirror trick" to finding your FRPs?
The joists are 12" so there is a lot of room for insulation.
Cool! That's good news. So basically you'll have an air gap of around 12 inches, which is excellent.
There is also the problem of sealing the large joist areas off on all the cavities above the outer leaf stud wall.
Your best bet there would be to just cut some wood blocking to fit the gaps, nail/screw it in place, and seal it with caulk. You do want to fill the gap with mass, and also get an airtight seal, so that's a good way of doing it. It's a lot of tedious work, sure, but nobody ever said that building a studio would be easy! :)
Not to mention that even with insulation you only have the outside sheet on the house to keep the traffic noise out.
Not sure what you mean by that: You say that the two gray walls are cement? Those are the outer leaf walls you are referring to, right? And the run from floor to ceiling, unbroken, right? Thick cement (concrete / brick / stone) walls are pretty much the best isolation you can get! Lots of mass. So that shouldn't be a problem. Where is the street with the traffic noise, in relation to your diagram? How bad is the traffic noise?
Given all of that I planned on sealing the outside walls and basement cement walls with 2 5/8 sheetrock
Adding layers of sheetrock directly onto an existing concrete wall won't accomplish much. You'd need many, many layers of sheetrock to match the mass in the wall, and even then it would only buy you 6 dB of extra isolation. But one or two layers of drywall up against your existing concrete won't do much. Look at the mass density of drywall vs. concrete, and you'll see what I mean. But what IS important with a bare concrete / cement wall, is to seal it. Cement is porous. porous is not good. Just paint it with something that will seal it well. Or plaster it ("render"), if you really think you need more mass in it.
Then on the inside leaf 2 5/8 sheetrock inside out so technically a 3 leaf system because of the kitchen floor packed insulation 12" double sheetrock 1" space the double sheetrock on the outside of the inside leaf. (see image below)
Not a good idea! 3 leaf raises the resonant frequency of the MSMSM system, and reduces isolation, espceially in the low end of the spectrum (drums, bass). Far better to take off the sheetrock on the underside of the studs, and use it to beef up the bottom of the floor above. So you'd cut the sheetrock into strips almost as wide as the space between the joists, then out it up against the underside of the floor above, hold it in place with cleats, then caulk it all around carefully. That will get you much better isolation, using the exact same mass. You'll have your sub-floor with a couple of layers of sheetrock on it, then a 12 inch air gap filled with insulation, then your inner leaf sheetrock. That should give you pretty good isolation. 3-leaf always loses against 2-leaf, all other factors being equal. This is what I would do if I had that situation:

Now I am leaning toward the 2 leaf if the isolation is reasonable. Which of these two plans will work best for isolation?
Definitely two-leaf! For the same total mass and wall thickness, two leaf wins against three leaf every time, and in a major way. Just moving that sheetrock from the underside of the joists to between the joists will probably gain you something like 10 dB of isolation! Well worth while, I think...
I will go with side absorption on door (if i have one) and other side as apposed to slats.
That's what I'd do too. And don't forget to keep the room as symmetrical as possible, at least as far back as your ears. What ever you do to one side should be done the same way on the other side, to keep your stereo image as tight as possible.
In my online education I found several instances where splaying did not help much for walls.
It seems to be a case of "six of one, or half a dozen of the other". Some designers swear by splayed walls. Others say it's just as good to go with straight walls, and then splay the treatment. Personally, I like the idea of splayed walls: I do think they help somewhat with two things: Keeping first reflections away from your ears, and killing flutter echo. Sure, you can deal with both using treatment, but why not just do it with the walls themselves?
Can it help on the ceiling of such a small room?
If you are going to splay your walls and angle your ceiling, then the walls need to be splayed at least 6 degrees each, and the ceiling 12 degrees. However, you already have severe height limitations in your room, so I don't think you have the luxury of being able to slope your ceiling at a useful angle. I'd just go with a straight ceiling as high as you can get it, and build any treatment you need under that.
I also saw a build here that used 4" of rockwool for the ceiling of a project that must have been a single leaf basement design,
Probably not. It was most likely an inside-out design, which you certainly can do in your case! Just fill your ceiling joist space with rockwool or fiberglass, and cover it with acoustic cloth for aesthetics. Soft ceiling, hard floor, = good acoustics. So just leave your floor as concrete (it is concrete, right?), or maybe lay some laminate flooring on top, if you prefer the look. - Stuart -
