It does take a while to get your head around some of this stuff! A "leaf" is just an envelope of something dense, such as drywall, plywood, MDF or something like that, supported by studs / joists, completely surrounding your room. In simple terms, if you think of an ordinary house wall, there is a "leaf" on each side of that. You have studs holding it up, and drywall on each side, which is the two leaves. so that would be a two-leaf wall. If you nail another layer of drywall directly onto one side of that wall, then it is STILL only a two leaf wall: all that you did was to make one of the leaves thicker and more massive. So you could have, for example, 3 layers of drywall on each side and it would still only be a two leaf wall. Each layer of drywall that you add helps a bit with sound isolation. However, if you build another stud frame say an inch away from that existing wall, and put drywall on one side of those new studs, then you now have a 3-leaf wall, which is a bad thing. Contrary to what common sense tells you, adding that third leaf will actually not make your isolation better, and will in fact make it worse for low frequency tones. Not intuitive at first glance, but fact nevertheless. The reasons are a bit complex, and I don't want to confuse you more at this stage, but there are solid reasons based on physics and mathematics as to why that happens. So you never want to have a 3-leaf wall if you can help it. There are things you can do to help if you have no choice and MUST build a 3-leaf wall, but it is far better to avoid that if you can. The problem with adding extra layers of drywall to an existing ordinary house wall is that it helps a bit, but not much, because the studs themselves, inside the wall, directly link the drywall on each side. So when sound waves on one side make the drywall vibrate, that makes the studs vibrate, which in turn makes the drywall on the other side vibrate, so the sound actually travels right through the wall. The solution is to "decouple" the wall, basically by slicing it lengthwise down the middle, so that there is no direct connection from the inside "leaf" to the outside "leaf". Of course, you can't actually slice a wall like that in practice, so what you do instead is to have two stud frames, and put only one leaf on each frame. So if you have an existing wall that you want to isolate, your best bet is to take the drywall off one side of it, build a new frame about an inch away, and put the drywall back on that second frame. Now you have a fully-decoupled 2-leaf MSM wall, which is the absolute best way to isolate a studio at low cost. "MSM" stands for Mass-Spring-Mass, which you also sometimes see incorrectly as "MAM" (Mass-Air-Mass). The two "M's" refer to the two leaves (since they have plenty of mass), and the "S" refers to the air gap between the two leaves, which should be filled with insulation, such as rock wool or fiberglass. See? It ain't so hard to figure out!

Decoupling just means separating things physically so that there is no mechanical connection between them. It could refer to separating two sides of an MSM wall, or separating speakers from their stands, or separating a drum kit from the floor, or many other things, but the basic concept is that you cut all hard mechanical ties and replace them either with air or with "resilient" mechanical ties. For example, it's really hard to make your speakers float on air ( :shock: ), but you can mount them on something soft and bouncy, like acoustic foam, some kinds of rubber, etc. That would be "decoupling your speakers". Etc. - Stuart -

Soundman202015 March 2011#7
oh...and.....if your leafs (leaves?) are different densities is that better?
Well, kind of. For three leaf, the recommendation is to put as much mass as possible on the middle one, but for a two leaf wall the optimum design has the same amount of mass on each leaf. You can vary it a bit if you have to. Since you got the "dumbed down" version easily, I'll go one step further to help you understand it better. An MSM wall is a tuned system. It is designed to resonate at a certain frequency. If you happen to play the exact note that it is tuned to, it will sing along with you happily, vibrating and resonating very loudly, and in fact AMPLIFYING that note, and transmitting it though to the other side wonderfully! So that must have you asking: "Why would you do that? If the wall is supposed to isolate, then when make it so that it amplifies?". The secret is that you tune the frequency of the wall so low that it is below the range of human hearing. So it will only amplify frequencies that you can't hear. That won't be good if you happen to be a whale recording whale songs in your whale studio, but for us ordinary humans, it is great. So, it is tuned to one specific frequency. Actually, it's kind of like a parametric equalizer that you tune to one note: it lets that specific note through but blocks all others. It's the "blocks all others" part that we are interested in. BECAUSE it is a tuned system, it makes a great way of isolating studios. It only passes that one frequency (and a small range of others around it), but it blocks all others. Technically, it is a bandpass filter. It is based on a principle of physics that is commonly called "mass-spring-mass". Basically, you have a mass hanging from a spring that is attached to something else massive. If you pull down on the lower mass and let it go, it will start bouncing up and down at its natural resonant frequency. If you try to make it bounce at another frequency, it will resist you and refuse to bounce. Or think of a pendulum, which is a weight swinging on the end of a string. Give it a slight nudge, and it will happily swing back and forth at its resonant frequency until the cows come home, but try to make it swing faster or slower, and it will not. It will fight back, and not swing at all. Try it: Hang a bottle of coke from a piece of string about a meter long, and see what its natural swing frequency is. Now do your best to make it swing at three times that frequency. :) Good luck with that!!! An MSM wall is the same: It wants to resonate at its one specific tuned frequency, and does not want to resonate at any other. It turns out that, from the resonant frequency and up to a frequency of about 1.4 times higher, an MSM wall is a really lousy isolator, and actually helps sound get through the wall. But above 1.4 times the resonant frequency it starts to isolate, and at twice the resonant frequency it is isolating reasonably well. From there on, the higher you go, the better it gets, all the way up the entire spectrum. So obviously the key is to design the wall so that the resonant frequency is as low as possible, and at least half of the lowest frequency that you need to isolate. So, for example, if you want to isolate everything below middle C (roughly 260 Hz) then you would design your wall to have a resonant frequency of 130 Hz (which is also C, but one octave lower), and your wall will be a lousy isolator up to about 182 Hz (F# below middle C), but from there on it will do an increasing good job. And of course, if you want to isolate the entire audible spectrum, then you'd tune your wall to something below 10 Hz, since the lowest audible frequency is 20 Hz. So how do you "tune" your wall? There are only two major parameters to consider: The mass in each leaf, and the distance between them. The "distance" basically determines the "spring" part of the equation. Acoustically, air is a really good spring. You don't normally think of air being "springy", but it is. Especially if you happen to be a sound wave! So the resonant frequency of your wall is set by how much mass you have on each leaf, and how big the air gap is between the leaves, period. Want a lower frequency? Increase the mass, or increase the air gap! Or both. Simple! :) There's an equation you can use to figure out what the frequency will be for any given combination of masses and air gaps, but there are also certain rules of thumb for folks who don't like equations: A single layer of 5/8 drywall on each side of a 4" air gap will get you reasonably good isolation for most instruments, except for the bass guitar and the drum kit (and other things that put out lots of low frequency energy). Two layers of 5/8" drywall on each side of an 8" air gap will take care of those too, pretty well. If you want overkill, go with three layers of 5/8" on each side of a 12" air gap, and you'll be good even for the huge deep rumbling of cannons and spaceships. That's about all there is to it, really! OK, just kidding! It's a bit more complicated than that, but that's the general idea. More mass buys you more isolation. A bigger air gap buys you more isolation. Decoupling buys you more isolation. Don't go less than 4" for an air gap, and don't use any drywall smaller than 5/8", and you'll be fine. Frequently we get people coming here telling us that they plan to use 1/2" drywall over a 1" air gap, and they get all snooty when we tell them that it wont work: Sometimes they go off in a huff, because the think they know better than the laws of physics, but some do stay around, learn, and end up building great studios. I often wonder what happens to the guys who leave and don't ever post the photos of the "wonderful" studios they built with 1/2" drywall... :) Do the math for that 1/2" drywall over a 1" cavity, and you understand why its a really bad idea: that wall won't isolate well even in the mid range! You'll hear people just talking on the other side of it, very clearly.
How about being unparallel?
All that does is change the resonant frequency along the length of wall. You'll have a range of frequencies, starting from highest at the thinnest air gap and running down to lowest at the biggest air gap. But your entire wall is only as good as its weakest part, so what you'll get is that the wall will act more or less as if it was tuned to the highest frequency (thinnest gap). So if you want to do that, make sure your thinnest part is already at a low enough frequency.
Seems many of the plans in John's book have that.
Yes, but that is for other reasons, that we'll go into in the next lesson! 8) (And also takes into account that "thinnest gap=weakest link"). Also, don't get confused with the ISOLATION of a room, and the TREATMENT inside it: They are two different things, but they often look similar on the plans...
Am I correct in assuming that those 90° corners are bad for building up standing waves of bass frequencies?
Yup! Which makes them an excellent place for putting bass traps! In fact, all modes in a room terminate in corners, so that's why you'll see a common recommendation here that the absolute best place for your bass traps is right there, where two or three surface all come together: You are guaranteed of hitting the mode, plus you get a free boost in effectiveness in the corners. - Stuart -
