knightfly11 November 2006#2
Is there any way you can space at least half of your absorbers off the walls by at least 75mm, preferably 100mm? It looks to me like you have a combination of things going - some comb filtering, and not enough mid absorption but too much high absorption. This one change may fix it all, if not then some sort of absorber between the mic and the wall in front of the singer. If possible, either double the foam thickness or replace it with mineral wool of around 48 kG/m^3, it will do more for you... Steve

knightfly11 November 2006#4
Your questions made me pay closer attention to your description - before, I thought from the pix that the singer was facing a glass at about 500mm - this can/will produce constructive and destructive echo, depending on frequency. This happens because at different frequencies the wavelength of the sound is different, so some frequencies are IN phase (original and reflection) while some are OUT of phase. Totally IN phase produces peaks, totally OUT of phase produces nulls, and frequencies that are somewhere in between will produce varying degrees of adding or cancelling of the sound. Since I know that the ATM 3035 is a better mic than what your graph shows, and it's unlikely that ALL those peaks and dips are vocal chord related (I could be wrong) I jumped to the conclusion that comb filtering was happening - from my previous description, if you were to graph the frequency response of a mic in front of a hard surface, you would see a lot of peaks and dips in the response - a graph of these looks kind of like a comb (like you'd comb your hair with), this is where it got its name. On closer reading, though, (mainly seeing that your graph is from a SINGER and not a test tone) I could be 'way off base here - now, I have to ask: are you asking this question because the recording sounds bad, or because the graph makes it LOOK like it should sound bad, or both? (you never did say how it SOUNDS - and that's the bottom line)... Steve

knightfly12 November 2006#6
You're on the right track with more absorption, since that will cut down on the reflections that cause uneven frequency response (comb filter effect) but I doubt you'll see much difference in room noise from doing that - things I would look at are Reverse the direction the singer looks, so the corner is behind them Make sure the music stand does NOT reflect between the rear of the mic and the singer's mouth - pick a different angle, move a light or the singer if necessary to eliminate light glare off the stand - microphones are NOT immune to rear pickup, especially at close distance; so you shouldn't have reflective surfaces returning echoes to the rear of the mic. Don't worry about wierd looking waveforms from a human singer - if they produced only smooth frequency response sine waves, they'd be called a FLUTE. The end goal is a good sound - so the most important thing you can do is EXPERIMENT. There's going to be some things about your space that either you don't think to mention, the pix don't show, or I don't catch, that can change the way your sound records - so make test recordings of the same singer with one thing changed per test, and compare them - the one that sounds the best, IS the best... Steve
