Labcoat Man26 November 2010#1
Hi, Firstly thanks to all the contributors this site, such a great source of info and experience and a real help to someone like me starting from scratch. This is a newbie post , my question is kind of specific but below a quick background, if you'd like more info please let me know and I'll provide as much as I can. Background Im leasing two old offices in a warehouse back to back to use mainly as an after hours studio and also for some activity during office hours. Each office is 4.35m (length) X 3.35m (width) X 3.3m (ceiling) and also considering opening up the dividing wall down the track. Meanwhile, I want a degree of sound isolation in one room to protect the corridor and a neighbouring office (enough to bring moderate monitor levels, acoustic guitar strumming/singing down to a level outside the room that blends with competing traffic / building noises around an industrial estate). I'm building isolated walls in front of the existing single leaf old wood partition walls with 110mm gap (including insulation) before the existing wall so the aim is Mass Air Mass in a two leaf system. My question is this, the wall facing the corridor is fitted with a cupboard that lies flat on the office wall and juts out in to the corridor space, its about 500mm deep. With the doors closed I think this then makes a triple leaf system with the existing wall and the new internal wall where the cupboard is. ie. MASS ( new internal isolated wall) -AIR -MASS (existing cupboard doors closed) AIR (cupboard interior) - MASS (back of cupboard) Should I a) seal the doors air tight? or b) actually leave them slightly open so the cupboard air space becomes part of the larger single cavity? Also would stuffing the cupboard with insulation be a good move to help stop it becoming some kind of bass resonator into the corridor? Any advice would be much appreciated! cheers Tim

gullfo1 December 2010#4
if the corridor behind the cupboard is ok for letting sound into, then that can help with relieving some of the bass. conversely you could end up getting noise in... the cloth is to keep the fibers in and allow the air flow into it. if necessary some panels in front of any room absorption can help support HF in the room. just ensure the key reflection points are covered by absorption and/or angles. adding some additional layers of drywall may be what you need to increase the isolation but maybe now is the time to run some tests. using some sine sweeps and white noise to assess isolation and some tapping (metal and rubber hammers) to check flanking paths.
