Mario Petrinovich wrote:
Oh yes, regarding how I see that root sequence (or whatever, :wink: ) applied with hanging diffusers, I.....
Mario this room is special and unique. D'Antonio calls it an Ambi-Anechoic room or something. In fact it is an anechoic room, but one were a very dense diffuse field is created at a very low level, and it doesn't show first reflections. In a normal room, you have first those first reflections, then the diffuse fields build up. Here you don't have this first part of first reflections. While never seen it, I think you can compare it with recording or mixing in an anechoic room, but with a dense diffuse field at a low level, which probably will remove or improve the feeling of being in an anechoic room. While all diffusers the absorption is soo high of this construction, that the room is practically excluded from recordings. Hence I think you can call it an anechoic room with some ambiance. (in fact D'antonio himself calls it that) Anything else that you suggest to make, is not that room anymore. This must have cost a fortune, even just to produce all those things. I think this room will be discussed a lot in the future, since it has properties, one finds nowhere else. But if this is really that much better, I don't know. It's a very expensive anechoic room adjusted with a low level diffuse field to make it a more comfortable environment to work in. But I should love to see, feel and hear it. Eric
Mario Petrinovich17 May 2006#23
I couldn't find what D'Antonio said. But I think, what Massenburg said in the link above is pretty accurate. It isn't ambechionic room. It is recording, mixing and critical listening environment. On the link above they said that D'Antonio calls only the principle in action that absorbs sound, "ambechionic". What they achieved with that room is, firstly, they minimalised bounday effect (and comb filtering), and secondly, they managed roughly the same ambience (reverbation time) for highs and lows. In the same time the room has no fiber absorbtion, because the "walls" of the room absorb sound. I think it is a good principle, which could be developed in the cheaper direction. I guess that less costly room would have more boundary effect, and more fiber absorbtion. But, I believe the principle is still ok, and possibly could achieve better results, with less money, than standard rooms. I would really like if somebody try to do this cheaper, to see what would be the result. But, we are still at the begining, and people wonder what the result of this specific room is. If the result is good, maybe we will see more of that. -- Mario
