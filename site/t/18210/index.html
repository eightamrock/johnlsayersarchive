No problem! It happens all the time... :)
The studio doesn't "have to" be done by the time we move in, but it'll be good to get a good start on it while the house is in "construction mode".
A very good idea! Make the most of having the builders and materials on site, and of supervising things carefully, to avoid silly mistakes like running water, gas, electrical or HVAC stuff through the area above where you will build the studio. It's VERY easy to control that now (despite the grumpy looks and the "You can't change that" comments), but much harder, if not impossible, to re-route that later. You can also make sure that things are done the way you will need them done around that area, to minimize the pain later, when you do build the actual studio.
The area I'm using is dedicated studio space, so I'm not going to be running interference with anything other than the bathroom on the other side of the Vocal booth. It is; however, under the front entry way, and den (aka paino room), so I will probably need to do some sort of ceiling treatment to stop much of the sounding going up, and/or coming down
IT is going to need a bit more than that, I'm afraid! Treatment does not stop sound: that's a myth (a very common one, unfortunately, and eternally propagated around the internet...). Treatment and isolation are two totally different things, using totally different materials. Treatment does not stop sound any more than a sponge stops water coming out of your tap. A sponge is great for mopping up water that spilled in places you don't want it, but useless for stopping it coming out the tap. Ditto with acoustic treatment: it is very good at mopping up sound that spilled places where you don't want it, but it will not stop sound getting out of your room. So you need to properly isolate your studio, not just treat it. First you isolate it, then you treat it.
I'm a drummer, so the drum room is an important feature to me... and the quietness of the drum room is an important feature to my wife
Then you DEFINITELY need some SERIOUS isolation! The drum kit is the hardest of all instruments to isolate, for two reasons: 1) it is the loudest of all, and 2) it puts out a lot of energy in the low end of the spectrum, and low frequencies are the hardest to isolate.
3) I work in radio, and do quite a bit of voice-over work, so the voice booth needs to be comfortable enough for 30min plus voicing sessions. The current one I have is somewhat crammed, with no air flow, so after 15mins or so it can start to get pretty uncomfortable.
Yup! HVAC is essential to ANY studio, and is also probably the most overlooked part. So it's good that you are taking that into account up front! Many first-time studio builders don't realize how important it is at first.
and in order to keep the rooms somewhat of a comfortable size, I'm not sure that double wall construction will work... that being said, if someone can offer an idea with double wall construction that would work, I'm all ears!
Put it this way: you need isolation, and lots of it, so you don't actually have a lot of choice here. You can either do proper fully decoupled 2-leaf MSM isolation "double walls", or ou can spend boatloads of money on highly exotic and very, very massive isolation materials, such as sheet lead or steel plate. Those will save you space, but you need tons of it. Literally. And a ton of steel is not cheap. Neither is a ton of lead.... A ton of drywall is a lot more reasonable... :)
Just another side note... I will be running 2 mic lines, and a headphone return up the den/piano room (directly above the studio), for the ability to track piano parts recorded within that room
A word to the wise: If you think you will need two of each, then run six of each. Cable is cheap, and installation is simple right now. Not so a year down the line, when you have some unusual need you never thought of, and can't do it due to lack of cables... Also run a couple of CAT-5 lines, just in case, and maybe even a VGA or video line. You never now what you might need.... The few extra dollars (pounds) you spend on that cabling now is nothing in comparison to the total cos of the studio, and might mean a big difference when the time comes. - Stuart -

Deesubsee23 April 2013#6
Hi Jay, Will your drum room mostly be for recording or rehearsal? What I'm getting at is that it's difficult to get great drum tracks in small rooms. Think about whether you really need the drum area isolated from the mix area. If they can coexist, both uses may benefit due to the additional room volume. Small rooms are very difficult to treat properly. If it's possible, adding more height to the room would be a bonus. Particularly, if you look at room-in-room construction for improved isolaiton, the structure will eat up height. good luck!

Why so many CAT cables? Most networked systems use a single CAT cable that daisy-chains from one device to the next. There's nothing wrong with running multiple CAT cables, but it does mean that you are running multiple independent networks, which complicates things greatly. Far better to have the minimum possible number of networks.
