Hi everyone I live in a two bedroom Victorian cottage in a small rural village near Ipswich, England. Due to the imminent arrival of my first child in september, I am retreating to the back garden to make way! I have of course bought 'the book', and have been doing my research on this site for a few months, but I have many questions still, as no-one else it seems, has to make do with a small garden shed. Despite this minor complaint, I am a one man band, with not much more than a laptop, some monitors and a few guitars, and rarely play loudly. Fortunately, my garden backs on to my five neighbours' back gardens, so I am about 30 feet away from anyone's back door. As you can probably guess, this is a budget project, and as I've already spent £1500 on getting a good quality wooden shed, the budget is smaller still. I'm looking to spend around £500 more to complete the project- although I do have a wonderful girlfriend who has more money than me! So my main aim is not to gain professional level sound isolation, but to make the best of what I've got in the now limited 10 weeks before a small bundle appears. Below is a sketchup of my shed along with its dimensions. One of the first problems I have encountered is obviously the size, which I will have to live with, but more specifically the height. If I use to much insulation on the floor and ceiling, I will end up with a very sore neck. Because of this, I am going to have to come to a compromise with the floor and ceiling insulation and have them no more than 100mm deep each. I have bought a few materials for the floor so far, including a rubber mat that I intend cut into strips and use as pads under the decking of the sub floor. I've also bought some 'thermawrap' foil-backed bubble-wrap to go between the floor joists, as it can get pretty cold in the winter. I was thinking of making a grid-like frame for the floor that sat on the rubber pads, with the thermawrap in-between, polysterene or rigid fibreglass on top of that (still between the frame) with a layer or two of either MDF or plywood over the top. This is not ideal, I know but I have to work with what I've got. I had originally thought of filling the floor frame with sand, but I think it may be too heavy for the floor to take- but I don't know! So I guess my first question is, will this be adequate, or is there a better solution in terms of materials I can use?

AVare19 July 2008#5
Polystyrene is useless as a sound absorber. The roof will be your weak link. I suggest building the floor from mass. Multiple layers of plywood, gypsum, or poured concrete. Andre
delusion19 July 2008#6
Whoa! You have the bad guy hide out thing going on with the shed exterior! Your door modifications look like an improvement. Maybe I'm just missing it in the pics, but I don't see any seals on the door/frame. You definitely want to seal that up as it's an inexpensive improvement that pays off. You would be amazed how much sound can get through a little gap/crack. Do the seals in the daytime so you can use the visible sunlight leak to determine if you're good or not. What are you plans for the walls? Can the structure take the weight of modifying the ceiling with more mass?

delusion20 July 2008#9
In that case, perhaps consider using John’s “inside out” wall design for the inner leaf to save on materials and highly valuable space! I used this concept in a small iso room that has similar final dimensions (9’8”x7’2”). I’m very happy with the isolation I’m getting and in a place that small every nanometer counts! You can just use the less expensive pink fluffy fiberglass in between your walls and get the same results. Use the denser rigid fiberglass/mineral wool/etc on the internal walls of the room for acoustic treatment. I’m talking filling the stud cavities of the inside out walls and then covering with cloth. Also, it looks fine in your diagrams, but in your pic it looks like your new floor is touching the outer wall framing at the door frame. Make sure your new floor does not touch the outer walls in any way.
Ross Peters wrote:

Hey Ross, good to see your project is back to life. As for attaching the fabric: yes, use staples. Stretch the fabric tight and then use plenty of staples around the perimeter. After that you can cover the "staple lines" with decorative strips of wood trim. -OR- Pull the fabric all the way around the frame and staple it to the back as in pic #3 and trim pieces won't be necessary. You can do bass traps modules by yourself with a little effort, although 2 people make it much easier....1 stretches and 1 staples. I would definitely consider walls and ceilings a a 2 man job though. Here's some pics of one wall that was done in this way.

John Sayers20 July 2009#24
I prefer the frame method - build your frame on the wall to make sure it's the correct size - then take it off the wall, brace it then wrap the cloth around it and staple at the back. With some cloth the weave allows you to see the frame through it - in this case you must paint the frame black so it isn't seen.
