Hello All, I’m hoping this will be my long last construction post. Thanks to all that have already given me advice, and as so many here are, I’m very thankful for Rod’s book that helped so many things become more clear. I will again go through the basics of my situation: Budget I would like to stay around $2,000 but I realize this may not be possible and if not, I'm willing to go beyond that, hopefully $4,000 would be reasonable cap. I do plan to do most of the work myself. Recording Environmet/Loudness I plan to build a combination room, as I mainly work alone. I currently record much of my stuff direct and/or MIDI. Currently my drums are a MIDI set. The instruments I generally record acoustically include congas, bongos, shakers, cabasa, mandolin (rarely), acoustic guitar (generally in combination with the pickup & Fishman Aura), mic’d guitar amp for electric guitar (although many times I record direct through a Boss GT-3 effects processor) and of course vocals (generally close mic’d). I’m hoping that when I’m done, the room will sound good enough as to inspire me to record more things acoustically. :) Although I play a reasonably large variety of music, most of my stuff could likely be best described as James Taylor-like. In regard to loudness, I cranked my studio monitors a touch louder than I would expect I would ever run them, and measured about 92 db in the listening position. My recording set up is pretty lightweight compared with most folks on this forum. Alesis Studio 32 board feeding Sonar PC setup via an Echo Mia Midi (audio & midi) card, and a set of Behringer Truth B2031A active monitors. Goals My goal is to create a space that I can quietly record in, while not bothering the rest of the household. I have currently been recording in the basement, although not in the exact space where I'm now planning to build the room. The issues I currently have in this space that I want to resolve as a part of this project are: 1) Noise from the utilities (furnace, water heater, water softener), the fan in my PC, and floor noise from upstairs being picked up excessively by my large condenser mic. I am currently thinking I would like to locate my CPU outside the room (likely in the planned closet) to remove that part of the problem. 2) Stop sound (notice it’s no longer “noise” :wink: ) from my recording/mixing from disturbing the rest of the house. My family is very supportive and have not complained, but I’ve noticed many times my wife moving to the bedroom to watch tv, rather than staying in the living room where it’s obvious my music heavily bleeds into. When I had my monitors running at 92 db, the reading in the living room (directly above) was around 72 db. My perception is that much of the sound was coming through the floor through the openings for the HVAC vents into the room from below. I should mention that the basement is on one end of our house, we have no backyard neighbors, and the closest neighbor on the “basement end” is 75 feet away. Although I have not taken db readings outside, I’ve never had any complaints, and when I go outside and listen with music loudly playing, even now, it doesn’t appear to be a problem, so I’m not concerned about reducing the sound leakage to the outside world. 3) The space is currently almost always chilly during the winter months, so it should be much easier to quickly warm with the space enclosed, insulated, and properly vented. 4) Of course I’m hoping to ultimately have a much better space acoustically in which to work. 5) Since my initial postings on this site, my wife and I have talked about the eventual possibility of putting a grand piano in the living room (great room) directly above my proposed studio space. I think it would be nice to do wiring in such a way that it would be possible to mic the piano in that room as the desire arises. It might be nice to have the capability to do other recording in that room when a more live environment is desired. Space Description The space I'm looking at is in the basement of our multi-level home. The space is currently unfinished and mostly concrete walls. I’ve provided a sketchup file trying my best to describe the unfinished area and what is currently in place. Above this area is a living room with carpet on the floor. The living room has a 13 foot ceiling (not that I believe that has any bearing on anything). There is plywood above the floor joists. The joists as you can probably tell, are 2 X 10, 16” OC. There is plenty of existing ductwork and PVC pipe for the heating & cooling system as well as to vent the water heater to get in the way. I should mention that on the other side of the angled stud wall are the stairs that go up to the family room, which sits atop the half concrete wall (behind the furnace). Construction Plans for Isolation I am in the process of beefing up the ceiling by adding two pieces of 5/8” sheetrock between the existing floor joists. I plan to fill the rest of the cavity with fiberglass insulation batts – a layer of Owens Corning QuietZone 3 ½” thick, plus a layer of Owens Corning R19 6”. Next, resilient channel. I keep getting conflicting information on whether using RC-1 (as opposed to RC-2) would be reasonable for two layers of sheetrock on the ceiling or not. I’d like to use RC-1 if possible, but certainly want something that is structurally sound. For the walls, I’m looking at 2 layers of 5/8” sheetrock on the outside, 2 X 4 framing, filled with fiberglass insulation, RC-1 on the inside with two more sheets of 5/8” sheet rock. I have approval from a structural engineer on the plan for the ceiling with the placement of a couple of headers, which I have taken into consideration in the proposed placement of the walls shown in the attached Studio Floor Plan.jpg. ”Bedroom” Studio My wife is very understanding in this endeavor, however, she would very much like the room that we build to be sellable as a basement bedroom when we sell the house. So basically I have agreed to basically construct the space in such a way that it hopefully will not be a turnoff to any possible future owners. I realize that this is probably far from ideal for recording, but so it goes in the land of marital bliss… Current Progress I have my building permit, so all is well there so far. I’m currently in the process of beefing up the ceiling with two layers of 5/8” sheetrock. The Big Question of the Day Living in South Dakota, where we can have 100 degree weather in the summer and -20 cold in the winter, we have a combination heating/cooling forced air system that I am hoping to tap into for heating/cooling/ventilation into the studio. At the current time, how we run the ductwork for this is my biggest area of concern for isolation. I plan to have plumbing & heating professionals add the appropriate ductwork. If you look at unfinished.skp, you will see two runs of rectangular ductwork hanging from the ceiling. One is the supply line and the other is the return. The one that runs in a T and goes fartherest away from the furnace is the supply line (heated/cooled air). The ductwork is sheetmetal and currently not lined. Although not shown in the drawing, there are three vents that feed the living room above from the supply line through 6” round sheet metal taps. These all tap off the top of the rectangular ductwork. They each basically have two 90 degree turns in them but are all only about 2 feet long. For example there is one that feeds the living room upstairs from just above the window (as shown in Vent to Living Room.jpg). In visiting with the plumbing people, they said the way they would normally vent into this room would be to tap off the rectangular duct (the next joist over from the upstairs vent I just mentioned) and position it just above the window, since that would normally be the coldest spot in the room. This would again include two 90 degree turns and would be maybe 4 feet long. I talked to them about using lined ductwork for new ductwork. To my untrained mind, I’m doing all this beefing up work to the ceiling, then we are creating an open airway from the room to the living room through the ductwork. I’m assuming this is a real problem, correct? I would really appreciate advice in this area. I hope to continue to journal my progress now that I finally have this post out here. Thank again for all your help. I’d be lost without it! These will simply tap off the existing sheet metal ductwork that supplies the rest of the house. In consulting a structural engineer, the recommendation was to shorten the span of the existing floor joists by adding a couple of headers (to support all the added sheetrock), so I've got one of those in place and am carefully planning the construction of the other one at this point. I have a floor drain slope that I am also preparing to partially level so that I can run one of the walls fairly close to the drain. I've settled on using RC Deluxe (single legged) to mount the inner leaf sheetrock including the ceiling. I waffled on whether or not to go with RC-2 (two legged) on the ceiling since it will be supporting 2 layers of 5/8" sheetrock, but it appears many on this forum have used RC-1 for this and had no problems, so that's the way I'm going. Last weekend I bolted down the plate for almost all the walls to the floor using cedar 2 X 4's except the portion that will be near the floor drain. Between the header and the wall plates my daughter said she can finally tell I'm actually building something when she came home from college for Thanksgiving! :lol: I should mention that for the ceiling, I've decided to go with 2 batts of insulation layered; one 4" (actually 3 1/2" X 15" X 93") and one 6" (6" X 15" X 93") The 4" is Owens Corning Quiet Zone and the 6" is regular OC R19. I’m sure that will dramatically change when the sheetrock is all up. Then the plan it to build rock wool traps to calm it down again. The second picture shows the side by side doorways to the utility room. Far from ideal, but we do what we can do. The LDL header over the doorways ended up being only about as tall as a 2 X 4 to make it under the ductwork, but my architectural engineer says we should be good, and honestly the floor in the living room above is noticeably more solid since this wall has been in place. This is a much appreciated side effect, as the knick-knacks on the living room entertainment center no longer dance around when Dad walks past, spurring scoffs from my daughters on how fat Dad is… :lol: In the third picture are the doors – one 32” for the entrance to the room and two 36” doors to the utility room. You might wonder why I went with a 32” rather than a bigger door as an entry door. The reason, whether it makes sense or not, is that the doors at the top and bottom of the stairs that lead to the basement are both 32”. I spent about as much time thinking about and investigating what to buy for doors as probably any aspect of this whole project, and rightly so in my mind. Rod’s superdoor, although no doubt a great design, was simply more than I was going to be able to accomplish and/or build. So after much deliberation and research, I decided to buy solid core exterior 1 ¾ doors with adjustable jams. I priced them all over and finally found these at Lowe’s. With a 10% discount for applying for their credit card, I got the three pre-hung doors for $675 including tax. I thought I had found the perfect deal for me until the Lowe’s guy called me when ordering them and told me the supplier would not sell them “un-bored”. According to him since they are fire-rated doors, it would be illegal to sell them that way. The Lowe's guy pleaded my case, but to no avail. Although it bummed me out, I decided to go with them anyway, partly because they were a much better deal than I found otherwise, and partly because I was ready to be done worrying about doors. I may still plug the holes, but right now I’m thinking I might try normal latches and modify only if I find it to be the true weak spot. I really wanted the doors in so I could make the header above the doorways to the utility room as thick as possible and still have the doors fit. So that’s accomplished and it’s good to have the doors purchased. I believe I’ve read that for a studio to be for hire, doors with a permanent sill would be a no-no, but I would have many other issues to worry about in my space in addition to that I’m afraid. I really hope they do a reasonable job (regarding isolation) as I feel this will be a critical point for me, especially with the noisy utilities right on the other side of these doors. I wanted to point out the type of electrical boxes I’m using. I’ve seen these boxes in other projects on this site, so they may be more common than I realize. If they’ve been described elsewhere I apologize. In the first picture below, notice a silver screw-head half-way down the left edge next to the stud. These boxes are adjustable. As the screw is tightened, the box face is moved further away from the wall. Of course it remains to be seen how well they will work, but it seems to me that when using RC + 2 layers of sheetrock, being able to adjust the electrical box depth will be handy rather than having to fix them 1 ¾" out from the stud to begin with ((5/8" X 2 layers) + ½” RC). I put putty packs on the boxes, and with this style box it’s not feasible to put a putty pack between the box and the stud, but hopefully the putty pack will still be beneficial. The second picture is the back view of the same outlet box (before it had sheetrock behind it) to show the best job of putty packing you’ll likely ever see from me. :D As always, I welcome your advice, comments or questions… til next time. :thu:
