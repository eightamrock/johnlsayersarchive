steptoe wrote:
So what you're saying then is that the new design is much better, do you think that the size of the live room would be more appropriate now? I also use less concrete blocks with the new design too which of course means I also save a couple of quid to spend elsewhere 8)
It's looking better (to me, anyway, but I always tend towards 'fewer, bigger rooms' and an 'acoustic problems are inversely proportional to room size' mindset), but depending on where your inner walls end up, the control room might be getting dangerously close to being twice as long as it is wide (a 1:2 ratio). It might sound counterintuitive at first, but you probably want to finalize what your construction methods and materials are going to be first (how many layers of what materials, how much air gap, how the system of ceiling joists is going to work, how much depth that is going to take up, etc.) so that you can figure out what your maximum possible ceiling height and maximum internal wall dimensions can be. Then, take your time to research 'room ratios', 'room modes', and the like, before deciding where to put the walls, based on the 'maximum possible dimensions' determined by your wall construction system and air gap. Read up as much as you can on this site, Rod's book, Wes Lachot's article(s) on his site, and elsewhere - use that information to decide how far 'in' to place your inner 'room within a room' walls, how high the inner ceiling surfaces will be (although since the existing roof starts at 7', you'll probably want to figure out how to get the ceilings 'as high as possible in the available space' while trying to avoid making the wall dimensions a simple multiple of the ceiling height), and, finally and consequently, where to put that dividing wall between 'studio' and 'mudroom with the freezer in it'. Since you're going to all the time, trouble, and considerable expense of putting up new walls, you want to make sure, while it's still 'on paper' and not 'set in stone' (literally - or at least 'set in mortar'), that you're not building four walls and a ceiling that are all multiples of the wavelength of, say, the low 'E' on a bass guitar (and think of esoteric things like: do you want your room modes to correspond to notes of the scale, or fall between the notes of the scale), before you place the first brick or cut the first piece of lumber. You can decide how geeky you want to get about room ratio stuff before you finalize your plans, and you can decide to make a conscious decision that two feet of interior length or width are more important to you than giving up interior space for an 'ideal' ratio, but once you start laying those courses of block, you'd have to tear down and all start over from scratch to change your mind. :shock: Disclaimer: Keep in mind that I'm only an aspiring amateur acoustics nerd, and not an accredited professional acoustics nerd - budgeting those quid you're saving elsewhere on a professional consult (for example, I believe Mr. Sayers offers an affordable phone consult service) for things like preferred room ratios and roof mass-loading vs. roaring jet airplanes overhead wouldn't be the worst use of some portion of your budget - and could probably actually save you "cost vs. results'" money.

chrisrnps14 July 2009#28
steptoe wrote:
The inner ceiling will look to start at approx 6.5' and rising to approx 8' in the center. The materials I've alerady thought about. 100mm concrete blocks for the inner walls with 4" gap filled with insulation between inner and outer garage walls. Inner ceiling will be lined with 3/4 inch ply with a layer of plasterboard added to that if needed held up with runs of 4"x2" timber supported by the walls of each room, of course I know these can't be suspended from the roof, or can they?
Not if you can help it. Ideally you don't want the inner walls and ceiling to so much as touch the exterior walls and ceiling at all - the roof/ceiling isolation is already going to be the 'weakest link' in the design (the roof is so much less massive than the block walls). The more you can bulk up the exterior roof and inner ceiling with mass (as long as you can do so safely) the better off you'll be. It's going to be tough to get your roof-insulated air gap-ceiling to try to approach the isolation you'll get from the block-insulated air gap-block walls. Although you have a very cheap source of plywood (and trying to work it into the design to creatively get the most effective use out of readily available materials at hand is well worth considering), the plasterboard will give you much better isolation.

It's good knowing that when the planes come over now that the majority of the noise comes through where the room hasn't been finished, I obviously won't know how good the sound insulation is until I have the whole room finished off and I'm able to move onto the next part of the build.
