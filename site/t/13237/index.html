Soundman20202 July 2009#5
Two big points I see on that diagram (in addition to what Javier mentioned). 1) Your inner leaf appears to be coupled to your outer leaf via those pillars. You MUST totally decouple your leaves if you hope to achieve decent isolation. 2) You only have single-leaf walls between your CR, LR and booth! That won't isolate very well. You need two-leaf walls there as well, unless you really like a lot of bleed in your recordings! :) Also:
the outside material you see on the picture is the original steel reinforced concrete sections.
That sounds holey and porous to me! You will need to carefully seal that entire surface to be totally airtight. Hermetic. Perhaps a layer of stucco on the inner surface (plaster), and a good coat of paint would do the trick.
not sure at the moment if I should leave this as an air gap or fill it with insulation.
Like Javier said: Fill it with insulation. Rock wool or fiberglass, 48 kg/M3 density. It is part of the "spring" in the MSM equation. It also damps flutter echo on the cavity, and brings down the resonant frequency of the entire wall. It gains you many decibels of isolation. Without insulation in the cavity, the resonant frequency goes up, and the isolation goes down.
100mm concrete block which will be placed against the steel girder uprights
That would be a mistake. If the inner leaf touches the girders, then you are coupling your inner leaf to your outer leaf, and killing your isolation. Leave a small gap so that it is impossible for the masonry to touch the girders, even if mortar drips out the back, or bits of rubble fall in the gap. Very important point! Any bridging at all between inner leaf and outer leaf will kill your isolation.
again there will be an air gap between the block wall and the garage door of approx 2 inches.
2 inches is not much! You need at least 4 to make it worth while, and maybe more in your case, if those planes are really loud. And once again, your block walls appear to be touching the outer leaf at the door frames. If you do that, you will have very poor isolation. The inner leaf must be a totally free-standing, fully-decoupled structure, with no mechanical connections to the outer leaf (except through the floor).
Now I'm not sure as to whether I can get away with dry wall (timber frame and plasterboard) along the left and right side walls instead of useing concrete blocks, it would save a lot of money if I didn't need the blocks,
Your isolation is only as good as your weakest link IN THE ENTIRE STRUCTURE. so if you do drywall for the side walls, then it makes no sense to do blocks for the other two walls, since they drywall is much less dense than the blocks (unless you use MANY layers of drywall, to attain the same mass as the blocks....) Also consider your doors, windows and HVAC. Those are all potential weak points. They need to be as good as the walls and ceiling. So you need really heavy, well-sealed double doors (back to back), two panes of thick laminate glass with a large air gap for your windows, and large silencer boxes for your HVAC (plus long duct runs and multiple 90° turns, etc.). But first of all you need to define your numbers. Without those, all of the above is moot. You might be over-building for your needs, and therefore wasting money, or you might be under-building for your needs, and therefore wasting money. - Stuart -

Soundman20202 July 2009#11
... wouldn't the fact that the fibreglass touches both the external in internal wall give you a bridging effect though?
Not really. It doesn't have enough mass and isn't rigid enough to create a flanking path. Now, if you really stuff it in hard, packed tight, then yeah, you'll create a bridge. But if you just lay it in there normally, without forcing it, then you'll be fine. And you do need to fill the entire gap: It gains you several dB of isolation, for several reasons.
With regards to noise bleeding through the inner walls I was imagining that certain things would be recorded on there own such as drums against a click/sample drum track and then bass, guitar and vocals seperately etc.
Exactly. But when you are tracking drums (for example), you want to make sure that what you are hearing in the CR is just the sound coming from the speakers, NOT the sound of the drums themselves coming through the wall or window. In your CR you only want to hear what comes out of the speakers, nothing else. You also don't want sound from those very same speakers bleeding back into the mics in the LR, nor the sound of you stirring your coffee, chatting on the phone, or the sounds of noisy equipment... The idea of isolation is just that: Keep all the sounds only in the place where they originate, and not let them through to any other place. Mics seem to have a habit if seeking out the most annoying distant sound in the entire building, and amplifying it by 200 decibels.... I think Murphy is on the design team of all mic manufacturers, and makes a special effort to ensure that all mics can pick up the sound of a pen tapping on the table in the CR MUCH better than it picks up the subtle nuances of that once-in-a-lifetime perfect voice take.... :) And if you have the vocal booth right there, then you might well want to use it together with the LR, tracking from both at the same time. So you do need good isolation between rooms as well. Take a look at the designs on this forum: You'll see that practically all of them use full 2-leaf MSM walls for ALL of the construction, both in between rooms and also to the outside world. There's a reason for that. ..
...then maybe fit screens in the corners to cut out the parallel connection between the walls,
I think you misunderstood what Javier was saying. "Screens in the corner" won't do it. If you want to kill flutter echo the way he mentioned, then you need to angle the actual inner walls themselves. And corners are for bass trapping, not for "screens".
With regards to the plane noise, there are restrictions on planes ...
Well, that's good news I guess! But you still need to get some real numbers regarding just how loud they are, and how quite you need to make them. Without that, it's just a guessing game.
the C-17 just gives us a low level rumble
... which just happens to be the hardest sound of all to block! :) The lower the frequency is, the harder it is to isolate. A low rumble is much harder to kill than a loud squeal.... Once again, you need numbers. Get a sound level meter, and actually measure the levels of those planes C-weighting, slow response. Measure on several different days, at different times, with different wind conditions. Keep notes. If you can get a spectrum analyzer for the job, even better! Then measure what level you need to record at. Wait for a really quite time late at night, when you can't hear anything around, and measure that. That's what you are aiming for in the studio, even with the biggest, heaviest play flying too low over your place, with the wind blowing the wrong way. Subtract "B" from "A". That's how much isolation you need. Compare that with the estimated numbers for your planned construction method to see if you are going overkill, underkill, or just right. Anything but the above is just guessing, and will cost you money.
...then add plasterboard to the interal surface of the roof
Don't do that until you check with a structural engineer! Yeah, you SHOULD beef up that outer leaf, for sure, but there is the issue of weight. You might be putting too much stress on the roof structure by adding a few hundred pounds of mass. The roof might not be able to take the extra load, without modification. Only a structural engineer can tell you for sure.
you'll notice that the block section is now seperated from the external walls.
Much better!
I have my first 2 loads of concrete blocks arriving tomorrow
Whoa!!! Slow down there a bit! You don't even have a plan yet, and you already have building materials arriving? That doesn't sound like a good idea. You need to get your ENTIRE plan in place first, including all doors, windows, HVAC, electrical etc. Not to mention the actual layouts for your rooms. For example, what ratio is your CR based on? Which way will the speakers fire? How will you deal with symmetry? What are you going to do about the issue of first reflections, which looks to be a big problem with your current layout, no matter which way you orient the CR.... And where are you going to put the bass trapping in your CR, since the door is occupying the place where it should go? Etc. Etc. Etc. Then, you need your structural engineer to check over your plans and make sure that you actually can do what you are planning to do, without it all falling down on your head one day... There are about a zillion and forty seven things that you need to do before you should even think of laying your first block. I think you've done about six or seven of them so far, leaving only about a zillion and forty to go.... :) - Stuart -

Before you get too far, do you really need that 'vocal booth'? The dimensions of it are almost square (only 1/2" off from a perfectly square room in two of the drawings), which will be problematic - and its small size will bring up the problem peaks and nulls right into crucial frequency ranges. You can make some portable "gobo" panels if you need a temporary perimeter around a vocalist, drum kit, amp, or instrument, which can (partially) reduce "bleed" between one sound source and another, the sound source and room ambience / reflections, etc. In the photo above, gobos are located behind the mic and in a semicircle behind Mr. Sinatra, which happen roughly correspond to the 'lobes' of a cardioid mic pickup pattern. Some gobo panels could be made pretty inexpensively, and if you design them to be absorptive on one side (fiberglass or rockwool) and reflective on the other (plywood or other surface), would let you play around with 'controlled' room ambience, first reflections off the 'reflective' side of the panels, etc. on amps, acoustic instruments, drums, etc. by experimenting with placing, adding, and removing panels around sound sources when you record. IMHO you've got more to gain from one bigger, better-proportioned live room than with a square vocal booth plus really long, skinny live room (neither of which would sound as good).
