n1ck wrote:
Prior to running into this problem I had planned on using butyl caulk along the entire perimeter, but now am wondering if I shouldn't use that pink / yellow foam crap. It seems like if I use caulk I'd be caulking for days, and I don't know that that'd even properly insulate gaps this wide, would it?
It may be possible to use expanding foam in middle of the cavity to add a very slight redundancy for your outer seals, but use an acoustic caulk for the outer seal between the timber frame and the masonry. If it's not sealed air tight then it won't perform to the level you require.......and yes, you will caulk for days. That's probably rule number four in studio builds: Thou shalt use an obscene amount of acoustic caulk. Caulk everything. Seal everything. You only get one real chance to do it right without having to pull things apart to re-do them. The technique which is mostly used is to use backer rod to fill the cavity so you can run a nice bead of sealant against it. all the best, Steve

stevev29 July 2015#31
n1ck wrote:
Will having just caulk (particularly the acoustic kind) provide enough of a weather seal for my needs? This is an exterior window in my basement.
no, you don't want to use the acoustic caulk as a weather seal. When you take out your old windows, square up the surrounding masonry so you can make your frames fit with a much closer tolerance than you have now. That will probably involce an angle grinder and fair bit of work unfortunately. Once you have a closer tolerance you can put some backer rod in the gap and then caulk a bead against that. On the exterior face of the window you would then have a timber casing bridging the window frame and masonry to protect the caulk seal.
n1ck wrote:
How do you apply the caulk so that when you're pumping it through one side it doesn't make a total mess as it eventually pumps out the other side? Wouldn't it all start pumping out as you fill the whole cavity up?
The backer rod stops all this happening. all the best, Steve

Not even taking off the drywall from the existing ceiling? Is she aware that the cost and complexity go up, and the degree of isolation goes down? In other words, that you'll have to spend more money and more time building it, for less result?

.... and that would make your walls 3-leaf as well, thus complicating thing even further, and increasing your costs even more... The question is: why do you want to do 3-leaf walls?
(except for the ceiling, on which I'll only drywall one side.)
Correct. If you did it on both sides of that frame, then you'd have a 4-leaf ceiling, which is even worse than a 3-leaf ceiling...
For ventilation, I'm gonna do two vents (each with a baffle box) and an inline fan. Question: where does the fan actually go? I've heard that it should ideally go in one of the baffle boxes to minimize noise. Is that true? Sounds kinda weird to me.
You are right! That would be weird. And it would be a mistake to use an in-line fan as well. If you had an inline-fan inside a silencer box, you'd have no way of accessing the fan for maintenance, repairs, or replacement. You'd have to rip open the box to do that... It's far better to have an exhaust fan on the far end of the outlet duct, or a supply fan on the far end of the intake duct. The characteristics of the fan will be determined by the room size and occupancy. You will use the equations to calculate what the flow rate, flow velocity, static pressure, and duct diameter will be for that fan. Don't guess. You will also use those equations to figure out the size of the mini-split unit that you will need to do the cooling and dehumidifying. Don't guess there, either...
so even though it's not up to code,
Then you have a problem.... a BIG problem! Do not ever build anything that is not up to code, and fully inspected and approved, with all the paperwork in order. Apart from anything else, modifying your house without that will certainly invalidate your homeowner's insurance, so anything at all that goes wrong in your house puts you on the hook, both financially and criminally. For example, if part of the ceiling falls and injures someone, you would be criminally liable for that, as well as financially liable for the medical bills, and for whatever else that person decided to sue you for. Bad, bad, bad idea to build anything that does not meet code, and/or does not have all the paperwork in order.
I'm strongly considering letting the vents exhaust and pull from inside the basement itself.
Code does not allow that. You cannot use one habitable space as the source for "fresh" air that feeds another habitable space, nor can you exhaust the stale air from one into the other.
And then for electrical, I'm thinking about either NOT having it
... and where would you plug in your equipment? Battery powered amp? If you ever want to record, battery powered console? Battery powered DAW? Battery powered mini-split HVAC?
or running a small piece of conduit with an extension cord and then sealing up the hole really well.
While technically feasible, once again that would probably not meet code, and could be a fire hazard. Hiring an electrician to correctly wire in a single outlet and single light inside your room would not be all that expensive. Especially when considering the huge liability you'd be taking on by doing illegal electrical work. If that extension cord overheating and started a fire, you can bet your bottom dollar the insurance company would not pay out a single cent, ditto for any injuries/deaths caused by the fire. If the fire affected your neighbor's houses, you'd be liable for that too...
