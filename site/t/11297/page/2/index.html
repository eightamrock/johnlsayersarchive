a) how much will this roof design limit the isolation of the structure as a whole
It will be your weak point for low frequency isolation. Three leaf reduces isolation greatly at the low end.
b) does the amount of open space in the attic area limit the negative effects of having the roof coupled to the exterior box
Not really.
c) would putting green glue between the rafter plate and the sheathing have any positive benefit.
Nope. - Stuart -

xSpace24 January 2009#32
I haven't completely figured out the "how much three leaf is too much". But I do know it is done a lot. It is one of those areas you live with what you have. I am of the mind that your two leaf ceiling, if I understand what I am saying:), will have enough mass to overcome some of the three leaf effect of the roof/3rd leaf. There again, how much, I haven't retained that information. But, the three leaf is in the top most part of the structure. It should not alter the walls ability to isolate as planned. For getting the most overall isolation from the exterior...what you are doing is probably the best method. When you pop a roof on there(as a two leaf), run soffit, ridge and gable end vents, you have so many holes that the loss in TL from your current three leaf is better then the loss of pure isolation from a two leaf. Make sense?
Soundman202024 January 2009#33
Though it is coupled, wouldn't the fact that there will be an air gap on two of the roof sides where the rafters come down to the plate and at the ridge vent mean that this is not a sealed space so it would not have as negative an impact as a sealed attic?
Not really. It doesn't need to be sealed in order to act as a third leaf. The "sealed or not sealed" issue only affects the TL frequency curve, but a leaf that isn't sealed is still a leaf. That said, Brien is right: You have to live with what you have. I think I'd run the numbers to see if this is even going to be a problem for you. Maybe it doesn't even matter. But you'll never know unless you figure things out. Best case, you might not have to do anything. Worst case, if it is a huge issue, you can always re-design the roof. After all at this point all you'd have to change is a few dozen bits of wood! Sad, but not the end of the world. It would be MUCH sadder if you didn't check the numbers, then carried on building and only find out when you are finished that it didn't work too well! Hopefully it won't come to that, but spending a few minutes figuring out the numbers might save you some big heartache down the line! - Stuart -
