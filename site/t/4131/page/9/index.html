Concrete weighs about 150 PCF, dry sand weighs about 100 PCF; if your block are 8" thick and 1" each side is concrete (these are typically NOT made of the same density concrete as poured, but less) then filling with sand would increase mass by (allowing for webs) maybe close to DOUBLE - so the mass of this leaf would be nearly twice, plus sand has quite a bit of damping properties (ask the GG man about damping) Sooo, strictly from a mass law standpoint I'd say (for that particular leaf, NOT the entire 2-leaf wall assembly) it'd improve TL within the mass law range by AT LEAST 6 dB; however, memory may not serve too well so here's a link http://irc.nrc-cnrc.gc.ca/pubs/ctus/13_e.html HTH... sharward6 November 2006#221
Will you be adding blocking between your new ceiling joists? The "staggering new joists between existing joists" (or, in your case, trusses) concept is a good one, but I noticed Rod calls for the use of steel braces in an "X" formation in his book in order to keep the joists upright. As a result, the vertical clearance is reduced by a few inches, since the intersection of the "X" has to be under the existing joist (or bottom chord of truss). Also, since you won't be tying your new ceiling joists to your inner leaf wall frames, you'll need to use resilient sway braces to keep your walls upright. Finally, you'll want to install drywall in this sequence:
  • 1 layer on the ceiling
  • 1 layer on the walls
  • 1 layer on the ceiling
  • 1 layer on the walls
This makes for a better seal, and it helps to support the outer edges of the ceiling layers. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward7 November 2006#225
If you were planning to firmly attach your inner leaf ceiling joists to your inner leaf wall frames, you wouldn't need sway braces or anything special. However, since you don't have that luxury, you need a way to maintain the structural integrity of your inner leaf wall frames. The fact that they'll be softly touching your inner leaf ceiling via a resilient seal does not address this need. You need something to keep the tops of your walls from leaning. (The anti-sway braces to which you linked are, I think, meant to make the walls less likely to bow when pressure is applied...? :roll: What you really need is something to keep the tops from moving.) Take it from me -- even with my wall frames tightly secured to the floor with 5/8" steel bolts, they definitely wanted to tip if I put any pressure on them. The leverage that 8' to 10' studs have on soleplates is extreme -- perhaps enough to break the soleplates if I had been so inclined. Once I tied the walls to the new ceiling joists with my seismic ties, that issue went away. Well, they still give a little, but that's because I have no sheathing or drywall on the framing yet. Once that's attached, I'm sure the box won't hardly give at all. And I must confess that I have a couple of temporary braces that tie outer and inner leaf framing together, just to keep things more stable until the sheathing goes up. Are you following Andy Eade's Rotten Basement Conversion Thread? He faced this issue last week and opted to fabricate his own solution, partly to save money and partly to save time, as he had framers due on site in less time than he'd be able to get the real McCoy delivered. :roll: I don't have Rod's book in front of me, but you may want to do some more studying on this subject. I'm pretty sure he covers it. --Keith :mrgreen:
