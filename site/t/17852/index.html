:thu: :yahoo:
1) Will self leveler change the existing slabs sound isolation properties in a negative way?
It will probably help, in once sense: it will be adding more mass to the floor, which can have a positive effect on isolation: And if your floor is not level, then it's a sensible thing to do, if you plan to lay laminate flooring on it, for example.
2) Since I intend on building 2 inner rooms which will be sitting on the same slab as their floor, does the slab need a relief cut in the middle to decouple one room from the other creating 2 separate concrete slabs/floors?
That depends on the answer to "How much isolation do I need?". But regardless of the answer, the bigger question is "Can you cut the slab?" You'll need to get a structural engineer in to answer that question. That would imply also cutting the foundation, since cutting just the slab is not going to fully decouple. You should have separate foundations and slabs for maximum isolation. Another option might be to cut out a section of slab under just one of the rooms, and pour a new one, with its own foundations, inside the footprint left by the section you cut out. But the only guy who can tell you for sure if / how to do that, is a local structural engineer who knows your local building code.
3) can you still decide to line the concrete floor with floor boards or similar once the studio is fully built or is this something that needs to be decided during design and construction?
Yes you can, but it would have to be some type of laminate flooring. That can be laid directly on concrete, provided the surface is flat and smooth. Solid wood flooring is not so easy to do, and more expensive.
So far, I have studded up the brick walls, put insulation in, and lined the walls with one layer of 16mm soundcheck plaster.
Whoaa!!!!! You seem to be getting ahead of yourself: When you say "studded up the brick walls,", do you mean that you attached the studs directly to the brick, with no gap? If so, that is no use at all for isolation. You'll have to take that off again, and do it correctly.
I have also put in a suspended ceiling with insulation and 13mm plaster.
13mm is too thin. You should be using only 16mm plasterboard (drywall, sheetrock, etc.). 13mm is too flexible, not massive enough, etc.
Obviously none of this will be seen once the new rooms are done, just thought i'd mention what has been done so far.
Why will it not be seen? You are talking about your inner leaf, and it sounds like you have already completed it, so why will it not be seen? You can have only two leaves for your studio: the outer leaf (brick walls and roof, in your case) and the inner leaf (plasterboard on stud frame and suspended ceiling in your case). If you now build something else inside that, then you are adding a THIRD leaf, which means you could be REDUCING your isolation for low frequencies. 3-leaf construction is a bad idea, unless done correctly and only where absolutely necessary. Going on to your next post:
yes, the studs are fixed to the brick wall and the plaster fixed to the studs.
OK; then that needs to come off. It is not doing anything to help you, and is wasting useful space. If you work carefully, you might be able to re-use some of those materials. The stud frame should be built about 2 cm away from the brick wall, and not touching it in any way. It also must not touch the existing roof, ceiling joists, or walls on any other way. Plus, you are talking about two rooms, so you need a separate frame for each of those rooms, and those two frames must also not touch each other. This is the basic method for attaining the type of isolation you are talking about.
The suspended ceiling (which is raked following the original roof line) is attached (via drop down anchors) to timber ceiling joists,
When you say "drop down anchors", are those proper acoustic isolation hangers, or just solid metal/wood hangers? If they are not isolation hangers, then the ceiling needs to come out as well, and be re-built correctly. You probably can never achieve the levels of isolation you are talking about unless you do this correctly.
Im not going to tear down what ive already done
Well, sorry to be blunt, but unless you do there is very little chance you will be able to get the high levels of isolation that you say you need, in order to be able to play drums in there and not infringe on your local noise regulations. Drums are the loudest of all instruments, and can easily hit 115 dB or more. getting that down to the level required by most municipal noise regulations is going to need some pretty hefty isolation, based on a proper 2-leaf MSM system. What you have done already prevents you from building a proper 2-leaf MSM system. That leaves you with the possibility of a 3-leaf MSMSM system, which implies some major extra expenses in terms of mass, and some major extra loss of room space, due to the much thicker walls that you need. So you basically have three choices: 1) bite the bullet, take it out carefully so you ca re-use as much as possible of the materials, and re-build it right. 2) spend a lot of money beefing up what you have done so far with a huge amount of extra mass (three-leaf construction implies that the middle leaf needs twice the mass of the other two leaves), then add your new third-leaf walls, also with a lot of mass, and large air gaps, which will leave you with very small rooms. 3) Be prepared to face the cops every time they come knocking on the door, and spend your money on paying the fines for breaking the law. Those are your only real choices here. And now that I'm looking at your photo, there are indeed other issues that need fixing: You have perforated your drywall in many places, for electrical cables. You cannot do that. You are trying to attain high levels of isolation, so you cannot make any holes in your drywall. Any place that air can get through, so can sound. The drywall must be a complete, unbroken hermetic seal around the room, with no holes, cracks, gaps, or weak points at all. The electrical work must be done INSIDE the final room itself, surface mounted. You can have only one single penetration for bringing in the main electrical feed, and that penetration needs to be done correctly to maintain the isolation. The power is then distributed around the room from that point, on the inner wall and ceiling surfaces.

I have a question from one of the charts on that thread, ... Below the triple leaf it says STC53 and below the double leaf it says STC63. Am I right in saying that there is a 10db difference between the two designs?
Correct. In other words, from the subjective human point of view, the the double-leaf wall would be twice as quiet. (We hear 10 dB as being roughly twice as loud, or half as loud). So by taking out the third leaf, you make things twice as quite! Surprising, but true... When speaking about isolation a 10 dB change is huge! Having said that, STC does not real measure decibels: it measures fictitious, mangled, tweaked, adjusted "things" that used to have a vague relationship to dB, ... :) STC isn't a good way of measuring sound isolation for studios anyway, but those graphs give you a good idea of the concepts.
If you were to make the inside 3rd leaf thicker (ie more sheeting) would it improve the differential db?
It's not so much the thickness of the leaf, as the mass of the leaf. Mass is what matters. But yes, assuming that you increase the mass of the middle leaf, you would get a small increase in the total isolation. You can also get an increase in isolation by increasing the size of the air gaps between leaves.
Do you have any advice on the how to seal the holes where the electrical cables come through?
Probably acoustic sealant would work, assuming that the holes are not too big. You could also try using mud and tape to patch it, but I'm not sure how well that would work.
I hadnt punched any holes for air con or fresh air becasue I am still not sure where to run it all, both internaly and externaly.
That raises another issue that you might not have considered: ideally, your HVAC ducts should be isolated where they pass through the wall, and the holes must be sealed. You also need to build silencer boxes for the ducts.
I was hoping that there was a split system aircon/heating unit that can also deliver fresh air.
There is one, but I don't recall who makes it. However, it does not supply much fresh air at all: probabaly nowhere near enough for what you need. And you still need an exhaust duct. - Stuart -
