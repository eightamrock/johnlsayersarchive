sharward8 July 2005#15
Guten Tag, Carsten! I have some questions. According to the illustration the neoprene pucks appear to be wider than the joists. While that does stabilize them somewhat and make them less likely to slip off, I believe that will have an adverse effect on their ability to deflect. You probably already know that you need to be extremely careful to calculate the weight of your structure so that you do not risk overloading or underloading your elastometers (pucks). I'm concerned that by putting pressure on only a portion of the puck, it will not have opportunity to squeeze out to the sides -- which, as I understand it, is critical in the performance of the puck. Perhaps I'm worrying too much... But I would hate to see all of this beautiful and difficult work be compromised by something that some careful calculations and trimming would have addressed. After I read some threads in this forum and in the Acoustics Forum I came to the conclusion that my last inner construction sketch is absolutely worthless. :( What I understood from it is: 1. You need to have a mass-air-mass construction to achieve any useful transmission loss. (Which was not the case in my ceiling construction, being directly connected to the outer ceiling) 2. Floating floors can be the “extra mile to go” and in some cases where you have no direct neighbours, your room is freestanding and your concrete slab is on the ground (all this is the case in my room) they may not be necessary. 3. Floating floors have to be heavy otherwise it’s resonance frequency can be in the audible range (up to 300hz). I really took into consideration not to build a floating floor at all, as I also have to do with the height of my room. To build a heavy floating floor and a reasonable solution for the ceiling would leave me with a room height of probably 180 cm or less on the lower side of the building. Dan Fitzpatrick13 July 2005#22
Hi Carsten, I think you are on the right track here. Although I'm not an expert so take this for what it's worth. Although this is a three leaf roof, what can you do about it? Not much. Three leafs are pretty common with roofs, it's hard to avoid. I'm going to have one, Keith's going to have one, etc. Although yours may be worse because it's a small airspace. then again it may be ok because it appears fairly light weight. All things considered, this particular third leaf may cause effects in a high frequency range which is better than if it were low. But i don't know for sure. Triple leaf systems are complex to figure out, and as i said i'm no expert. I have a similar situation with one of my planned double walls being up against a garage door. that will be a triple leaf. but i'm actually going to make the gap as small as possible in hope of keeping any resonaces out of bass range. But what i really wanted to point out is that your inner joists appear very "light" to carrry your new inner leaf ceiling. (not sure about the existing joists -- do you know if they can carry the extra weight?) maybe this is just that you didn't diagram your wood members accurately, but the inner leaf joists look like they are about 40mm thick. this is way too thin to carry two layers of gypsum. even if you use 55mm as in your walls you will not be able to span many of the distances in your plan safely. maybe you already realized this, but i wanted to make sure because it will also affect your finished ceiling height. also -- what is that brown line above the top yellow layer of insulation? there should be nothing solid there, not even paper or foil. and the airgap in my opinion should be completely filled with insulation, it makes a difference according to what i've read. dan
