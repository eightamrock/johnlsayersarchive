britune9 August 2009#37
I was going to build above my garage. Rod's book warns against it. An elevated wood deck (your floor) will resonate and vibrate and pass sound. Unfortunately, it is hard to get isolation there without adding a huge amount of weight, that you stucture may not be able to support. You can put in a concrete floor or a sand filled floor, both are extremely heavy. Using the traditional "room inside a room" will add a lot of weight. If you have ridge vents and soffit vents, it is hard to get a sealed outer leaf. Here are the "above the garage" links to the others I know about. ... viewtopic.php?t=8426 viewtopic.php?t=9178 viewtopic.php?t=6932&highlight= viewtopic.php?p=60097&highlight=#60097 viewtopic.php?t=6932&highlight= I don't know of one "above the garage" isolating studio that was ever completed and shown on this board. There have been some that didn't need much sound isolation. I've decided to build in my basement when I get the time. Best of luck.

rod gervais16 August 2009#42
britune wrote:
I was going to build above my garage. Rod's book warns against it.
OK, let's see if we can gt this right......... I never EVER said - (in my book or elsewhere) to not build above garages (or in attics - or on 1st / 2nd floors, etc., etc.). What I did do was point out the problems relating to attempting to build isolation assemblies from elevated wood decks, and this was specificaly related to constructing floating isolated assemblies. I have (quite sucessfully) led people through builds above their garages (although they always seemed to have breezways between the house and garage - which (I think) helped to make it work. I have also (quite sucessfully) designed rooms inside of buildings (1st floor of a middle unit in a NYC Brownstone building being a great example) where paying attention to detail and careful planning created rooms that can be used 24 hours a day - although probably not with heavy metal and 1200 watt Marshall Amps. People tend to get tunnel vision - and (unfortunately) miss out on some big pictures because of it. If there was any one thing that I would like remembered about my book (from an advice point of view) it would be that (in the end) we are dealt the cards we are dealt, and thus have to work with the spaces we have to work with. I said repeatedly in the book things like - "there will be times where you are forced into things like triple leaf wall assemblies" (this after I said avoid them like the plague) - "however - you can't change what you can't change - so live with the cards you are dealt and make the rest of what you have the best that you can make it". One thing that helps with installations on normal floors - above garages - in attics, etc. is to develope a floor above the original floor using a solid mass of 2" 703 for an iso pad (stops dead any drum action effect) with multiple layers of overlapped plywood above that (green glue between layers if it's called for). This not only physically isolates the deck - but adds a little insulation in the mix which will help with the mid lows........ There are materials and processes I have developed uses for over the past 3 years that I was not using at the time of the book release - I will include these in the 2nd edition. Please don't paint pictures quite so black and white when using my material as a reference - I really did try to show that there is plenty of grey in this as well. Thanks, Rod

jpricekc wrote:

lightweight concrete is around 14 psf of mass - so from an isolation point of view for air transmitted sounds it will go quite a way in helping you out. But from the view of flanking transmissions - not only will it not help it might actually hurt. The 703 decouples - the concrete is actually a pretty decent transmitter when looked at from the viewpoint of structurally transmitted sounds..... So no - it is not a suitable replacement - but would indeed enhance the system. Rod
