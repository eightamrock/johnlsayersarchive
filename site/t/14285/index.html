Please read this document to understand better that the 38% rule is not a rule but a starting positioning suggestion that Wes Lachot uses in his work. In fact it is a placement "guideline" of the listener more than it is placement of speakers. At the end of the day you could set your starting position anywhere you want so this "rule" can be ignored completely. But be prepared to deal with the issues that arise from being in the center of a room and getting no good response from your monitors, or finding that with your monitors shoved up against the wall and your head 12 inches from them doesn't seem to give you the separation you had hoped for and puts all the sound directly in your face ;) The 38% Rule from Wes Lachot (Thread started by Eric.Desart ) http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.p ... =guideline

Osorio, You are very fortunate to have a room that is 6.2m in depth. (By the way, just a little shorter, about 6m, would actually be a better ratio with the width.) Here's the thing: with a room that size, if you get a lot closer to the front wall than the 38% spot, you'll start "hearing" the wall, if you know what I mean. The 38% point is usually pretty close to where I end up with rooms this size, and I have listened to many rooms this size, over and over, testing them, etc. Your milage may vary, of course. Incidentally, the 38% guideline has for me always been more exactly true for soffited monitors than for free-field monitors. There are so many different kinds of free-field monitors, each with it's own issues, that it's hard to generalize. For instance, some have rear-firing ports, which is a huge hassle in my opinion. But still, with free-field monitors just as with soffited monitors you are faced with the fact that you need to be 3.8m or so off the back wall if you want to keep the null frequency from that wall below the important bass range (30 Hz or so, as per the 1/4 wavelength equation), so that puts you somewhere near the 38% point right there. And, like I said, if you get much closer than 2.4m from the front wall, you'll start hearing bass reflections off that wall very prominently (at least I do). Then there's the modal footprint of the room to consider... The really tough question is where to put the monitors--that is, how close to the front wall to put them. I sometimes use the RPG program Room Optimizer to help with this, but I've found it really varies depending upon the monitors themselves. It's truer than true that some serious bass-trapping behind those monitors is a very good thing in any case. And yes, xSpace is correct that for me the 38% spot refers to the position of the engineer's ears, with respect to the (hard) front wall, and 62% from the (hard) rear wall. -Wes

The 38% point is a good starting point for the engineer, and that means 38% from the front hard wall (not soft treatment--that stuff isn't really a "wall" as far as bass frequencies are concerned). The inside of the inner leaf of the sound isolation system is where you measure your room dimensions. This is the same thing Stuart is telling you in his post above. You may have been figuring your room ratios wrong if you were measuring from the inside of the wall treatments. As for soffit mounting smaller speakers, I don't think it really makes sense. The reason we soffit mount large monitors is to smooth out the low frequency response anomolies caused by speaker-boundary interactions, but very small nearfields don't even put out that much low frequency information to begin with. I think you may be better off just treating the front end with bass-traps, and using a sub so that you can hear some bottom end. -Wes

In a studio design context, "acoustic walls" usually refers to walls made of fiberglass (or a similar material) and covered in fabric, wood slats, diffusors, or some other strictly "acoustic" covering, but normally not gypsum. Soundman202019 April 2010#10
I'm a bit confused about your diagram, Osorio: When I look closely, I see something labeled "brick wall", then what looks like a thin air gap, then "concrete wall", followed by a thin air gap, then gypsum with a bigger air gap (partly filled with mineral wool), then the final inner-leaf gypsum. To me, that is a FOUR LEAF wall! :shock: That could give you problems with low frequency isolation. Even if the brick and concrete are totally coupled (joined together and acting as only one wall, with no air gap) then it it still a three-leaf wall, and would still bring you issues with low frequency isolation. I'd suggest that you leave off the first layer of gypsum on your diagram, and move it to the inner leaf. That would give you a much larger air gap, and it would put all that mass on only one inner leaf, where you want it. That will increase the low-frequency performance of your wall substantially. Your wall should be only two leaves, not three or four, to improve your low-frequency isolation (although, with both concrete and brick on the outer leaf, the difference might not be large). And like Wes said: That isn't an "acoustic" wall. It is an "isolation" wall. The purpose of that wall is not to treat the room acoustics at all. The purpose of that wall is simply to isolate the room from the outside world. Isolation and acoustic treatment are two different things. The isolation wall stops sound getting in and out, and the treatment deals with the acoustic issues of the room itself. All of your treatment goes inside the room that is defined by the inner-leaf, and the dimensions of the inner leaf are the dimensions that you use for calculating your ratio. The isolation wall should ideally be a two-leaf MSM system, meaning that you have only one outer leaf ("M"), only one air gap ("S"), and only one inner leaf ("M"). If you have more than one air gap then you no longer have a 2-leaf MSM system, and your isolation will suffer at the low end of the spectrum (kick drum, toms, bass guitar, low end of the keyboard, etc.). It gets even worse if you have several very thin air gaps, which is what I see on your diagram. I'd suggest that you re-design your wall so that it is only two leaves. And yes, I'm somewhat familiar with construction in Brazil: I go there regularly, to install and calibrate post-production systems. The way you build in Brazil is pretty much the same as the way we build here in Chile, except that we build everything a lot stronger, due to the earthquakes.
