Since the "major" problem seems to have been solved, I want to take a stab at explaining the "cleat" thing. Perhaps there should be a prize for the one that provides the explaination that clears the haze. The dictionary defines (loosely) cleat as something that is attached to something else that is used to hold a third thing, for example, one of those anvil shaped things that is attached to the deck of a boat to hold a rope. Ok, your specific case. I want you (either literally or figuratively) to stick your head through the floor joists, into the area between the joists and the dirt, and look at the bottom of the joists. Now, your joists are 2" wide (1 1/2" actually, but we'll use 2"). Imagine that you want to make a "T" that has a 4" wide top and a 2" wide vertical section. You'd have to take a 1x4 board, lay it on the 2" joist with 1" sticking out on either side of the joist. An upside-down "T." Can you picture that? Now, let's suppose you did that on two adjacent joists. If you looked down from the top of the joists (you can take your head back out of the hole), you'd see a 1" lip sticking out on each side. These are cleats. You could now lay some 1x1 or 1x2 boards between the joists and they would rest on these lips and would in turn, hold up the insulation. It would preform the same function as the string I was talking about, just using wood instead. The cleats are just the lips that the slats of wood would rest on. How about that? Does that clear things up any? len

Insulation, vapor barriers, etc - Removing the old wood made NO difference as to whether you needed a vapor barrier, or where it should be placed; it's just that when I realized your under-floor space had no ventilation, I started worrying about moisture problems. Whether or not the wood stayed on the dirt, moisture would rise into that space - so you needed a vapor barrier to stop it. If you put that vapor barrier up against the floor, it would seal the entire area below the barrier and the entire area would remain humid. This is perfect conditions for rot/mold/mildew. By placing the vapor barrier against the ground instead, you stop moisture from rising into the cavity from below; now, all you need to do is keep excessive moisture from traveling through the floor DOWNWARD, and to keep the humidity in that space (and the space ABOVE the floor) below 40%. By not overlapping the tar paper layers and not using faced insulation, this leaves your entire floor SOMEWHAT permeable, which WITHOUT humidity control could cause problems, when the room heat drives moisture DOWN thru the floor and the vapor barrier on the ground TRAPS that moisture. Thus the need for mechanical means of keeping humidity low enough in the room - this will not only reduce the amount of moisture driven through the floor downward, it will allow the moisture level BELOW the floor to eventually normalize with that of the above-floor space, and everything should be happy. Wood is NOT a vapor barrier; nor is gypsum wallboard, plywood, MDF, etc - although the paper on gypsum wallboard PARTIALLY acts as one, just not a very GOOD one. This is why it makes no difference whether you removed the old flooring or not from a moisture standpoint - my concern was just that, after sealing this area up, the wood would start to rot from lack of circulation and cause the REST of your construction to eventually follow suit. These problems don't exist (for the most part) in so-called NORMAL construction, because of the ventilation as shown in the last sketch. But as I said in that sketch, it sucks for sound isolation. The paper backed insulation you have can be used in your walls, so long as there is only ONE vapor barrier and it's on the INSIDE of the wall area, just under the wallboard. That insulation can also go in the ceiling, depending on how you do your ceiling for acoustics. Cleats - if you looked at your bed rails, and they're typical, you saw what a cleat DOES - it forms a lip that will support the slats which run perpendicular to the long rails, so your mattress can rest on those slats and not fall through between the bed rails. Function-wise, the bed rails (the ones running from head to foot of the bed) are your floor joists, the extra small wood pieces fastened to the bottom of those rails would be your cleats, the slats holding your mattress from falling to the floor would be the slats you would run across between your joists near the bottom (supported by the cleats), and the mattress would be your insulation batts. The cleats would be fastened to the bottom edges of your 2x6 joists with either 8d finish nails or with screws, and would run full length of each joist on both sides, except at the very edge where there's not room to put them on the outside of the outermost joist. I noticed I'd forgotten to add the "X" across each board in the drawing to show that it's viewed from the END grain of the boards; I've replaced that drawing hopefully making that part more clear. Anywhere you see an "X" inside a brown rectangle, that means you're looking at the END of a board that's stretching AWAY from you, as opposed to the SIDE of a board, or the TOP. Oh, and all the GREEN things are nails or screws - I've just settled on those particular colors (along with aqua for gypsum and gray for metal, etc) for all sketches I draw. The staples and string method MIGHT hold, but I've never trusted staples that weren't at least an inch long - normal staple guns (T50 type) can only use 9/16" staples MAXIMUM, and since the staples would be supporting weight that's trying to EXTRACT them from the wood, I personally wouldn't trust them to do the job considering you are trying to CRUSH the insulation against the floor for damping. (Len, thanks for the cymbal analogy, btw) - I might be overly cautious about the staples/string thing, but once the floor's in place you can't go back and fix it if the insulation falls away from contact with the floor :cry: If the floor joists were further from the ground, you would have enough room to swing a hammer and could use the pallet strapping/roof nail method which IS strong enough to keep good "crush" on the insulation, but it doesn't look like they ARE that far above ground. That's why I suggested the cleat system, which I'm hoping is becoming a bit clearer by now... Another way that would be possible if your friend has a Palm Nailer, would be to use the string but fasten with roofing nails. A palm nailer has a tight enough working headroom that it should work to drive roof nails UP into the BOTTOMS of the floor joists. palm nailer This is the one I have; it came packaged with my framing nailer, and works really well; you just hook up to an air compressor, hold it against the head of a nail (there's a hollow place in the end) and it drives the nail, slow enough so you can stop before the nail is completely flush, so you could wrap string around the head of the nail. The pieces of 2x6 your asked about that run across the joists every so often are called blocking in this part of the country; other terms are "noggins", "cats", "stringers", depending on which part of the world you're in. but they aren't called cleats, because they're not really supporting anything so much as "spacing" or "blocking" your joists so they don't move sideways or twist. I hope this helps clarify things; go back and look at the "cleat" drawing now that I've shown which boards are end-wise, maybe that will help... Steve

Since you also mentioned that you didn't think the ceiling would support 2 layers of drywall added to it, I suspect that if isolation from the neighbors is going to be a problem, it will be noise from the roof more than noise from the floor. Somewhat off the topic at hand, you mentioned in your first post that you KNEW you'd have to deal with HVAC but you were going to put off thinking about it until later. Might I suggest that while you have the floor open, you might think about it now since I don't think you'll be able to get to the floor again after you put down the plywood. I'm sure someone will jump in here and correct me if I'm wrong but you might want to at a minimum run flexible INSULATED ducting under the floor for the return ducts at least. It's pretty cheap (25' for under $25 for 8'). I probably over did mine (2 supply lines and 2 returns in the main room AND the control room, a single supply line and return line in the drum room, and a supply/return 6' pair for the vocal booth. I added a supply/return pair for the office/hallway area just in case). As I'm sure you've read here, soundproof=air tight so ventalation is also important. HVAC systems usually skip the "V" part and just heat/cool the same stale air over and over again. Previously our homes were so leaky that we didn't have to think about fresh air because it would find it's way under doors and around windows, electrical boxes, etc. Most houses have only supply vents in the rooms and one big return vent usually right near the HVAC system but the air can return under the doors which hopefully won't happen in your rooms so you'll need the returns in the rooms. To maintain your isolation, you'll want to run SEPARATE flex ducts from each of the rooms all the way to the HVAC system. In other words you DON'T want a single line with "taps" off it at each room. Doing it this way will allow the sound travel from one room to the next through the ducts. You don't need to "design" the whole system right now but putting this ducting in the floor now while it's "easy" will save you some work later. Some things you just can't postpone. len
