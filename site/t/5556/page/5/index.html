Shar, Interestingly - before drywall ever came into existence as we know it today - it was produced in 16" wide sheets as plaster lath. The sheets were 4' in length. It was available in varying thicknesses - with the thinnnest thickness 3/8". These sheets would be installed on the wall - and then a 2 coat plaster job would be applied over them. Plaster jobs today are typically done in only the most expensive homes - with a special blue board made for the purpose. The board edges are taped - and then a single "flood coat" of plaster is applied over the entire wall. It makes for a great job with no seams showing in direct light - as happens with any drywall application. Sincerely, Rod

We have been using a “plumb bob” (?). When he hangs the plumb bob from the top plate 16OC mark it should hit the bottom plate 16OC mark but it doesn’t. Why? Why is it off?
Kathy my fellow builder...you have to compensate your measurements on the top plate for the 15 degree angle. If you think about it... What is the length of your bottom plate? What is the length of your top plate? It's longer yes? Yet your wall isnt wider at the top than it is at the bottom. The top is tilted at a 15degree angle, thus it's travel is longer. To properly 16OC for this wall you should measure and mark your bottom plate first. Then use the plumb bob to line up with those marks on the bottom plate. The place where you have to move your plumb bob string on the top plate to line up to those marks is where you mark your top plate. Try it and you will see that the distance between the your framing members will be 16OC all the way up. :D

knightfly13 March 2006#121
You're coming along well, Kathy; are your pix of framing showing the final, or do you intend to add firestops? I noticed that apparently Scott taught you the framing method that's predominant more on the eastern side of the USA, called "balloon" framing - more places West of the Mississippi use "platform" framing, where each 8 feet is it's own frame. You should be able to "google" those two terms and see some examples. Basically, though, you're gonna need some fire-blocking between each stud where they're longer than (IIRC) 10 feet; 8 feet is typical, just to be safe. This is just more framing lumber cut to length and nailed horizontally between studs, to prevent a "chimney" effect in the event of a fire. Slows down the spread a LOT. Holler if you need more info, or tell me to shut up if you just haven't gotten to it yet :wink: Steve
