Kathy30 August 2006#234
Ok, here’s the latest drama: So, as most of you know I bought this house a year ago. We’ve already been thru one snowy winter and New Hampshire has also been hit with massive rain storms and flooding this past spring. But not to worry - the joists in the old unfinished ceiling of the barn always appeared dry as did the floor below. In fact, I was rather proud of my non-leaking structure. Do you see where this is headed? So, Saturday night two weekends ago we had some rain - a bit of a downpour but nothing compared to the slamming we got in the spring. Sunday morning I go into the barn for something and what do I see? You guessed it. A huge puddle of water just to the left of the drumriser. You could have knocked me over with a feather. Normally, I’m real good at documenting everything and taking pictures as we go but grabbing a camera at this moment never entered my mind as I saw the entire previous years work flash before my eyes. All I could do was hold my head in my hands and cry out, “No, no, no”. I looked directly up above the puddle but could not see a leak. However, the beam and crossbeams were saturated. I got up on the ladder but couldn’t see where it was coming in from as the ceiling boards didn’t show one particular hole - just some wet areas. I called my neighbor over who also got up to look and he couldn’t pinpoint any better than I could. But those saturated beams spoke volumes. He grabbed a ladder and went up on the outside roof. When he got up there he discovered that the barn structure had NOT been re-shingled or re-roofed as the house had been. It was 20 or 30 years old. Although he could not see a specific hole he did see many cracks in the shingles and a nail sticking up in the general area where the water seemed to be coming in. Having absolutely no budget left and never fathoming in the prospect of a new roof I thought I was in serious jeopardy of finishing my new studio. His first suggestion was to get a gallon of the liquid tar gunk and go over as many areas as possible to seal it up. But as luck would have it, he actually works at a place that makes this heavy duty roofing paper with adhesive backing. It’s about $75 a roll. He said he would bring me home 8 rolls for free if I wanted. He said it could be placed right over the existing shingles and since the roof is slanted towards the back of the property it wouldn’t even be seen. He then added that many porches, garages, and additions sometimes use this alone and never put shingles over it since this is 100% protection and shingles are more of a cosmetic touch. My fear of heights (being on a roof!), my complete lack of know-how, and the physical challenges involved dealing with 60lb rolls of roofing paper really raised the bar this time. I mean, come on.....at some point in time you just have to succumb to the fact that this is basically man’s work and I’m going to kill myself! But as it turns out, this wasn’t that time. Scott’s just not available right now so I asked my best female friend if she was up for giving me a hand. To my surprise, she had no fear of being on a roof and said we could do it no problem. With some instructions from my neighbor on how to cut, measure, and apply the roofing we figured out our own system and completed the job in less than 5 hours. Instead of hauling up the heavy rolls on the ladder, we rolled it out on the driveway and cut to length the strip we needed. We then rolled that back up and took it up the ladder. Half the weight. We then positioned it, temporarily secured it with rocks and peeled off one half of the adhesive width (it is manufactured so that the adhesive comes off half at a time from one end to the other). Once that laid down we folded it back on itself to expose the other half and pulled of the remaining adhesive. We continued on - overlapping each new strip on top of the last and securing the adhesive even more with roofing nails. I can now add roofer to my growing list of skills! We did it and I am so proud. And happy that I just didn’t try to patch one section. This winter I can feel positive that no moisture is coming inside. Below are the pics that go with the story. As a footnote, I need to say that there was a bit of a controversial ending to this story but before I tell the very end I thought I would just post this much along with the pics and see what happens.

knightfly1 December 2006#241
Kathy, 1/4" glass is roughly the same density as 7/8" gypsum, so putting two separated panes of this would be just a bit less than having a narrow gap wall with 2 layers of 1/2" gyp on each side. IF you keep the air gap as wide as possible (by putting the glass on the OUTER surface of each side of your door) and then do a surface molding to hold the glass in place, that will be as good as you'll get with that thickness glass. If the door is a 1-3/4" exterior SOLID SLAB door, the window part would limit you to around STC 40, with the low end TL being around 25 dB. HTH... Steve

Hi guys! Guess where I'm writing you from??? My newly built studio, of course. I KNOW, I KNOW, I KNOW......I have been a real a** about updating pictures. It's just been so hectic. Axe, thanks for the compliments and for reading thru all those pages! So, I just hung my guitars, got my midi going, etc....a few more pictures on the wall and a little bit more here and there of odds and ends and I'm officially done. But I AM sitting in here and it is 99% functional and WONDERFULLLLLL...... Real quick, I ran out of money on the budget (of course) and did not get the minisplit system yet. So how am I sitting in here when it is 11 degrees outside in New Hampshire? Well, while researching the splits I talked to a veteran HVAC guy who told me that the splits with backup heat strips are only 500 watts and not worth the extra (plus I discovered that those units all require 230 volts which I did not wire for - I needed to stay with the 110 volts and none of those have backup heat strips). He said if the unit did get too cold outside and the heat struggled many people just supplement the room with one of those sealed oil filled radiator looking things you can get at Walmart. He said those are 1500 watts alone and will fill in the gap if and when the minisplit heat would start to struggle in extremely cold temps. So, I went out and bought one to see what it would be like and if it would even do enough to allow me to move into this studio/barn this winter even BEFORE being able to afford the minisplit (I didn't want to move my gear in and find that I couldn't keep the place warm enough and damage anything) so I tested it out. It warms this place beautifully - sometimes I get too hot and am in shorts and a t-shirt while it is 10 degrees outside! I guess doublewalls and double drywall and a million inches of insulation (and so on) goes a long way. I moved my stuff in and all is well. My electric bill went up about $50 which is acceptable to me. The minisplit will go in this spring so that I don't boil this summer and it will be even nicer for winter next year having the proper heat as well. So, let me get working on those pictures for you. When I post them I'll start where I left off and go in stages. I'll see you soon!!
