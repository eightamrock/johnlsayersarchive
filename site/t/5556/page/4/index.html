At this point in your build, you only need to figure out roughly where the registers are going to go and where the main unit will be.
Kathy wrote:
•Insulated ducting comes in what sizes and brands and what exactly should I use?
See above. Do the research. You might call out an HVAC company and have them give you an estimate for a system. Just as a point of reference, in my studio (about 1500 sq ft, 5 rooms/areas to do, $6,000. That was the system, installation, ducting, permits, etc. You need to make SURE that they understand what you are trying to do because they are not going to have recording studio experience. As I said before, in a normal house installation, each room has a supply register and then one return register for the whole house. Since your rooms will (hopefully) be air tight, you'll have to have both supply and return vents in each room. They also need to know that there will be NO FRESH AIR in the rooms so ventalation will have to be put in/taken out of the A/C system or people will start dying from lack of oxygen.

Probably. I put two of each in my control room and main room, but only one each in my vocal booth and drum booth.
Kathy wrote:

Since the venting is so cheap (relatively speaking), I'd put the ducting/vents in the vocal booth. In a small room with the door closed, you can raise the temperature to an uncomfortable level in just a few minutes with only body heat.
Kathy wrote:
•Do I secure it with bracing or anything or just lay it on top of the plastic?
You should be able to get away with just laying it on the plastic. The flex ductiing I'm talking about is insulated (available at HD by the way). The only issue is that you don't want to kink it when you bend it up from the plastic to the vent in the floor. You also want to GENTLY "snake" it a little so there isn't a straight line from the register to the unit. Helps keep the sound from traveling down the ducting and into other rooms.

As to how big, this is where your research will give you the answers. The unit will have to go outside and be ducted inside. This not a DIY job. You'll have to take heating and A/C into account. No matter how mild your summers are, you are more than likely going to NEED A/C because your equipment and bodies in a sealed room WILL generate an enormous amount of heat. This is not something that can be easily (and effectively) retrofitted after the fact. If I were to take an educated guess, I'd suggest at a minimum, 1 return vent in the floor of each room and a supply vent in each room in the ceiling (and at the opposite end of the room). You want to use the biggest (within reason) ducting you can (I used 8" diameter except for the vocal booth which used 6"). Here is where you might have to educate your HVAC man because in homes these days, they like to use high velocity small diameter (like 4") ducting because they can run it between the studs. The problem is that the smaller diameter, the faster the air has to move through it to provide the same amount of air. Fast moving air is your enemy because the whistling WILL get into your microphones. 8" will probably be fine for you and this size will also have a nice slow air velocity. Your registers should probably NOT be the type where you can adjust the flow with a little handle because those little moveable vanes will also whistle.
len-morgan wrote:
You don't need to "design" the whole system right now but putting this ducting in the floor now while it's "easy" will save you some work later. Some things you just can't postpone.
You probably CANNOT get away will "room air conditioners" for a couple of reasons: 1) Isolation. If you look at the front of one, you can see daylight on the other side! You might as well just cut a hole in your wall. 2) Noise. They are extremely noisy. 3) No ventalation. These units recycle the same (stale) air over and over and there is no provision to get fresh air into the stream (or to take bad air out). I would suggest that see if you can find someone with a central HVAC system and take a good look at it. See how big it is, how the ducting works, etc. Perhaps your carpenter/policeman friend can take you on a field trip to see a few installations. If he does contruction for other people, he more than likely is friends with someone that installs HVAC equipment that might be willing to come over and give your place a look without having to have a contract in hand.
Kathy wrote:
Since I will have two rooms (control and live) I guess I will need two systems? Or one system that works both rooms - do they call that a split?
No, one central system. A "split" unit has part of the system ouside the building and part inside the building with a couple of small ducts (4 or 5") between the two. These are the same as "room units" or window units in that have NO provision for fresh air. It should also be noted that I think building codes everywhere require that there be a separate electrical box AT THE UNIT so that it can be disconnected and worked on safely (i.e., the service man doesn't flip the breaker in the house and then someone comes along and turns it back on while he's holding onto the wires). You'll probably need a 220 VAC connection for the A/C part too. You'll have to decide what kind of heat you'll need (electrical/oil/gas/nuclear reaction, etc). Hope that helps! len PLEASE don't make the mistake of thinking that you won't need fresh air.

rod gervais16 February 2006#78
Kathy, 1st let's talk duct - I don't really care for flext duct runs - they tend to compress in areas that we don't want compression - which means an increase in velocity - which means an increase in noise. You're much better off with something akin to John Mansville Super duct. If you're doing heat and AC - then you should have high returns and low returns - high for the sumer time - low for the winter time. Grilles in floors are not highly reccomended because they collect dirt and dust and mites - low returns are much better suited in the side walls. Fresh air is an issue - and should be ducted from the outside to just prior to the return air side of your air handler unit - which also requires a relief duct (again to the outside) be installed at leasst 10' away from that (also on the return air duct) this can be adjusted with a barometric damper to control the pressure release......... otherwise you will limit the amount of fresh air you can draw into the building. One nice - fairly inexpensive way you can acheive this would be if you have a spare area that you could use as a cooling/heating chamber - a seperate room that you could install a through the wall heat pump - or even just a standard thru wall air conditioner - along with a small boiler that ran to hot water baseboard in the chasmber. You could then just run your duct and let it use the room as a large plenum - use a fan only on the return duct - which tends to pull the sound away from you. Same thing in this case though - you want high and low supply runs inside the chamber - high and low return runs in the rooms - bacasue in summer you want to pull the cold air off the floor of the chamber - in winter the heat from near the ceiling - and just the opposite in the rooms for the return air. I hope this helps a bit. BTW - the disconnect has to be within view of the equipment - saves people from getting zapped. Rod.

Shar buddy, What it says is :
The glazed areas need not be openable where the opening is not required by Section R310 and an approved mechanical ventilation system is provided capable of producing 0.35 air change per hour in the room or a whole-house mechanical ventilation system is installed capable of supplying outdoor ventilation air of 15 cubic feet per minute (cfm) (7.08/L/s) per occupant computed on the basis of two occupants for the first bedroom and one occupant for each additional bedroom.
That is .35 air changes per hour - or one complete air exchange every 2 hours 51.6 minutes - let's call it one complete change every 3 hours. That is a little more reasonable. The "or" doesn't come into place here seeing as this is not a whole house system. Think for a moment about the amount of air you are heating (or cooling) and then just throwing out of the building (you have to exhaust eventually or you will stop pulling in fresh air) the energy costs would be enormous.
