knightfly21 March 2006#155
For your described modus operandi, there's nothing wrong with a single room approach - in fact, for the most part it's more efficient and less claustrophobic. Things you give up (besides the ones you already mentioned) are having a more live room to record in - a CR, in order to make sure you're not making your mixes too "dry", has to have pretty short decay times (these rooms are too small to actually have this called "reverb", but it has a similar effect on mix choices. In a 2-room setup, normally the live room has some actually usable acoustics (although seldom close to perfect) which become part of your recorded tracks. The CR is usually made more dead, so you can hear how much reverb there is in the tracks and add electronic 'verb accordingly. If the CR is too "wet", you'll not know your mix is too dry until you play it elsewhere. Live drums - I know you said you'll use electronic, but just in case - one definite improvement in efficiency when setting up a drum sound, is that you can listen to what the drums will sound like "on tape", without having to do a bunch of test recordings and listen to them WITHOUT the actual drums bleeding thru your walls and into your ears. Same with any OTHER acoustic recorded tracks, really; what you hear in the same room is NOT what is going to the recorder, so there can be a LOT of "cut and try" going on. A well-isolated separate room is the fix for all this time wasted. Another thing to consider - if you look at the second picture on your very first post in this thread, the roof line of the studio isn't set up so you could easily add on to the side where the roof is lowest, but you MIGHT be able to add on where the RV is parked in the pic; with a ceiling shape like that, your mix position should be facing away from the wall where the door is showing, so if you were to add a double-glazed CR window on the wall next to the RV, and just continue your roofline out in the direction of the RV, you could LATER add a live room and keep your larger, 1-room feel and working style going. Just a thought. That would leave you some options and still let you save $$ and start out working like you're used to... STeve

len-morgan31 March 2006#164
Kathy, By NOT building a 4th wall isolated from the rest, you are giving up a little on isolation (because the air gap between the inner and outer wall is smaller) but probably more importantly, where this wall joins the new walls, you are going to have a DIRECT connection to the structure of the building and therefore possibly blow the entire isolation you've worked so hard for. What you might be able to do, and I'd wait for real experts to chime in on this before proceeding, is build it like you would a ceiling on RC. In other words, you new walls would come up right to the edge of the old current wall but not physically attach to it, and the drywall in the corner would be calked but NOT mudded and taped together. That way they could vibrate independantly. On the other hand, you might be able to cover the old wall with RC and hang the dry wall on that. You'll only lose a couple of inches of floor space at most, get better isolation from the structure of the building (because of the RC) and increase your air gap (and therefore your isolation) by a couple of inches as well. If you've got 2x4 studs (really 3 1/2", then adding RC that's about 1 1/2 inches "high", would give you a total air gap of 5 inches which isn't too bad. As I said, please wait for confirmation ( or a thorough drubbing of my idea) from the experts before proceeding. Remember, I was the one that suggested you pull up and sell the old barn floor and sent you off in a couple of wrong directions that (thankfully) those wiser than me corrected you on. Now, to your earlier question about 1 room vs two, I'm going to comment on the idea of being "less than professional" because you don't have a "real" control room. Knightfly's comments about accoustics in the control room vs live room need to be taken to heart and Rod's comments about symetry are also valid. What you should remember as you work through this decision is my (and probably a lot of other people's) experience building our own studios: We all have a hammer and a saw but I can look at two wall sections, one built by me and one built by the carpenter/electrician that helped me and tell you who built which. And we both used the very same hammer and saw. What I'm getting at is what your rooms look like has almost nothing to do with the quality of the music you can produce there. If you can produce top quality music because of your talent, gear, luck, or whatever, the does it really matter where you did the recording? If you hire out your studio, someone should make the decision based on the music that's come out of, not the interior design of the space. If you put out a good product, it wouldn't matter to me if you did it in the men's room a Union Station. I don't see the recording space used when I listen to CDs - I hear the music. If someone is going to judge your space based on what it looks like or how it's layed out, they more than likely wouldn't know a quality recording if it jumped up and bit them. Take Sandersd's advice and build the space for YOU, not some potential future buyer. You can't live your life trying to please other (especially unknown other) people. Build the best space for you and enjoy it! len

len-morgan31 March 2006#169
Sorry to stir up the pot so much with my comments. However, all is not lost (or that expensive or difficult to make right). First, as to "building a wall a week..." and that being the reason you have to attach them to the structure of the building, that just isn't true. I built my entire studio (5 rooms, separate double stud walls, etc) and just leaned the frames up flat against the walls until it was time to "use" them. As I "folded" out each wall frame from it's temporary location, I nailed the corners to each other so they would all support themselves. After the walls were up, I attached the new ceiling joists between the trusses so they didn't touch the structure and attached my ceiling dry wall to these new joists. None of my walls touch the structure of the building at any point EXCEPT where the bottom plates are anchored to the cement slab. Yes, this will make me susceptable (sp?) to flanking through the slab from room to room, but since cement doesn't move quite as easily as the building ceiling trusses would, I accept that less than perfect construction. Your walls being connected to your new floor is the same sort of compromise. Not perfect (which would be a totally floating separate concrete floor a la Paul Woodlock) and there are few of us that would want to take on a job like that. I got tired just READING about his work! Now, in your situation, if you HAVE to connect your new walls to your old ones, you can use isolated sway bars (I think that's what they are called here) like the stuff Mason Industries and others make. In it's simplest form, it's too metal plates that are joined together with a piece of neopreme so that vibrations on your inner walls will dissipate a lot of their energy into the rubber before it can transfer to the outer wall. Think of it like the shock absorber in a car: It doesn't stop you from feeling EVERY pebble on the road, but it dampens what the tires "feel" from what you feel inside the car. If you retained anything from the reference material on this site and others it should be that you never, never, never want to join your inner and outer walls with a "hard" connection. That's like rule #1. These sway bars will take care of most of it. Now, is this a problem FOR YOU? Perhaps not. You say isolation is not an issue. You seem to have decided on a single large room instead of live and CR so your room to room isolation is a moot point, except for your iso booth. In that case, I would plan on making it a completely self supporting structure within the bigger room so you can really achieve some isolation from the rest of the room. Connection through the floor will probably be unavoidable unless you cut out the floor where the iso booth will be and build a separate floor just for the iso booth that goes all the way down to the ground (maybe standing on a cement pilar like you are building the rest of the floor on. Since the room will be relatively small, one fairly large one in the center of the room should give you a decent platform to build a floor on and then the walls of the iso room on top of that one. Again, it all is going to get down to how much isolation you want. As to the RS sound level meter, if you want to measure how much isolation you are getting now, it's probably close to worthless. The reason is that the lowest it can measure is 50 dB which is the same as loud conversation. You probably already know that your building is doing AT LEAST that much isolation. If you put a CD on and turned up the signal as loud as possible, you might be able to register 100+ dB on the meter and then go outside and measure 50 on the meter. This doesn't mean you have only 50 dB of isolation, just that you have AT LEAST 50 dB because you can't measure any lower than that. If you plan to get a program like ETF (or the new version "R&D something") it would be useable for that since you are measuring RELATIVE levels that are in a much narrower range. As to putting RC on all the walls (and ceiling), again, that's your call based on the isolation you are going to need, bearing in mind that it's not something that you can (easily) change later. If you don't have a real isolation problem (and don't forsee one in the future, like a change of neighbors), then it's probably not necessary. On the 4th wall, you wouldn't even have to worry about that since you've essentially hard attached your other 3 walls to the structure of the building. In my case, I have a house (currently vacant) less than 40 ft from the back of my building and a dentist's office about 20' away from one side of my building so isolation was/is a big factor for me and is why I made sure that all of my walls are totally isolated from the rest of the building structure (except the floor). This will give me the most isolation. Again, you've changed your design, (one room vs two) which is ok, but that also changes a lot of other factors in the construction. As I said above, room to room isolation is no longer an issue so you may be ok with the rest of what you have now. Last point, you wanted a "crow's nest" above your iso booth. I don't see any problem with that or any big engineering problems other that just making sure that the ceililng of the iso booth can handle the weight of someone/something being on top of it. However, I wouldn't recommend having someone up there WHILE you are recording in the iso booth. If you do, then you'll have to deal with impact noise from foot steps, etc. Maybe a simple way to solve this problem would be to put the stairway to get up there on the inside of the to the iso booth so if someone was in there with the door closed, someone else wouldn't have a way to climb up there. :-) Sorry for such a long post. len

Now, in your situation, if you HAVE to connect your new walls to your old ones, you can use isolated sway bars (I think that's what they are called here) like the stuff Mason Industries and others make. In it's simplest form, it's too metal plates that are joined together with a piece of neopreme so that vibrations on your inner walls will dissipate a lot of their energy into the rubber before it can transfer to the outer wall. Think of it like the shock absorber in a car: It doesn't stop you from feeling EVERY pebble on the road, but it dampens what the tires "feel" from what you feel inside the car.

Rod's book describes the process very well (and the right TYPE of RC as well). The only thing that's "difficult" about it is you have a smaller target to hit with the drywall screws. You also have to be VERY careful (and I don't remember if Rod mentioned this or not) that when you put the screw through the dry wall and into the RC, you want to make sure the screw doesn't go through the RC and touch the stud underneath it (otherwise you are making ANOTHER hard connection point). Since you run the RC perpendicular to the direction of the studs/joists, when you mark where your studs are, they become points to NOT put screws rather than where to put screws. If you look at the shape of the RC in Rod's book, you see that the RC is acting like a spring holding the DW so any motion (from music) is absorbed by the spring action and less is transfered to the studs and therefore to the entire structure. On the RS sound meter, i have one and that IS a frustrating thing about it, that it doesn't go below 50. but one way to use it i've seen mentioned recently that would work, and perhaps work better than the standard way, is to turn up the volume inside, see what that registers, and then see how low you have to turn down the volume inside before it is not noticeable outside. this inside sound should be well over 50db (we hope!) the way this is BETTER is that your using your own ear to determine what is acceptable leakage, not a meter. a meter can't really judge what your neighbor may or may not be bothered by, but you can. you could even generate tones at different frequencies inside, say 40hz, 50hz, whatever you want, to test low end iso, read the meter, then go outside to see if you can hear it. optimally, you'd want to create a pulsing on/off tone, because that would be a lot easier to hear than a steady tone. and is also the type of thing that a neighbor finds more noticeable and annoying.
