rod gervais14 March 2006#130
Kathy wrote:
But without a vapor barrier on the outside wall won't there be a lot of moisture that gets the insulation wet on the inside wall? :?:
No - without a vapor barrier on the outside wall the wall cavity will have exactly the same relative humidity as the outside air - which is exactly what you want to happen. When the cold outside air reaches a warm interior surface (the inner vapor barrier) it won't condense - so it stays dry.
Also, without paper backing how will I secure the insulation to the outside wall cavity? :?:
One easy way to do this is to apply nylon strapping to the inside face of the studs - the stuff used for wrapping shipping crates - you can get rolls at Home Despot. They also make metal stays for this work - which are about 16" long thin rods that you can buy at any shop that sells insulation (Perhaps even HD). Rod
So to recap:
    • I'm stuffing both wall cavities with insulation and there will be about a 2 inch gap of air between each insulated wall. • The outside exterior wall will be insulated with R19 and NO paper backed vapor barrier. • The inside newly built wall will be insulated with R19 that DOES have a paper backed vapor barrier that will face towards the drywall layer and inside of the room.
Yup - although (if it were me) I would use standard unfaced insulation on the interior face - and then apply a poly vapor barrier in lieu of the foil face. It's a much better vapor barrier without all of the little edges you have with the paper. Rod

Steve Oh, a bit more on the dynamic of vapor barrier - in a heated environment (whether by nature or by man) the heat tries to "even out" by traveling toward the colder location - when it does this, it carries any moisture (in the form of vapor) WITH it - so a heated room, when it loses heat thru the walls, also loses vapor INTO the walls - once there, it gets cold and condenses on the cooler surfaces. This leaves the inside of the wall WET, but with little or no air circulation, and stuff starts to rot/mold/mildew. This is why the vapor barrier ONLY goes to the heated side (inside in the North, Outside in the South) - That way, we don't allow MORE moisture to enter the wall from the heated space. There's more on this at the Building Sciences site, linked from the REFERENCE section... Steve

knightfly15 March 2006#133
This depends on the door you get - most typically run 2-1/2" larger in each dimension than the stated size of the door, but the best advice I can give you is BUY THE DOOR FIRST. Each manufacturer normally states the rough opening size, which can vary depending on the way they do their frame, etc. For a studio, solid slab EXTERIOR doors work a lot better than interior ones, and by "solid slab" I do NOT mean a "stile and rail" type with recessed panels, I mean a flat-sided, 1-3/4" thick SLAB of solid wood. A pre-hung exterior door with good quality threshold, etc, will give a lot better isolation - but don't "wimp out" on framing, these are heavy. I did a sketch of rough framing for another member here http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... 2&start=15 Check the drawing about half-way down that page. If this seems like overkill, remember that your seals will only work if the door doesn't sag after you've made the seals work. Here are a few more informational links that should be helpful - http://www.hometips.com/content/hinged_intro.html (The door shown in the picture is exactly what you do NOT want - nowhere NEAR as much mass as a full-thickness slab type door. You may have to look harder for the slab type door, but DO it; there's no comparison between the two types for sound control. Install guide - http://www.hometips.com/articles/door_install.html HTH... Steve

knightfly16 March 2006#138
Forgot to make this clear; there are two basic types of hardware and frames and doors - referred to as "interior" and "exterior". Interior doors, frames, hinges, and some doorknobs, are all based on a door thickness of 1-3/8" - most interior doors are either of the "stile and rail" type or a flat (but HOLLOW) type. These are nearly worthless for sound control as is (but work pretty good as a SINGLE door when modified per my directions in the REFERENCE section on doors) Exterior doors, frames, hinges, etc, are all based on a nominal door thickness of 1-3/4", not 1-3/8" like the interior versions - the hinges are wider, deeper, thicker, just generally beefier because code requires exterior doors to be "solid core" - With the exception of the SLAB type doors you found on Ebay, a lot of these doors are a joke security-wise, and sound wise too. Some of these "solid" panels (in between the stiles and rails of the thicker part of the door) are only maybe 1/4" to 3/8" thick - so while they're "solid", they're still WIMPY... Main reason for this post is to make sure you watch what you buy for hardware - hinges and frames aren't all the same, and you need to match everything carefully. That's one of the reasons the pre-hung doors are so popular with everyone; you just slide the whole thing into your rough opening, snug it up with shims, check for level and plumb a few dozen times, then drive some fasteners thru into the rough frame, put on wallboard, add molding and you're done. (BTW, this WON'T work for studio the same way; where those shims go in a normal house, so follows the sound. This, and any extruded door jams, etc, must be filled with mass or it's "leakin' lena" time. Gotta run, hope this helps... Steve

knightfly16 March 2006#147
Oh, sorry; missed one of your questions - door size. The main problem with doors, assuming they're heavy slabs, isn't the size of the door; it's the seal. A solid core door at 1-3/4" thick has a bit more mass than two layers of 5/8" drywall, so the m-a-m performance would be a bit better than two layers of drywall each side; this is true whether a 2 foor or a 4 foot wide door, the only thing being that the wider door would have a bit more seal, therefore a bit more loss. The upside of wider doors is moving gear in and out; I have 4 foot doors in my old studio, and I would never do anything less (at least for a live room) - it's just so nice being able to move large bass amps, pianos, organs, etc, without having to stop and say "gee, which way can we turn this so it'll go through the door??" Just a thought... Steve
