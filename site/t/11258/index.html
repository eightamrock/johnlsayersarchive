The case of the dissappearing Darryl and Dan Show!! Well, at lest you guys are back, with a different thread! Anyway... Hi Darryl & Dan, and welcome aboard! I'm probably NOT the best person to be helping you out here, since I'm also just starting out on my own build myself, and learning as I go, (its kind of like "the blind leading the blind" here!), but I noticed a couple of things in your post (or rather your Word document) where I think you might not be on the right track. My comment is about your walls, not so much your floor (I'm not too clued up on floors yet). Your best bet with the walls is definitely the right hand option in your diagram on page 8. This much I have learned You want ONLY two chunks of mass, and ONLY one air gap in between, partially filled with rockwool, fiberglass, or some such material. If I'm reading it right, the plan you described on page 5 is actually going to give you four leaves with three air gaps. Not good. I've also learned that RC (resilient channel) is not worth the cost. Neither is mass loaded vinyl. Just go with lots of layers of sheetrock (plasterboard, gypsum board, whatever it is called in your neck of the woods) on each side. It's relatively cheap, and it is massive. In other words, you should be thinking of 2 or 3 or 4 layers of sheetrock on the inside of the studs on "your" side of the wall you are building, and this should be nailed/screwed directly to the studs. No RC or anything else in between. Then a big air gap (several inches), partially filled with insulation of some sort, then the studs on the "other" side of the wall (also with insulation), and fianlly 2 or 3 or 4 layers of sheetrock on the "outside" of those studs. Just like the right hand diagram on your page 9, but with more layers of sheetrock. So what you are aiming for is "M-A-M", which means "Mass -Air - Mass". That's all you need. Lots of mass on one side, then a big air gap, then lots of mass on the other side. And you should use some gunk called "green glue" in between the layers of sheetrock. http://www.greengluecompany.com/ It isn't really glue at all: it is an exotic acoustic polymer that remains flexible and "damps" the walls, increasing your isolation greatly, especially at very low frequencies. I've also been told here that there simply is no substitute for Green Glue. You can't use anything else. If you can't get the real Green Glue, then its better to just nail/screw the sheetrock layers directly together, with nothing in else in there. So I have heard... I guess you could substitute MDF or plywood for one layer of sheetrock, but I'm not sure what that would do for you acoustically, and I doubt it would be any cheaper. The general consensus here is just to go for lots of layers of sheetrock. Oh, and regarding your question about what to build each of the two timber frames on top of: You want as little mechanical connection as possible between the leaves, so I'd say to put the "outer" one (the one that your neighbors in the print shop will see) on top of their floor, and the "inner" one on top of YOUR floor, whatever it turns out to be. And speaking of floors, I don't think it's a good idea to have any air gaps in your floor, since basically you are "floating" it like that, which I have learned is a really big no-no! But like I said, I'm not too clued in on floors yet: I've learned what NOT to do with them, but still haven't figured out what you actually CAN do. One final suggestion: You are going to be piling up an awful lot of mass in your walls and floors: Probably a couple of tons, by the time you are done. You might want to check with a structural engineer to make sure that the underlying building structure can take all that extra weight! It would be really sad if you built a great-sounding, fantastically soundproof studio on the third floor, only to find it down on the first floor one day.... OK, that's my 2 cents worth for now. Good luck with your build! Stuart
