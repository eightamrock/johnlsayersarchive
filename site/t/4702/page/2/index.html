sharward25 November 2005#39
Cool -- I'm glad we're all friends. :-) As far as the horse trailer mat idea goes... Maybe, maybe not. Yes, it is rubbery and might work as an elastomer. However, if I were going to put pucks in my design (which I was originally going to do), I would want those pucks to have some credible testing and specifications behind them. Unfortunately, those mats don't have either. :-( Maybe you're OK with that. However, the risk is that they might not work well for that application -- and even if they do, there's no assurance for how long. I would not want to rely on an untested, experimental, cheap product on which to rest my entire plan, both literally and figuratively. Regardless of what elastomer you use for a floating floor, make absolutely sure you do all the math. What math? For starters, there's the math that ensures you won't overload your elatometers, because overloading could cause premature wear (meaning, they may only last months or days instead of years), or, worse, bottoming out, which would render them useless. Underloading is also detrimental -- they may not spring enough or at all, rendering them useless. There's also the math to ensure that the floor you are floating will have an acceptable resonance frequency. Here's where I'm actually way beyond my expertise -- what I do know is that I don't know enough to do that on my own, and, no offense intended of course, you probably don't either. Not that there aren't people here who can help with that, though, if you decide to go there. That's not to say it's impossible or a bad idea to float. It just means that if you proceed down that path, you need to be prepared to do all the planning and calculations to ensure that the results will indeed be better than if you hadn't floated at all. For what it's worth, though, I think you're definitely on the right track in your "decoupled" thinking. That's all for now... I've got to get back to my concrete manicuring! :roll: --Keith :mrgreen:
