doublehelix31 May 2008#9
Yuck! Lots of work ahead there my friend! I look at how far you have to go, and just shudder! You say that you bought a two-family house and occupy the first floor... AND that you are planning on renting out the top floor. You will need to reduce sound transmissions to the second floor for sure. This is a lot more work, and will probably require a room-within-a-room design to keep your sound from traveling upstairs. It also works however to keep heavy footfalls/phone rings/dog barks, etc. from leaking into your recordings from the upstairs tenants. This is not trivial!!! Be warned! Sound will leak through the windows, flank through the structure, etc. Even sound in the basement will travel through walls via flanking, through HVAC ducting, through every little nook and cranny, etc. I think you might want to consider going room-within-a-room, and even that will allow some sound to escape. You are going to need more than a beefy ceiling to prevent sound leakage! Beware the walls and flanking!!! Sound will creep EVERYWHERE! I also would put some extra time and energy into the HVAC system. Trust me on this one!!! Nothing worse than a stuffy room to stifle creativity and create tension, even with 20 minute breaks every couple of hours! One other suggestion from my experience: The control room can NEVER BE BIG ENOUGH!!! I knew my CR was going to be small, but by the time I got the room-within-a-room construction done, it had shrunk to a mere 10' 2" X 12' 9". OUCH!!! I have since spent a fortune getting it to sound good. If you need the vocal booth, I understand, but it comes at the price of sacrificing CR space. Plus, a small and cramped vocal booth REALLY SUCKS as well!!! I get claustrophobia in a small vocal booth. Maybe you can design your place so that the vocalist can go in the basement, and you can keep the extra room upstairs for the CR. Makes for easier construction, gives you more room in the CR, gives the vocalist more room in the basement to prevent claustrophobia, AND gives a bit more isolation to the vocal track to keep out stray extraneous noises like dog barks and phone rings. Just a thought! 1) Three 20 amp circuits is MORE THAN ENOUGH, and is probably overkill. Consider a star-grounding system as described in Rod's book. A sub panel is not a bad idea, but check with an electrician. 2) With the drywall, go for the more mass rather than worry about different resonant frequencies. I think if they layers are smashed together as they are with the drywall it becomes less important, but I may be wrong here. I know that I was advised to go with 2 layers of 5/8" rather than one 5/8" and one 1/2". 3) As far as the insulation, I used the pink fluffy stuff between my studs as well,and it seems to be pretty solid and quiet. 4) As I recommend a room-within-a-room design, the windows are going to have to go. Or alternatively, take a look at my build thread at the "ugly window" to see what I did to mine. I basically added another pane of glass to the "inside room" so that I could keep the outside window for some natural light. 5) Surface-mount electronics are fine, but ***I THINK*** you can and should still run the wires through the walls. Check on this with an expert before proceeding!!! Use conduit throughout the build, and just make VERY small holes in the drywall to bring the wires through, then seal those holes tighter than a gnat's behind! You might need to add a junction box behind each wall hole... Remember, "a hole is a hole", whether it is in the drywall or in the floor. Sound will escape through either opening. Get an electrician's help, it is what I did, and I am so glad that I did. 6) Regarding the floor... keep the hardwood, and get some nice rugs to throw down. I would not carpet the entire floor as some reflections are nice. Plus, you can always pull some of the rug back if need some reflections, like with an acoustic guitar for example. 7) Legal issues always suck. Remember, they are underpaid public servants, and they get their joy by being the "big boss". My suggestion: Let them act like the "big boss", and things will go much more smoothly. Best of luck!!! I don't envy you starting out like this! It has been a long, long road for me, and I am very glad to be coming to the end of the line.

brianrcdd31 May 2008#18
Some quick info on the power - Most homes in the US have 2 legs of power coming from the pole, with 120 volts on each leg. The third wire is common and is grounded. The AC is 180 degress out of phase which essentially means the voltage across both power legs is the sum of the two or 240 volts. 240 is then available for high power appliances like water heaters, stoves, AC, etc. Note that some locations use 110/220 volts instead of 120/240, but the principle is the same. More information can be found here: http://www.rd.com/19509/article19509.html So connecting to one of those legs is not "safer", it's your only option if you don't want to send 240 volts into your gear. A typical breaker panel distributes the various loads evenly by connecting some circuits on each of the legs. But unless you intentionally wire across both, You'll never get more than 110/120 at any given outlet. So I'm not sure what your electrician was getting at.

gullfo3 June 2008#24
in the US, power comes in (typically) on 2 110v legs for a total of 220v. then each half of the power box is split across these. if you wire your studio across both legs, you increase the possibility of having 110-220v potential if there is some defect in the wiring or equipment. having the entire studio run off a single leg will reduce the possible malfunction to 110v which most equipment (but people cannot) can deal with (because you have less risk of 2 legs (meaning someone put hot on the neutral) coming through unless the wiring is really screwed up)...
