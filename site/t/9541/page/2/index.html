You can not make a 0 less 0, no matter how much absorption you apply, still these dips become less deep. And for others: The simplest axial mode (say first order mode) creates a plus at one end of the room (over pressure) and a minus (under pressure) at the other side of that room. This plus/minus oscillates in function of the frequency. But in the center the crossover is always the point where you go from plus to minus (and vice versa), meaning that you always cross that 0 point (this nul as often referred to) somewhere in the center. No matter the strength of that standing wave (hence no matter how much absorption you apply) that center ALWAYS crosses that 0 point (- infinite dB). So why becomes that dip less deep by applying absorption? That 0 always remains a 0, no matter what. Whether I go from +1000 to -1000, or from +50 to -50, I always cross that 0 (used just arbitrary numbers here). This is directly related with this whole discussion, namely that that point shifts towards the reflecting wall (because that reflection has lower amplitude) and after being reflected becomes the stronger incident wave, causing that point to shift back towards the other wall. The more absorption you apply the larger this area becomes over which this 0 (max kinetic energy) goes back and forth and the more it averages itself with the pressure part of the wave. Hence that wave 0 point moves forth and back, while the STATIC interference pattern itself (that dip) just becomes less deep and less explicit with the neighboring area. You really can picture that applying absorption in function of modes cause that point of maximum kinetic energy (that 0) to walk around over a larger becoming area. But it's much easier to understand/feel this difference between static interference patterns and the waves themselves, when animated.

lovecow wrote:
We're not talking about changing the laws of physics here. We're just talking about using a consistent nomenclature.
My first encounter with the term "standing wave" was in 1961 when I was 13 years old and into ham radio. At the time I hung out at the transmitter shack of a local 50 KW radio station, where a friend of my older sister was the engineer. That's where I learned that standing waves are caused by reflections at the point of an impedance mismatch. In the case of radio, the reflections happen in a length of coax cable, for example where it terminates at the antenna. If the coax impedance and the antenna impedance are not precisely matched, some of the waves reflect back down the coax toward the transmitter source. In a high-powered radio transmitter this is a big problem because the result is power loss and overheating. Then in the late 1970s I became interested in acoustics while building my big pro studio. That's when I learned the exact same thing happens acoustically in the air. Sound reflecting off a wall goes back into the oncoming sound source and creates standing waves in the air. In this case the impedance mismatch is the difference between the air and wall. But the principles are identical, and neither case requires resonance. Standing waves also happen in a length of water pipe, and that may be easier for folks not familiar with electronics to visualize. Imagine you have a large water pipe, and a metal washer with a small hole is soldered to the end of the pipe as a cap. In this case the pipe diameter is suddenly reduced to the washer's hole diameter, so some of the water reflects off the face of the washer back into the oncoming water stream. This is a very close physical analogy to what happens with radio waves and sound waves when they are reflected back into the oncoming source. When I first heard it suggested that standing waves are resonances, I knew that to be false. Now, I don't know if the radio version or acoustic version of a standing wave was defined first. But it's clear to me that a standing wave is a wave, while a resonance is something very different. And that's how we got to where we are today. :shock: --Ethan
