knightfly26 June 2006#61
Who's confused? About what? Oops, I just got lost in thought; it wasn't familiar territory... :? OK, all betur now :arrow: Soooo, how tall are you planning your block wall? How tall will the 3-layer gyp wall be, and is this 3 layers of 5/8? What kind of soil do you have? (Nooo, not "dirty" - answers could include sand, clay, rock, loam, etc... Have you decided on a roof/ceiling construction to match your walls and doors, and if so, what? All these will allow you to calculate the total weight that would be resting on your footings - from there, you'll need to involve a local engineer that knows soils, compaction, etc - he will be able to give you a spec for your footings - expect something in the range of at least 24" wide by 8" deep, probably more. Be sure to mention that you intend to FILL the blocks with sand - also, are you still considering the dry-stack, bonded method? That adds a small amount of weight to the calculations too, because there's an extra 1/2" of masonry product on EACH SIDE of the block wall. HTH... Steve

sharward4 July 2006#70
The 12" air gap will actually be about 16" when you factor in the depth of the studs on your inner leaf. Not that it's a bad thing -- if you can afford the space, then make it so. The farther apart the leaves, the better the isolation. I wish I could afford three feet like Steve's going to do (eventually)... :roll: You will want to pour your new concrete on top of a rigorously prepared compacted base. I used 3/4" crushed granite, hand-tamped, then machine tamped. Rod Gervais recommends a base of sand as I recall in his book (which is what I probably would have done if I had the benefit of the book before I prepped my soil). Your new "beefy footing" isn't very beefy, according to that drawing. It looks like it's only about 6" thick. It should be a minimum of one foot, and possibly much deeper. I believe you have to go deeper than the frost line. This is critical due to frost heave: "Frost heave is created by the freezing and thawing of water in the soil. Ground surface movements of 5-15 cm are common and movements of as much as 30 cm have been observed during a freeze-thaw cycle. Differential frost heave can cause severe distortion of structures which may not return to their original position at the end of the thaw season because the underlying voids have been filled with soil or trapped water." (Source: http://www.north-rthn.org/FactSheets/94_214.html) Your existing slab doesn't have rebar in it -- does it? Assuming it doesn't -- how do you intend to get rebar into the existing slab? --Keith :mrgreen:
