Hmmm... I think you're referring to the part of the footing that is visible above the slab or ground. The footing is much deeper than that -- maybe 2 feet, maybe longer -- definitely below the frost line. The depth is important, but the integrity of the soil and whether or not there is steel reinforcement is even more important.
Settled so much that there's a lot of earth-to-wood contact with this old building.
Not good. :(
How deep should the slab be to support the weight of the drywall and framing? It's had cars parked on it and I don't see any cracking or splitting. I also know that it was poured within the last 5-10 years so it's not old like the footing.
Structural slabs (like you'd find in today's houses) are typically a minimum of 6" thick and have a 6" x 6" 10 guage wire mesh. I kicked it up a notch in my case and used 1/2" rebar on 12" centers. I also used a five sack mix (code calls for a minimum of 4 sack) and I added fiber mesh to the mix which increases its strength significantly. Some would say you need either steel or fiber mesh, but I did both. I didn't want cracks and so far I have none.
Do those scissor trusses put most of their weight on the concrete walls/footing? I'm assuming so.
Yes, which is why they need to be strong. All the weight of the entire building (not including the slab) is transferred to the walls, and then to their sole plates... So the number of pounds per square foot (or, rather, square inch) is significant. Yes, car tires can weigh 1,000 pounds or more counting the vehicles they are supporting... But the building is even heavier. Plus, if a garage slab fails, you pull the cars out and pour a new slab -- not simple, but not rocket surgery. If a foundation fails............ :roll: ...........You're in for a world of hurt.

knightfly wrote:
If you're starting from scratch (and I do mean scratch) and don't want to mess with floated floors, I'd seriously consider an ISOLATED slab similar to what Sharward's doing - in your case, I'd put up heavy footings for the outer perimeter, use surface bonded block on top of that, fill the block with sand, then pour a separate, monolithic slab inside that perimeter leaving at least 12" of air gap, and use 3 layers of 5/8 sheet rock on 2x6 studs 24" OC for your inner walls. Doors to match, with an airlock large enough to only need one door open at a time. Of course you'd need to equal that performance with your roof/ceiling (not an easy task) but it's doable if you start out with that goal in mind - Surface-bonded block info http://www.quikrete.com/diy/QUIKWALLSur ... ement.html http://www.thenaturalhome.com/drystackblock.htm HTH...
