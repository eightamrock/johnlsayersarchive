Aloha and welcome to the forum, Aaron :D Congrats on your build! Glad to hear that your wife is on board as well. Since no one else has chimed in yet, may I offer a few thoughts on your project?
A soundproofed practice studio (that isn't permanent) for a loud rock band.
You are right. Your list is not for the faint at heart! To the best of my understanding, it appears that "soundproofed" and "not permanent" are not compatible. In the principle outlined on the forum to accomplish "soundproofed," it requires mass-air-mass (and lots of it) and a well executed construction (as in a screw - not to mention more often than not, several) in the wrong place can defeat the whole exercise. :shock: This usually results in a very heavy, airtight structure, which so far has not been accomplished by anything that could be considered "non-permanent." Given that understanding, it may require one to re-assess the expectations one might hope to accomplish. Which of the two is more important - soundproofed or temporary - and amiable relations with neighbors/local law enforcement or not? :roll: Here's a couple of attachments on the STC charts available here to give you an idea of what the construction looks like. STC Chart in left tab: http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/index.html http://www.stcratings.com/assemblies.html#01 Similar "beef up" projects: http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=2921 http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=3876 Hope these help, Aloha 8)

guitardad7223 March 2006#8
I'll kind of repeat what John just said: When the police are called the sound level reading they will take will be at you property line(s). Depending (if there in a bad mood) they can take levels at any one of your property lines. I make most of my living playing "blue collar" gigs... that's pubs, clubs, bars, poolside, weddings etc... I've been sound level checked many, many times by the police or by concerned club owners predicting a complaint will be called in. 55-60db at property line is typical by law. With a sound meter on "A" weight you can get that reading with out playing any music if there is a busy street or other normal noise in the area. You may be able to "cheat" a bit if at the late hours the ambient noise is louder then 45db... but that also depends on the temperament and sound knowledge or ignorance of your neighbors. When you get your sound meter "play with it" and you'll get a much more realistic idea of how much sound proofing you may need. I think -80db is bit of an over shot. 120db is really, really loud. I measured one of my loudest live groups and on "C" weight (higher bass sensitivity then "A" weight) we only peaked at 105db at back of the room... average was 95-100. Good luck.

knightfly1 April 2006#12
I have to take exception to Dan's comment here - "i would not bother caulking those existing cracks in the exterior. if you add 2 well caulked layers of drywall it will be airtight, and airtight is airtight." If you do NOT seal up that outer wall before adding the gypsum inserts, you've just given away 3/4" of wood mass that's about equal to another layer of 1/2" drywall for your outer leaf. Yours is probably the LAST case where that would be a good idea. You need to also check SPL's with "A" weighting - the difference in the two readings will help you learn how much of your sound is LOW frequency, which will require wider air gaps, more mass, etc, to bring under control. If that 126 dB is at 50 hZ, since the threshold of human hearing at that frequency is around 55 dB you'll need about 70 dB of TL at 50 hZ - Not gonna happen in any retrofit, and definitely not "temporary". More like 12" concrete, 24" air space and 4 layers of 5/8 drywall, with a super-ceiling that can keep up with that (oh, and a floated floor with the inner walls resting on it) Not trying to burst your bubble so much as to keep you from being disappointed... Steve

The problem with cutting slabs in an effort to isolate them -- something I considered briefly before being read the riot act by Rod the construction engineer -- is that you would then be putting a lot of weight on the edge of a cut slab, as opposed to a footing that is deeper and stronger. You also run the risk of settling. See this thread, which was instrumental in my decision to extract and replace my slab. I'm not saying you need to do the same thing that I am doing... But you'll at least see some of why I decided to do so. Do you have acreage that could accommodate a new building? knightfly2 April 2006#17
Yes, that's what I meant - only solid concrete. In order to completely stop high levels of low frequency sound you need to REALLY utilize the mass-air-mass principle - increasing ANY of the components will help isolation by lowering the resonance of the structure. Ideally, you want this resonance to be at least an octave and a half below the lowest frequency you want to contain, so for a full-spectrum barrier the m-a-m frequency needs to be below 10 hZ; preferably below 7 hZ, but that's nearly impossible for any affordable construction. If you're limited on the amount of mass you can afford, you can help things out somewhat by increasing the air gap. This can be fairly practical if you're starting from scratch and have the space to make your building about 4 feet larger on a side than you need for inside space - you just make a wide air gap and two seriously heavy wall leaves. Probably the most practical way of doing this would be to use 8x8x16" hollow concrete blocks for the outer shell - this can be done either by an experienced mason in the normal, mortar each block method, or by a newer method called by either "dry stack block" or "surface-bonded block" construction. A Google search on those two terms will bring up several links for better understanding. Either way, all block cavities not already filled with grout/concrete during the wall construction, will need to be filled with dry sand before the roof goes on; inner walls can be placed 24" inside the outer block wall, built with standard stud construction, and 3 or more layers of drywall placed on one side of the frame; this gives a wall that is 2 leaf, with enough mass and air space to achieve 14 hZ resonance, so good isolation from about 30 hZ upward. The trick would then be to equal that performance with the ceiling, which will be harder by far; one way to approach this is really tall outer walls, so you have enough room to put a massive inner ceiling frame, one that will support 3 or 4 layers of drywall, and enough air space above that to help out the m-a-m formula, and an outer shell that's equally massive. None of this is cheap, so unless your stated budget is REALLY flexible we may both be wasting our time with this... Steve

knightfly5 June 2006#25
If you're starting from scratch (and I do mean scratch) and don't want to mess with floated floors, I'd seriously consider an ISOLATED slab similar to what Sharward's doing - in your case, I'd put up heavy footings for the outer perimeter, use surface bonded block on top of that, fill the block with sand, then pour a separate, monolithic slab inside that perimeter leaving at least 12" of air gap, and use 3 layers of 5/8 sheet rock on 2x6 studs 24" OC for your inner walls. Doors to match, with an airlock large enough to only need one door open at a time. Of course you'd need to equal that performance with your roof/ceiling (not an easy task) but it's doable if you start out with that goal in mind - Surface-bonded block info http://www.quikrete.com/diy/QUIKWALLSur ... ement.html http://www.thenaturalhome.com/drystackblock.htm HTH...
