Osse wrote:
I wonder, is it neccessary to install two doors of this design, or is it going to be overkill? I know it's no use of doors better than the walls, but with these walls I think I will get rather high TL and the use of heavy door designs might be justified. another question is: is this kind of dual cleat/weather strip design neccessary, or is just a single one okay?
That's all a matter of opinion... In my opinion, doors are the weakest link in the entire build process. While the theory of not needing any more mass than your walls and a single seal, is generally accepted as fine, but in practical terms, I disagree. Unless you are a master carpenter, or employ a master carpenter for your doors, there will be imperfections in the door assembly that reduce the overall effectiveness of the seal. It may be .5mm gap or something not perfectly square. I'm finding that adding a bit of additional mass, using the double seal, double caulking edges, etc. will make up for the real world of imperfections.
second question: regarding framing around the door, .... Whats the purpose of the header above the door? Isn't it enough to just install a regular piece of frame above the door? Is is neccesary to use 3 vertical studs on both sides of the door? Of what reason, if you have horizontal ones strenghtening them up I mean isn't it enought with 2 of them?
With Rod's Superdoor and/or any decently built isolation door it's going to weight at least 350 lbs... up to 500 lbs, or more! You need that frame stiff and massive enough to hold that mass perfectly level for the next 30 years. Standard framing lumber is NOT going to handle that mass without sagging, twisting or distorting in one plane or another. IMHO, you cannot provide too much mass, bracing or stiffness to the frame. As an example, here's my loading dock door framing;
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.dmmobile.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2008/01/dscf0243.JPG
The header is a 2x8 box frame filled with R-19 insulation and caulked. The hinge side is the left side with 5 2x8's. The right side is 2 2x8's. The door frame which went in, is an additional 2x8 frame. All of the interior door frames are boxed 2x12 headers filled with insulation and caulked, with double 2x6 vert studs with 5/4" stock door jambs.
3rd question: ...When opening the door, if very heavy, the weight of the wall is changing from vertical to horizontal to some extent, will the wall be able to handle the extra weight from the door when opened, if using 6 of those hangers for a wall approx 4 meters(13 feet) long and 3.5(11,6 feet) high, with widow and 3 LAYER drywall?
Again, you want the frame to be stiff and massive enough NOT to allow that change of weight to occur. If it does, eventually, that door will likely come down!! OR possibly worse... the door AND part of the wall! If that happens, then there's the real possibility that part of the ceiling will come down too!!!

The header serves two(2) purposes. The one most important to you in this situation is the header maintains the proper distance from stud to stud, prevents bowing or twisting of the studs in the door at rest position od the door open position. The primary purpose is to support weight loads as vertical members are spanned across openings. Depending on the door(s) you purchase or build, the weight can be considerable. By that I mean it can be high! This is one reason why the use of a double stud is recommended, to maintain a straight frame that will carry the loads placed on them. Frame it the way you are seeing it framed and you will have a better door opening then trying to modify or save a few boards here and there. My door has 1/8 inch lead, 1/2 inch densguard sheetrock and 3/4 inch cedar laminated on it. It is heavy!!! edit: I see that MadMax is posting while I am making this post...he has some good pictures of how this is correctly framed including the blocking at 48 inches from the ground up to maintain the rigidity of the frame.

Just a note, Doors shouldnt be looked at as just a door, if not as a whole system. For instance, a 55 STC rated door, will end up finally at just STC49 when properly installed and sealed with apropiate rubber seals and stops. That means, you NEED to make your door not only the same mass as your walls, but a lot more! A STC40 rated door will end up to STC36 in the perfect scenario, so that will be even worse with real life imperfections.
