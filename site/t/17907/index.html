Soundman202020 October 2012#3
I agree with Brien. If there is no need for isolation, then I don't understand the purpose of the walls. Is that maybe just to change the dimensions of the room to a better ratio? Or is there another purpose that you didn't mention? Either way, if you do put drywall on that framing, you will still have created a tuned MSM system, and it will still resonate at some frequency. The frequency depends mainly on the surface density of the drywall and the depth of the cavity, and it may or may not be a frequency that is useful to you, for treating the room. Basically, you will have a room surrounded by tuned panel traps. If they are tuned low enough then they probably won't have too much effect on the room acoustics. So the question is: what frequency will they be tuned to, and is that useful for treating your modal response? You could vary the cavity depths and panel masses to treat several frequencies, if you wanted to get fancy, but that might not be worthwhile. But to answer your question: If you do not put insulation in the cavity, then the internal resonances in the cavity will not be damped. You'll have a panel trap that doesn't trap very well: it will just vibrate. It will still remove some energy from the room at that frequency, of course, but at the same time it will probably also cause some ringing, which isn't a good thing. So I'd say that you still need insulation in the cavity to damp the resonance, even if you don't need isolation. Also, please read the forum rules for posting (click here). You seem to be missing a couple of things! :) So I guess the conclusion is that if you don't need isolation and don't need to change the room dimensions, you should probably take out that framing, but if you do need to change room dimensions, then fill the gaps with insulation, as for any other tuned device. Another option you might want to consider: use the framing to turn those walls into slot walls, and for other types of treatment. - Stuart -

Slot walls sound like a really smart idea. But Styrofoam is just as useless for slot walls as it is for tuned MSM walls, so it has to go. Also, you need access to the actual concrete behind the Styrofoam in order to seal it properly, which is yet another reason why the Styrofoam has to go. In order for the slot walls to work, the wall has to be sealed air-tight, so you need to coat it with something that will accomplish that. If it is in good condition, and has no major cracks, then a simple coat of paint might be enough. But if it is poorly finished, then a coat of mortar/stucco/plaster would be the solution, followed by paint. Since a slot wall is basically a Helmholtz resonator, the cavity does need to be totally sealed. very important. - Stuart -

xSpace16 November 2012#16
The trick, etrelstad, is to build a room by conventional means, taking all the known ramifications of physics as it applies to a structure, and to do this in such a way as to relieve yourself of the obligation of being mindful of the thermal environment you just created. Tricky statement I know. Acoustics is a word...but it applies to everything in different ways in a thermal environment and the two often share responsibility for the benefits of either reduction in sound or higher thermal environment. Take for example, the decoupled wall assembly we so highly prize. Not only is that an acoustical break, it is also a thermal break. You only need to look at a double insulated window or a thermal coffee mug to understand this principle. We have people that sell us acoustics and we have people that sell us thermal ...the two are the same in respect to how does it function. It functions because of the break we discuss as the path vibration rides on, but this is also the path that thermal exchange rides on. So in answer to your question, yes I would paint it since this is a good vapor retarder. What I would not do is to try to stop the migration of condensation, which is what I had said up topic.
