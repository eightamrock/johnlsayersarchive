Ok... that link isn't really what I was talking about. I know all about the blocks you put in the window to keep the glass in, that wasn't my question. But the reason that picture isn't what I was talking about is because the peices of glass aren't shown at an angle... and I know the "to angle or not to angle" discussion is as old as time itself, but I've decided I'm going to angle my glass. Right here the guy says "Don't worry about angling the glass except for redirection of early reflections at the mix position; flutter echo off the glass will likely be solved by rear absorption in a small room anyway. Only other reason to splay the glasses is visual glare, which can be controlled with lighting position - " So his descriptions and diagrams are great if you're making your glass horizontal. And I totally disagree that you can control the visual reflections with different lighting positions. I've got some of the most customizable lights in my control room that I've ever seen. I have infinite possibilities for lighting, and over about a 1 year time frame, I could never eliminate the hall of mirrors effect of having the glass horizontal. That's when I switched the temporary plexy glass to angled. All my visual reflection problems went away. Before anyone flames at me about that decision, I offer some solid reasons. First is this:
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.saecollege.de/reference_material/images/Windows.gif
My other reason is I have experience with angling or not angling the glass. How so you may ask? I've had temporary plexy glass in my CR window for the last 2 years, and I've tried having both sides horizontal like Auralex suggests, and I've tried having them both angled like SAE suggests. With them both horizontal you see about 4 reflections of yourself before you see into the live room. And if the lights are off in the live room you can hardly see out at all, it might as well be one way glass. But when I angle both sides of the glass, well then it's great. Only at just the right angle can you see even one reflection, but best of all, you're taking the hall of mirrors effect right out of there. If you do see a reflection, you only see one, not 4. Having said that, in the picture I just posted from SAE, they show the glass as a 2 pixel wide line. Well, my glass isn't going to be 2 pixels wide, it's going to be 5/8" and 1" thick argon-filled. Take something that's 1" wide and set it on the same angle the glass is in the SAE picture. You'll see that the glass itself is sitting up on its own corner. My question is, is that bad for the glass? I've been building my studio for almost 6 years now, so I've thought all of this through a lot... except the "glass sitting on its own corner" thing.

bassman13 February 2006#5
It has been shown that angling your glass will not improve your isolation. It is much better to leave them parallel and maximize the distance between the two panes. Use a thickness that equals the same weight as your wall construction per sq ft. With the angled glass, the narrowest dimension becomes the weakest link in the structure. However, angling of the glass can help the inner acoustics when the pane poses a reflection problem in the control room. -ashley
