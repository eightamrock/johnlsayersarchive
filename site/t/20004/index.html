OK, so high level of isolation is necessary. It would be good if you could put numbers to that (in terms of how many decibels of isolation you need). OK, about your questions...
1. For a freestanding building with concrete slab on grade I won't probably need a floating floor in the live room.
Correct. There should be no problem at all with getting good isolation, based on your general outline.
2. There will be outer walls and inner walls spaced apart.
Correct: The materials used to build each "leaf" of that double-wall, and the gap between the two leaves, are calculated using simple equations, to get the level of isolation you need.
3. Inner walls would support inner ceiling, spaced apart from the roofing structure.
correct. Once again, the building materials and the size of the gap are calculated.
4. Can inner walls be made of wood frame and gypsium board or should I rather think of denser material?
Yes, no problem with that. Here too the spacing of the wood frame away from the outer leaf, and the surface density of the drywall (gypsum board) are calculated in order to give you the amount of isolation you need. What are you planning to use for the outer leaf? Brick or concrete block would be excellent choices, as you need high isolation.

Soundman202022 June 2015#6
I just went to a studio which is built on grade. It doesn't have inner shell, just one layer of 30cm concrete wall with styrofoam thermal insulation on the outside (under plaster layer). Concrete floor. No resiliency at any place in the building. It has insufficient transmission loss.
Yup! That's what single-leaf construction will get you: Mass Law is not very heartening.
Measured by the outside wall with Radioshack meter this studio gets around 38dBC or 48dBA isolation.
For 30cm thick concrete walls, that's about right. 45 dB isolation would be the rough prediction for that.
What I hear from drums the most is the area between 100Hz and 250Hz, but higher frequencies are also audible.
So mostly snare and toms? Or is the kick smaller and tuned high?
Frequencies lower than 100Hz don't seem to be much of a problem (don't appear to be annoying).
There might not be a lot of energy below 100 Hz for a drum kit that is tuned high. It would be better to test the low end using a six-string bass and a nasty amp...
If I build a sealed inner shell and if I create double leaf wall: [concrete block - 5cm mineral wool - 20cm air space - 5cm mineral wool - double gypsum board on studs], am I going to see significant drop in transmission in this 100-250Hz area? I feel that 20dB more isolation would be needed to make the sounds out of the studio acceptable for neighbours. Thanks!
Theoretically, that would give you roughly 55 dB of isolation, but it would go a lot lower down the spectrum than what you saw. If you built it right, it would isolate down to about 30 Hz., and isolate very well from about 48 Hz. upwards. The isolation dip at 150 Hz would be gone. Or rather, it would be pushed down way lower, to a point where it doesn't really matter (unless you want to record earthquakes, canon, and alien spaceships blowing up Planet Earth...). In other words: Yes, with that plan you'd get pretty good isolation down to low frequencies. Of course, this assumes that you design and build the doors, windows and HVAC system to high levels of isolation too. Especially HVAC: that's usual the part that people forget about, until it is too late to get it right... - Stuart -

Soundman202022 June 2015#8
Are these equations available?
In many places, and many forms. The best resource I know of for understanding 2-leaf MSM construction is the Wyle report. It's old, but very good: https://app.box.com/shared/jcaoavdc8g Also, for further understanding and more equations I'd suggest two books: "Master Handbook of Acoustics" by F. Alton Everest (that's sort of the Bible for acoustics), and "Home Recording Studio: Build it Like the Pros", by Rod Gervais. There's also a great paper called "Accuracy of Prediction Methods for Sound Transmission Loss" from the the 33rd International Congress and Exposition on Noise Control Engineering in 2004, but I don't have a link for downloading it. - Stuart -
