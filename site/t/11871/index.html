The point of the triangle is behind your head, about 20 cm back from your ears (unless you have a strange shape head!). So the your speaker axes just touch your ears, and meet behind your head. But your ears are at the 38% mark, not the point of the triangle. HOWEVER! Don't get too hooked up on that 38% point. It's not a universal law that is carved in stone! It's just a stating point, a general rule of thumb to get you in the ball park. You might find that you need to be further forward or further back for the best position. Trial and error. I read somewhere that many engineers ending up sitting a little closer forward than that. You need to experiment a bit.

I went through the exact same dilemmas a few months back, when I was designing by room geometry and my soffits, and it took me ages to find the answers and think it through logically! But just to be sure, let me re-cap: Your EARS are at the 38% mark, which is where the room modes are supposed to be most favorable. As far as the speakers being totally isolated from the soffit panels, no. They are confined tightly in a plywood box within the panel just like in the design. No stand, just a wooden platform that forms part of the wooden frame that holds the walls.
Ahhhhh! Now THAT sounds like we might be getting somewhere! Your speakers absolutely DO need to be isolated from the soffit panels, and also from the structure. They way you have them mounted now, I strongly suspect that the speakers are directly causing the panel / structure to vibrate and resonate! When you are playing music loudly through your speakers, if you touch the soffit with your fingers can you feel it vibrating? If so, that is probably the issue. The way I understand it, the speaker must NOT be coupled to the soffit panel, nor to the structure: It must be decoupled as much as possible, which is why the normal method that I've seen is to have a thick layer of something like neoprene, rubber or something like that between the speaker and the massive box that contains it, so that the speaker can vibrate all it wants without transmitting that directly to the soffit panel (which is only there to stop sound from "wrapping around" behind the speaker) or to the structure (which is only there to hold things up). In other words, the soffit panel is supposed to acts as an infinite baffle, but in your case it is acting more like the speaker cone! If it is vibrating, then it is creating it's own sound, which is interfering with the sound radiated by the speaker. That's why the soffit panel needs to be so extremely massive: it has to resist vibrating and resonating in sympathy with the the speaker, at all costs. Something light weight won't work for that. The more massive it is, the harder it is to make it move, and the lower the resonance (and resonant frequency) should be. That's why my soffit panels will be concrete. I just haven't figured out how thick I need to make the concrete yet, but those panels will be massive! I also haven't fully figure out the details of mounting the speakers inside the soffits, but the idea I'm leaning towards at present is to build a concrete box whose inside dimensions are a little bit bigger than my speakers, with that difference being the exact thickness of whatever material I end up selecting for the isolation. So if I eventually figure out that I'm going to need an 8mm thickness of neoprene (or whatever) to get reasonable isolation, then the inside dimensions of the concrete box will be 8mm bigger than the outside dimensions of my speakers, all around. I strongly suspect that your speakers are causing your soffits to vibrate and resonate, since the whole thing is directly coupled to the speaker. Probably if you decouple your speakers form the soffit, then things will improve a lot. Do the "fingertip test" on your soffit panels: while playing loud music on the A7s, gently touch the soffit panels with your fingertips. If you can feel the, vibrating, then my guess is that you have found your problem (or one of your problems). - Stuart -

Soundman202020 April 2009#23
it got quite confusing as some people said it needed to be decoupled, others suggested it needed to be as tight as possible inside the box.
Actually, it needs to be BOTH! :) The speaker needs to fit tightly inside the decoupling lining (let's just call it "rubber", for simplicity). So as you push the speaker into the rubber-lined box, it will be really hard to do that for two reasons: 1) it's a tight fit anyway, since the dimensions of the "hole" inside the rubber lining are exactly the same as the dimensions of the speaker, and 2) you will be pushing against the air pressure building up behind the speaker. Basically. it's like pushing a piston into a sealed cylinder: the air has no place to go. Fortunately for folks like you and me with active speakers, we have vents at the back of our boxes, so that helps, but you are still trying to force an object into a hole that is exactly the same size. But the point is, it needs to be a tight fit so that no air can leak around the edge of the speaker box. The front of the speaker cabinet needs to be sealed tightly to the soffit panel.
