sharward28 September 2005#2
Here in the U.S. there are often "setback" regulations that dictate how close one can build to the property line. Setbacks are often specified differently depending on what part of town one lives in -- downtown areas are often more forgiving than the suburbs -- and vary depending on the direction from the street (e.g., front porch 15 feet, garage door 20 feet, side 5 feet, back 12½ feet, not counting roof lines). In my case my side setback is 5 feet, which is the distance between my house and the fence. In other words, the builder left no room to spare. Assuming you live in an area where such things are regulated, you'll want to verify with your local building officials that you're allowed to build in that entire space. It looks like you're proposing to build very close to the front of your lot -- that may be restricted as well. It appears your neighbor has already built to the fence line. Perhaps this is allowed, or perhaps your neighbor did an illegal addition. :? Another factor could be utility easements -- are there any gas lines, electrical lines, water lines, sewer lines, or anything else in the ground in that location that would require access?
I was wondering if I could use the wall of my house as part of the outer layer of the studio?.
It's better if you can avoid doing that. Sharing a wall may save you some money and floor space, but it will increase opportunity for flanking and structural-borne noise invading the house. There may also be other compelling reasons not to connect the new space to the house. Here in the U.S., building permits are often less cumbersome if the building being constructed is separate from the house -- a utility building, vs. a home addition. If this distinction exists in your part of the world, you may wish to explore the ramifications of each. For example, you may not be allowed to have a bathroom if it's a separate building. Hopefully I'm not off-base in mentioning all these odd things... They may have no application to you in your area, but on the other hand, they might -- and these may be applicable to others who view this thread in the future. :)
Floated floors are probably not even optional - I'll need them with my isolation requirements.
Not so fast. In your case, an isolated slab may do the trick, especially if the building is freestanding. That's another advantage to having a separate building. In fact, you may be able to do two isolated slabs, one for each room. They'll need to be thick and strong because your room will be extremely heavy for its size. Do some searches here for isolated slabs. One of the threads you'll find is mine -- good luck sifting through that one! :lol:
I was contemplating a solid concrete floor as the celing for the outer shell (as if there would be a second story built on top?).
If you're doing much of the work yourself (as it seems you will), that's probably not practical. You can achieve some great isolation with conventional materials. If Rhinowall/drywall isn't too expensive in your part of the world, you can probably just use lots of that in your ceiling. But don't forget, you'll need two leaves overhead. You'll need to come up with a plan for heating, air conditioning, and ventilation -- "HVAC" as we say. That's probably one of the most challenging aspects of any design. That's all I have for now. Surely you'll have feedback from others too. --Keith :mrgreen:

Isolated slab ... floating floor ... I use the term interchangeably ... need to do some more reading ... again. Shows I don't have the concept down yet.
In short, a "floating floor" is a two-leaf system -- the upper leaf "floats" on elsatometers, such as rubber (Neoprene or EPDM) pucks. An "isolated slab" is a slab that sits alone on the ground. The problem with floating floors is that they need to be very thick and very heavy in order not to resonate at a frequency that would be very bad for isolation. It is also often difficult to bring a floating floor to a density that "keeps up" with the walls, so it often becomes a weakness in the overall design. For example, simply speaking, on a scale of 1 to 10 (10 being best), if you have an 8 ceiling, 7 walls, and 4 floor, you have a 4 studio. Don't think for a moment that you can get away with an inferior surface and make up for it with the others -- the lowest score prevails. :shock:
