I just rediscovered this awesome thread which includes, among many other very quotable quotables, this statement from Paul Woodlock himself:
Paul Woodlock wrote:
. . . To calculate a floating floor properly, you need the specifications of your intended Elastomer. These specs will include nomographs of the modulus of elasticity against time temp and load. . .
The U-boats offer None of this data. :x Moving on...
Paul Woodlock wrote:
. . . For all: A floating floor is a mass-spring-mass system. And as such has a resonant frequency. At this resonant freqeuncy you don't get isolation, in fact you get the reverse...amplification. The amount of amplification depends upon how damped the elastomer is. Anyone whose ever driven a car where the shock absorbers have given up the ghost, will know painfully the effects of amplification at resonance as their car bounces wildly down the road. To avoid the floating floor making things worse the resonant ( or 'natural', as you'll sometimes see it described ) freqeuncy must be calculated to be BELOW the audible freqeuncy range. Or at least below the lowest freqeuncy your monitors can reproduce. . .
He goes on to say...
Paul Woodlock wrote:
A wooden floated floor is effectively a huge drumskin. So floating a wooden floating floor,even if you do calculate a low enough natural frequency, can severely affect the acoustics of the room. . .
And finally...
Paul Woodlock wrote:
. . . A floating floor is really the LAST Line of defence in Soundproofing. In other words, UNLESS the rest of the soundproofing is of a VERY HIGH Isolation value, there is little point in floating the floor at all. Floating Floors ( a floating anything ) without calculation, or when it's actually unecessary to float a floor is very likely to waste lots of time, effort and money.
I recommend reading the whole thread to get everything in context, but those are some beauties I wanted to make sure to underscore for you, and anyone else watching this. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward5 August 2006#17
I know what you mean about the "6 month" thing... What that means (if your city handles it like mine) is that you must schedule an inspection within six months of your permit issue date. Each inspection buys you another six month window. You don't have to be finished completely in six months -- you just have to prove that the project is moving and not abandoned. If you get close to a six month expiration date, you may be able to get a courtesy extension. What you generally don't want is to allow your permit to expire, because then you have to start the entire process over again and must adhere to any code changes that have been implemented/adopted since the original plan review -- not to mention paying the plan check fee again. As far as the weight calculations are concerned, there are two reasons this is important. One is to make sure you don't overload anything to the point of being dangerous. That's the most critical part, obviously. The second reason only really applies if you are floating a floor on elastomers -- that's where you need to make sure you don't overload or underload. I'm delighted that my thread is helping -- and you deserve at least a cookie for suffering through its length! ;-) But, really, Rod's book is way better in terms of its use as a how-to guide. Mine thread is a diary, which can be informative -- but his is an actual how-to guide, almost a textbook. In the event of a conflict, follow Rod's book!! :) --Keith :mrgreen:
