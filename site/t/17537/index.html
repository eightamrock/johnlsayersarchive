Soundman20209 May 2012#11
so the speakers would be sitting on the desk itself, rather than on stands.
There are several problems with speakers sitting on the desk. The big one is vibration: The vibration of the speaker cabinets will be transmitted into the desk itself, causing the desk to vibrate and act as a speaker. That muddies up the sound terribly. Second is reflections. With the speakers sitting directly on a surface, there will be reflections from that surface bouncing around the room at strange angles. Third is height: The speakers should be positioned with the acoustic axis 1.2 m (about 47-1/4") above the floor, which is ear height for most people when seated. It's unlikely that your desk and speakers will work out to the correct height. Fourth is position: It isn't very likely that the desk will just happen to have a place to put the speakers in the correct location for your room, but you have no choice with a desk. If it turns out that the correct location is a few inches behind the rear edge of the desk, or a few inches off to one side, well that's unfortunate: you are stuck with putting the speakers in the wrong place. For those reasons (and others) stands are a better option, in general.
I'm not sure what the bigger problem would be in terms of CR dimensions - narrow width or almost-square dimensions.
Personally, I would avoid the almost square dimensions, even if it meant making the room a bit narrower. Of course, if it makes the room so narrow that you cannot get the speakers far enough apart, or that it causes some other issue with the speaker geometry, then that would make it a tough call!
To cut the costs down a little, I'm planning on only sheetrocking the ceiling of the CR and the Vocal booth; the bathroom and the live room would have simple acoustic tile ceilings. Do you think, this would provide enough isolation (i.e. making only the CR and the booth separate "boxes")?
No, that wouldn't give you much isolation at all. Acoustic ceiling tiles provide very little isolation at all (that isn't what they are meant for). Isolating a room is kind of like building an aquarium: Unless you put glass on all sides of the aquarium, it will not hold water. Unless you seal it totally watertight, it will not hold water. Even a tiny hole means that water will leak out. It's the same with a studio. It's just like an aquarium (except upside down): unless you treat all 5 sides to the same level, and seal them all airtight, it will not "hold sound". Even a tiny gap somewhere is enough to compromise the isolation. Just like water will always take the easiest path out, so will sound. If you isolate 4 sides really well but didn't do the ceiling, well then you wasted a lot of money on doing the walls! That would be like building your aquarium with glass on four sides, but putting only cardboard on the bottom, instead of glass. Obviously, that won't work. Neither will only isolating some sides of the room. And just like water leaking out of an aquarium will splash around all over the place, so will sound leaking out of your room. Once the sound is out, then it is out and free to go wherever it can. Sometimes people think that "I don't need to isolate the north wall, because that's just the kitchen there, and I don't mind if the sound gets to the kitchen". The problem is, if the sound gets out through the north wall, it will be audible to the south, east, west, and up, not just in the kitchen! So if you want to isolate, you have no choice: you have to do all sides of the room to the same level. And that includes doors, windows, HVAC and electrical. - Stuart -
