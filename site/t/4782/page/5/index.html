andy_eade wrote:
Is there anyway to overcome this 3 leaf problem other then drilling the cinderblock and filling with sand?
I just went through a complete review of cinder block 3 leaf thing. All opinions and immediate one from Knightly were YES fill the blocks and the 16” footer under wall could take the extra weight no problem. If I were you I would try and figure out the weight difference 1st if that’s what you choose, but your situation is different then mine. I was surprised how quickly 4 people (architect, concrete guy, contractor & kinghtfly) I asked said yes it could take the weight. I only have about 10 feet to fill of one outer wall fill. And I'm choosing to fill it with sand because I can do it myself. Do it myself mortar sounds messy, paying to have a truck pump concrete in would be too expensive. I’m planning on using my hammer-drill, got to buy a masonry hole saw (carbide tip set of 8 on eBay $25, I can’t find diamond tip price yet or even if they make them) and then spoon/funnel sand into cavities. If I clean cut with the hole saw I can use the cut out circle and cement to patch holes. I’m not looking forward to that job. Most likely cavities of your block are same width and length as mine 5” x 5”. I know mine are about 82” high. And the other option which is not as good is to add mass to the inner side of the cinder block... which will screw up decoupling floor, ceiling and other walls. I couldn't think may way around it. AND I just found STC rating for 8" cinder block: hollow = 48, filled with grout = 55, filled with sand = 52 Hope that helps a bit, Marc

This is probably the worst nightmare of any potential homeowner in general, but the worst nightmare of potential home studio owners/builders for sure. man, I hope that you invested in several pairs of overalls, the best NIOSH mask and extra cartridges that money can buy, gloves (including surgical ones) and are wearing safety glasses that fit snug around your eyes. If not, I hope you have been downing several of those scrumptious tetleys after being done pulling that crap apart. :lol: Let this thread be a reminder to ANYONE attempting to convert a house or a part thereof for the purpose of putting in a studio: wear protective gear. Even if you dont have these extreme moisture and insect conditions, always remember that the "bite" is in what you CAN NOT see. And the effect of the "bite" may not pop up for years to come!!! What kinds of things am I talking about: for starters: nail fungus, also called onychomycosis. brittle nasty looking nails. an infection that lives deep in your nail bed. topical ccreams dont work. you'll need Rx meds such as Lamisil or Sporanox, both heavy duty drugs, that require LFT tests (liver function tested via vial of blood). You will need to be on these meds for at least a year, with no alcohol consumption. also you will need to throw most of your shoes away. costly indeed. but imagine if you are a performer and your finger nails look like shit: does not promote your image does it?? also playing guitar might actually become painful. eye infections: anything from conjunctivitis to skin rashes or worse lung problems, sinus head aches, constant post nasal drip. imagine having to sing with that 24/7?? fuggettaboutit! also, the danger lies not only in what you pull apart. It also lies in the materials used during the conversion.

Dan Fitzpatrick17 February 2006#119
Andy, although i have heard of one guy around here somewhere successfully using a router to cut drywall from the walls, intact, and use it for beef up, i doubt you'd be able to do the same with the ceiling, just because you'd need someone to hold up the loose end as you cut it so that it doesn't break from the gravity (Stupid gravity!!) or maybe you just want large pieces so it is easier to dispose of? not sure about your electrocution problem, i had a few wires in my ceiling but not much. what i did, i used a large crowbar, about 3 feet long, to punch holes in the drywall, and then i used the hook end of the bar to pull down pretty large sections of drywall at a time, sometimes close to full sheets. it actually went pretty fast and i didn't need to stand on a ladder or anything. unfortunately my disposal plan called for putting relatively small pieces in 3 mil "contractor" garbage bags, and hauling the bags to the dump. so even though i ended up with large pieces, i spent some time manually breaking them up into small pieces so i could bag them. of course, if you are really worried about live wires in the space, then you want to turn off the power, or at least don't use a metal crowbar to do the work ... hope that helps, but i guess you'll figure it out pretty quick anyway :)

2) Second question - There is vinyl tile on top of the concrete slab. Is there any reason why I need remove it, or can I just build on top of it?
Do you know how old the tile is? It might have asbestos in it. This page shows years and manufacturers of asbestos-embedded vinyl tile, including information on how you can get a small sample tested if you're not sure of its age. If it has asbestos, your best (safest) bet is to leave it alone.

Edit #2 10 minutes later: - Just found the answer to most of thisquesions in this thread.
I recommend consulting with a structural engineer to be sure. Better safe than sorry.
4) Picture 90 illustrates some splits I noticed in some of the ceiling joists - is this okay?
I'm not 100% sure, but I think it's OK. The most critical area of joists is along the bottom edge in the middle third of the span. That's where the fibers are under the most strain (pulling apart). If you had vertical cracks that went against the grain, that would worry me more.
I did check for further termite damage in the joists, and though there is some poop along some of them; there does not appear to be any damage in them.
That may or may not be true. Your termite inspector should be able to advise you on that.
5) Lastly, there is existing electrical wires running through some of the joists (see pic 82) going to outlets and some of the light fixture. Should I be removing them and refiting them through conduit?
In residential construction, that type of wiring (trade name Romex) is fine as long as it's not exposed, and it won't be exposed in your case. You shouldn't need conduit for that. Although...
