andy_eade20 February 2006#130
Well here is today's news so far. I have talked to a couple of Asbestos removal companies to get further inmformation. And here is what I learned. - Towards the end of the 70's (when my hous ewas built); absestos was in all the headlines. Consequently alot of homebuilders avoided using such material for floor tiles etc. - Manufacturers like Armstrong were producing identical tiles both with and without asbestos. So there is a 50% chance that my tile does not conatin asbestos. - The mastic used to glue the tiles down may also contain asbestos. Apparantly builders continued to used these products into the 80's and in some cames the early 90's. Apparantly with mine being black in color; there is an 80% chance that it does contain Asbestos. In which case they would need to treat the whole area, including tile. Approx cost of removal for my area (250 sq ft) $1200 to $1800. So, for now I have overnighted samples of the tile and mastic down to a lab in Richmond, who will run an analysis on the products within 24 hours - so I should have a definitive answer sometime Wedenesday. One of the other guys I was talking to this morning said that I should at least feel happy in the knowledge that I did not begin removing this. One of his jobs today was going out to a house where the guy had already removed 2/3rds of a floor before finding out that it contained asbestos. Besides the fact that he subjected himself, wife, and children to it's damaging effects - they have now had to abandon the home until it's treatment is complete. Treatment which will now incorporate indentifying how much of the fibers were released into other areas of the house. Clean up from his blunder could cost him tens of thousands to fix! So... if there's one thing we all can learn here. When begining the demolition stage: - Always Listen to Keith's wise words of warning!!! -Regardless of what you can see - proctect yourself for the worst based on what you can't see. - Seal off all doors or area's where air can escape from the work zone with Polythene sheeting and duct tape. Seal off any A/C Outlets and returns. Cover door bottoms with a wet towel. The entire area should be airtight. - Steel toe boots, NOISH Respirator, Goggles that cover the eyes completely, Gloves, Bunny Suit (Disposable Overalls) and hard hat are all a must. - Question everything. Call an expert, or call one in. Better to spend $50 to find out there is no risk than to knock 20 years (or worse) off your life (or your family's life) for free. Besides, most experts you call or come out for an initial consultation charge nada! - Complete thorough research (as I know we all do here) before beginning any stage to fully understand it's complexities and how to deal with them. i.e. I found mold - how do I get rid of it. - Know your limitations. Know what is within your ability, and what makes more sense to call in a proffessional for. The demo stage can be quite fun. Breaking stuff up with a wrecking bar - heck - it's like being a kid again, but please take care to mitigate any risks first. more news to come soon. Andy.

knightfly4 March 2006#142
My span calculator shows nearly 20 PSF dead load for your SPF #2, your calculations are fine; you should be able to add a total of 3 layers of 5/8 drywall no sweat. I would distribute those layers this way; one layer of "beef" up between joists, as per keiths and other threads, and two layers hung on RSIC clips/hat channel, with GG between those two layers. Complete fill with UN-faced fiberglass batts, normal house weight. Don't recall if you're doing multiple studio rooms, but if so you should do your walls first and NOT do a continuous ceiling between rooms. If you plan on having a dehumidifier in the space when not in use, sheet rock/steel is fine. Just remember to use a thermal break between frame and concrete, get a couple cans of zinc chromate primer and paint any cuts/trims of the steel studs before covering them up, and do NOT use any insulation with ANY kind of vapor barrier between rooms... Steve

Sandersd15 March 2006#148
I know [sic] have to wait 3-4 weeks for the cement to cure before I can begin framing walls on top. The guy that did the work suggested not firing any kind of bolts down into the cement for the bottom plates as he said it will not hold and will just break the new cement. He suggested using liquid nails.
FYI: Concrete's most rapid period of strength gain occurs in the first 7 days. The accepted time standard for measuring concrete strength is 28 days. However, concrete will continue to gain strength for long periods of time (months, even years!) As long as moisture is present to continue the chemical process of hydration. If you're waiting three weeks to start framing, you can shoot all the nails your heart desires. Think about it: in new home construction all the interior walls are set using powder actuated fasteners. How long do the framers wait before starting the framing? Whatever you do, wait at least seven days. Drilling for 1/2" anchor bolts is also a great way to secure the wall plates. :D
