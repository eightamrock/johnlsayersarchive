knightfly9 October 2006#265
I don't agree with the no sway idea in your case - if you hard frame to your upper floor, then you'll have a direct flanking path between any footfall noise above and your inner wall frame below, which may (not sure, but wouldn't take the chance) lower the isolation between studio and living space. If you keep a gap between top plates of studio walls and upper floor, the sway brace will be a locator/stabilizer for your inner wall frame (done as I show in the sketch) WITHOUT direct coupling between upper floor and wall and lower floor - then, if you hang your ceiling on RSIC's INSIDE these inner walls and use backer/caulk at joints and do NOT run the wall layers hard against the upper floor joists EITHER, you'll lessen the structure-born transmision between elements of the construction. Finally, using pallet strapping on the inside of your walls will help keep the insulation against the wallboard, eliminating slip-down AND providing damping. This damping would also extend to your frame somewhat. The end result should be that any flanking would need to travel down your outer concrete walls (damped by earth), thru the floor slab and back up thru the walls in order to radiate into your studio space. Between earth damping of your outer walls AND your slab, I doubt this would be much of a concern at any normal volume levels. Doing a heavy fill in the ceiling over the RSIC supported drywall layers, combined with the iso that's contributed by the RSIC clips, (and were you gonna do the betwee-joist beef? Don't recall) should combine to minimise both footfall and other noise in either direction. Any sound flanked thru your concrete INTO the inner wall frame will be damped by insulation and STOPPED by the gap at the top of the wall. I'm not sure why anyone does NOT think this is a good idea... Steve

Well I have to be honest - I've never held or worked with the sway brace from Kenetics - and seeing the cost mentioned below - I probably never will. BUT - I have worked with and held in my hands the braces in my book from Mason Industries (which I HAVE to believe can be bought a whole lot cheaper than the Kenetics product). The WIC braces from Mason work on the same principles we all know and love - which is that the material must be properly compressed (not too much - not too little) in order to properly isolate. These babies are really pretty sweet - and are preloaded in the factory, with the degree of compression of the isolator determining the center frequency of the product as a whole. They are (quite simply) 2 "U" shaped pieces of metal - with each having an extended leg - which then have a piece of ribbed rubber compressed between the 2 of them when they are assembled. If you look at figure 4.29 in the book - you'll see the little rib edges in the plate style (WCL Furring Style Sway Brace) - and I will assure you that the WIC has the same slots cut into them. The WIC clips are what I reccomend for walls - the others are generally used for commercial ceiling applications.
Specifically I’m wondering how they attach to the ceiling joists (if that’s where they attach), and how many one needs per wall or certain length.
The WIC clips attach to the studs between 2 adjacent framed walls - or to Angle Iron clips that are attached to concrete if the new wall is next to a foundation. These conditions are detailed in Chapter 10 - figures 10.9, 10.12, 10.13, 10.31a and (finally) 10.14. Typically these are installed on 4' centers - but this also may be dependant on local seismic conditions - so check with the local authorities to be sure. BTW - be certain when installing these that you do not load them up - meaning you do not install them under stress.... you want to gently float them into place, and then firmly fasten them - this will ensure that they remain loaded exactly as the manufacturer intended.
