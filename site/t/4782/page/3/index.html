Dan Fitzpatrick24 January 2006#52
Hi Andy, from my rough estimate using Brian Ravnaas' MSM calculator, based on 2 panes of 3mm glass with 13mm airspace, you are looking at around 220hz MAM frequency. this is pretty high. at some point below this frequency (maybe around 100hz?), the two panes should start acting as one pane. so below 100hz, the difference between dual pane and single pane should be non existent for the equivalent mass of glass. also above 300 hz, the iso will be better than if it were one pane of glass. it's only in the neighborhood of 220 hz (+/- about 90hz i think) that having a dual pane would be worse than a single pane. anyway, don't know if that helps or not but it's interesting i guess. dan Edit: some others have reported bad ringing sounds coming from their sliders at frequencies suspiciously close to the expected MAM frequency, apparently using dual pane sliders CAN BE a huge problem

Dan Fitzpatrick24 January 2006#54
No problem Andy. Just remember that I'm really not much of an expert, i probably just know enough to get myself and you in trouble. and you are right, there are no studs between the glass so that would make each leaf lighter, and therefore the MAM frequency would be even higher. using eric desart's calculator which is simpler, gives a MAM frequency of 300hz for 3mm (1/8 in) glass and 12.5mm (1/2 inch) airgap. benefits of the dual leaf begin at 300 x 1.4 = 420hz. mass law take effect i think the same amount in the other direction, 180hz (approximately. not 100% sure here). between these two extremes, the dual pane window will perform worse than mass law (worse than if the two panes were touching with no airgap). so if you did find the single pane slider that had the same mass of glass, it would perform better at 300hz, worse above 420hz, and would make no difference below 180hz. A triple leaf is bad for a couple of reasons. One is it divides your precious airspace, which you want to be as big as possible, into two smaller airspaces. big airspace is just as important as big mass when building a wall. so don't divide it. second while with two leaves you get a mass air mass resonance, with three leaves you either get two MAM resonances that are the same, making the "hole" worse, or you get resonances in two different places, so you have two "holes" where you could have had one. also when to MAM resonances work together they can have suprising bad effects. with me so far? ok, so the difference in proportion between the space in the vocal booth and the space between your gypsum leaves and your glass leaves is so extreme that i have a hard time recognizing this as a three leaf effect. in fact if this were true then nobody would ever be able to build a studio with 3 or 4 rooms because there would always be 3 or more leaves between some rooms. would you get better isolation between your drum room and CR if you used only 1 leaf on those gypsum walls? (leaving the studs open on one side). maybe, it probably depends on the frequency. but your glass doors will probably be the weak link anyway, so actually it probably doesn't matter. i guess the bottom line is, if it were me, i wouldn't worry about having dual pane glass, i don't think it will matter. sometimes you have no choice but to have 4 leaves or whatver. Rod Gervais uses the example of a hotel. you have a room, a hallway and a room across the hall. there you would have to have 4 leaves, there just isn't any way around it. but it is fine, the relative width of the hallway vs. the space in the walls is much different. hope that helps ... or i should say, hope that didn't confuse you more. :D EDIT: i just read this thread which i hadn't seen before ... worth putting in a reference to it in this thread for sure. http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4790 not sure what is causing this "ringing" but another reason to be cautious when using sliding glass doors. :?

Dan Fitzpatrick31 January 2006#59
Hi Andy, sorry i didn't reply, i wanted to see what others said, thought maybe i'd said enough ... also this is a tricky question, that it is difficult to give a "for sure" answer to. maybe that is why nobody answers. my guess, which could be wrong, is #1 is the best. i think in 2 or 3 that the sliders will be a weak link, and doing a double stud wall with double doors is a more robust approach. between 2 and 3, all things being equal, meaning that the same amount of gypsum is used in both cases (not as you stated it), the one with single leaves on each stud wall would be better. i think this is pretty open-and-shut, since you will have the same total mass but avoid the quad-leaf effects of the stud walls covered on both sides with drywall. your option 2 and 3 do not have equal mass as you stated them. #2 has 5 layrs of drywall total and #3 has only 3 layers. so it's not a fair comparison really. you have the resonance effect in #2 but more drywall, so maybe that cancels out the resonance effect somewhat ... but it is impossible to know this without actually testing it. applying theory works best when choosing between 2 options where only 1 thing changes between them.

That "option" you mentioned me mentioning :? would ONLY give you better iso between the drum room and CR; another advantage would be that you could use the stud frames to hold absorption for the voice booth, which usually needs to be fairly dead (you could always add slats over the cloth in a few places if it's TOO dead) - However, if you ALSO want good enough ISO for a screaming vocalist NOT to be heard thru the walls, but only in the monitors, then I'd bite the bullet and doo m-a-m walls/doors EACH SIDE of the vox booth - this WILL cost you a small amount of iso between CR and DR, but probably not more than a couple of dB. The GAIN in iso between the VB and CR may well be worth it to you. To me, there are only two reasons for using sliding glass doors in a studio - sight lines and NOT eating up floor space for the SWING. If ISO is your main worry, stay away from sliders; the BEST seals are marginal, and TWO each wall is EXPENSIVE. (Or REALLY crappy :roll: ) That said, a lot of people get really claustrophobic in small rooms with no windows - have I mentioned the old cliche', damned if you do damned if you don't lately??!? If there's room, yet another option is to just use doors as doors; IOW, get heavy glass for both leaves of both walls of the Vox booth, and locate the doors OUT of sight lines. This would have the advantage of good sight lines, as well as making seals easier to accomplish (hermetic seals on the glass, little if any loss of iso because of the glass, and cheaper doors (the solid mahogany ones for example, with drop seal kits) Only downside I'd consider with THAT scenario is finding "swing" room for the solid doors, and keeping them out of sight lines while NOT screwing up traffic patterns... sharward6 February 2006#72
If your untrained eye could see discoloration, then it is possible that you noticed something that the termite inspector should have noticed but didn't. Ask the new termite inspector to give you a detailed written report, including the condition of the paneling, estimates of the age of infestation, whether or not it's likely to have been visible evidence of infestation at the time of sale, etc. This report may become "Exhibit A" in litigation. You may have a case against:
  • The previous owner of the house, if they knew or should have known of the damage prior to close of escrow and did not inform you ("failure to disclose") or expressly lied to you about its condition ("wilful misrepresentation"); and/or
  • The termite inspector and/or home inspector who inspected the home prior to close of escrow if they were negligent in detecting visible* damage at the time of the inspection; and/or
  • Your real estate agent if they became aware of the problem and failed to inform you.
Those are some big "ifs" and, even if one or more of them is true, you have burden of proof. That's why it's important to document everything, just in case a need arises to bring action at a later date... But not too late, as there is a statute of limitations that you may be very close to reaching (often it's one year, so it would be wise to find out for sure). You probably already know that homeowners' insurance does not cover wood destroying pest infestations or slow leaks. I'm crossing my fingers that the damage is localized and relatively easy to repair. *Note that "visible" means visible even if there are movable things in the way. If the basement were full of the previous owner's belongings at the time of the inspection, the inspector has a duty to move or ask the owner to move items out of the way to gain necessary access to all areas to facilitate a complete inspection
