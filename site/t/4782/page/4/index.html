My questions - Is the wood in the pix dry and crumbly, or still wet? The pix aren't quite showing; is there slightly granular "sawdust" in a lot of places, or just crumbling wood? (The "sawdust" is actually Termite poop) Which side of the poly (shown against the blocks) is that wood on - is the wood INSIDE the poly? And, is the wood just furring strips for mounting the panels, or is is some kind of structural frame? Describe your fake oak panels; does the finished side feel "plasticky", or does it actually feel like wood? Just how much of your walls are below dirt - if necessary, is it gonna be a big pain to dig them out and re-coat? Some thoughts - It's very possible that tree has violated your outer membrane; tree roots can split boulders. It needs to go away. And you're right, water can follow dead tree roots as well. It's a good start to extend your drains, you want to lead any and all water AWAY from the foundation walls. That poly is NOT in the right place - in your location, you'll (I think) be heating more than cooling, so any vapor barrier needs to be just under the inside wallboard - in fact, kraft-faced insulation would be the best bet; the paper is the last thing before the first layer of wallboard on the inside of your new stud frame. Where the iffy part comes is that you have a waterproof membrane outside your block, then a vapor barrier at the very inside of the inner wall - this can trap moisture (the likely cause of your existing problems, since that poly is NOT in the right location) and cause dry rot. I asked about the present condition of the wood because dry rot is caused by moisture, but won't feel very moist necessarily; If you've downloaded some of the papers from Building Sciences, you may have read that moisture is driven thru a wall by heat; so when you heat the space, it drives moisture thru the wall and into the wall cavity. There needs to be a vapor barrier to STOP this, and it needs to be right behind the inner wallboard so the moisture doesn't make it into the cavity. If you have ANOTHER vapor barrier at the blocks, you get trapped moisture between the two and everything there either rots or mildews. Depending on how your walls/basement floor were built, they might be able to allow some moisture to drain out through the bottom of the wall and the footing; but maybe not, if that area was sealed tight during construction. You may have no choice but to bust out a place for a sump pump... I asked about your "FOAK" because plasticised paneling will ALSO act as a vapor barrier, so even with your unfaced insulation you could have had two vapor barriers in place, trapping moisture and doing what we see in the pix. Water, especially when the ground water level stays low, doesn't spread out much when soaking into the ground; because of this, you might get away with extending your downspouts AND cutting the sod back maybe 3-4 feet from the house, laying down one of the blankets that are used for covering septic leach lines, and putting the sod back; This should come up your block wall a few inches above dirt level. Doing this would help your soil near the house to drain further from the foundation, so that water would penetrate straight down and not add to the moisture problem in the block wall. Running a dehumidifier will NOT pull more moisture in from outside; the physics are as above - heat inside will drive moisture OUT thru the wall. The dehumidifier will only REDUCE the amount of moisture you have available inside so this = LESS of a problem, not MORE. Steel studs and gypsum are the WORST thing you can do where moisture is a problem; Wood studs and wood paneling can handle 10 TIMES as much water per cubic foot as steel/gypsum before it will cause trouble. Masonry can handle 10 times THAT amount, or 100 times as much as steel/gypsum. For that reason, you may want to use heavy plywood for your inner walls INSTEAD of gypsum. Regardless, once you start building you'll want a thermal break under your wall plates. You'll also want to look for some fiberglass pallet strapping - in these cases, you need to leave air space behind your insulation for drying, but still have your insulation kept in contact with the wall panels for damping. This can be done by building frames on the floor, fastening strapping across the back sides of studs every 2 feet with roofing nails, then standing the frames up and adding insulation and panels and caulk. I'm outa ideas for now; let me know on the top questions... Steve

Sandersd15 February 2006#97
I plan on cutting a foot or so off the top of these doors (as they were 8ft originally). Does anyone have any tips on how to cut them without damaging the vener too much?
Put a piece of wide masking tape along where the cut will be and place your pencil mark (cut line) on the tape. Then, using a straight edge, make a cut using a sharp knife along the cut line, cutting thru the layer of veneer or at least as deep into it as you can. This will cut the surface fibers of wood and prevent them from splitting and chipping when you cut it using a saw. Oh yeah - you only have to do this on the top side because of the way the blade turns, but if you want to be safe go ahead and tape/cut both sides. Yup, I'm familiar with the "owner declaration" affidavit -- the one that says, basically, "I'm doing the work myself and/or I'll hire licensed subcontractors as needed." Get the exact dates of your inspections. To be safe you may want to file suit in small claims court right away against the home inspector, the termite inspector, and perhaps the previous owner, all as co-defendants, just to preserve your rights before the two-year anniversary (likely the statute of limitations). File for the maximum amount (I think Virginia's maximum is a measely $2K) -- you can reduce the amount later if needed, and you can always dismiss the case later if you determine there's no way you could possibly prevail. Remember: Home and termite inspectors carry liability insurance for a reason. This is definitely not frivilous.
