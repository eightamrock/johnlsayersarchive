Dan Fitzpatrick22 March 2006#156
Andy, I don't know exactly the situation you have or the picture in Rod's book (stupid Amazon!!) ... but i don't know of any reason why you would not use screws to fasten the drywall. maybe the 1x3 technique is for when you just don't have a thick enough floor above to screw into? which may be your case. but normally, you would just screw in beef up material (around the perimeter only). that's how i did the wall's i've done so far. i haven't had to beef up any ceiling though. even if you use screws it doesn't hurt to use the 1x3's as a backup ... by the way these are what are called "cleats," which you were wondering in another thread. maybe this is just good practice, to use cleats? but screws should be able to hold up the drywall if you have enough material to screw into i would think? other than that the only tip i have is make the pieces around 1/2 inch smaller than the space as measured, so you have an easy time gettng the pieces in and have space on the sides for caulk to go. it's a pain to trim drywall that's cut a little too big. it's easier to caulk a larger gap. oh yeah if you do use screws, get one of those "fixed depth" screwgun attachments, so you don't go through the paper on the drywall. it makes it much easier. my thread has a picture somewhere. and did you see my ode to backing rod? :P it really helps the caulk stay intact and also saves waste of caulk.

Thanks for the link, just making sure :D Cleats - I'd push 'em up against the drywall inserts while the mud/caulk is still wet, and SCREW them to the joists - you can use 1-5/8 to 1-3/4" drywall or deck screws, spaced no wider than every 12", and no closer to the end of your cleats than 2 or 3". sharward23 March 2006#164
andy_eade wrote:
I was really questioning whether I could use the screws as you would for normal drywall installation i.e. up through the drywall into the subfloor. I'm assuming that this is inadvisable for two reasons: 1) comprimising the mass with "holes". 2) Not enough depth for the screw to be drilled into and hold the weight of the drywall.
99.5% #2 and 0.5% #1. I would actually advise you do both screws about 1" from the edge of the drywall strips into the subfloor and 1x2 cleats along the joists. The screws in strips through to the subfloor will minimize the air gap (ideally none) between the subfloor, and the cleats will relieve pressure on the screw heads and act as insurance against sagging of the strips and/or failure of the screws. Imagine the screws failing and a piece falling down diagonally, bridging the upper leaf to the lower leaf! :shock: You'd probably figure out that there was a problem but wouldn't be able to pinpoint it... And even if you could, imagine the multi-layer hole you'd have to make in order to fix it. :roll: Again, green glue may be useful to mitigate resonance of the subfloor and the drywall strips, all without adding any significant amount of weight to your beef-up materials.
I had planned to used GG on the last layer of 3 5/8th layers of the ceiling hung from the clips. Do you think it's worth using it on the beef up layers too? If it's going to make the difference between silencing my late night drumming from the upstairs or not then it's clearly worth the time, money and effort!
I would do it just for peace of mind's sake, since you can't do it after the fact. If cost is an issue, then keep in mind that you can get away with less than 100% of the optimal coverage and still get good results. I used about 70% of the optimal coverage so I could stretch the one case I purchased over a larger area. You can go as low as 50%. If I recall correctly, the optimal coverage is two tubes per 4x8 sheet, i.e., one tube per 16 square feet. 50% of that would be one tube per 32 square feet.
