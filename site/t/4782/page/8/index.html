I presume the above is close to national standard, so Sharward’s previous guess of air conditioned space may be wrong. Some more stuff:
INTERIOR SPACE DIMENSIONS 1208.1 Minimum room widths. Habitable spaces, other than a kitchen, shall not be less than 7 feet (2134 mm) in any plan dimension. Kitchens shall have a clear passageway of not less than 3 feet (914 mm) between counter fronts and appliances or counter fronts and walls. 1208.2 Minimum ceiling heights. Occupiable spaces, habitable spaces and corridors shall have a ceiling height of not less than 7 feet 6 inches (2286 mm). Bathrooms, toilet rooms, kitchens, storage rooms and laundry rooms shall be permitted to have a ceiling height of not less than 7 feet (2134 mm). Exceptions: 1. In one- and two-family dwellings, beams or girders spaced not less than 4 feet (1219 mm) on center and projecting not more than 6 inches (152 mm) below the required ceiling height. 2. If any room in a building has a sloped ceiling, the prescribed ceiling height for the room is required in one-half the area thereof. Any portion of the room measuring less than 5 feet (1524 mm) from the finished floor to the ceiling shall not be included in any computation of the minimum area thereof. For accessibility provisions related to vertical clearance of areas adjoining an accessible route, refer to Section 11-4.4.2 . 3. Mezzanines constructed in accordance with Section 505.1 . 1208.2.1 Furred ceiling. Any room with a furred ceiling shall be required to have the minimum ceiling height in two-thirds of the area thereof, but in no case shall the height of the furred ceiling be less than 7 feet (2134 mm). 1208.3 Room area. Every dwelling unit shall have at least one room that shall have not less than 120 square feet (13.9 m 2 ) of net floor area. Other habitable rooms shall have a net floor area of not less than 70 square feet (6.5 m 2 ). Exception: Every kitchen in a one- and two-family dwelling shall have not less than 50 square feet (4.64 m 2 ) of gross floor area.

You are just cutting these 1X3’s for cleats right? I would not use a hand saw (sorry sharward, I love power tools). I wouldn’t bother with a work bench or table saw unless you have other uses for either tool down the road. I’ve been putting off on the work bench till I really need one. I thought needed a table saw, bought it, left in the box and took it back after I finished that project. I’ve got a circular saw, a power miter saw and knee pads. I use 2x4’s or other objects to lift material being cut off the ground. Sometimes I use 2- $10 work horses that were gifted to me, but I prefer the concrete floor. I use splash goggles not safety glasses for accurate cuts when I have to get my eyes real close to see the cut line and blade with circular saw. My miter saw has a laser guide. For long cuts I use a guide of a straight 2x4, piece of plywood or my level and clamp it down to the board so that I can jam the edge of my circular saw against it for a guide… this takes a few measurements to get right. Table saws are one of the most dangerous saws out there. Many people warned me not to bother. You’ll hardly ever see a table saw on a construction site. Maybe I’ll buy one buy the end of my project but if you research them most pros advice against the cheaper ones. Sandersd28 March 2006#194
sharward wrote:
I would not put a wall directly on top of that pipe, especially if youre going to support the weight of a ceiling on top of the inner walls. Think of how much weight you'd be putting on that thin layer of cement/concrete/whatever directly on top of the pipe! Shocked
I second that. All that weight would crack the cement and the wall would settle causing your drywall seams to crack, etc, etc, etc... Bite the bullet and set the wall on solid fdn an inch or two from the cut line. You might could do something with a ledge angle attached to the block wall, similar to a brick ledge, and set the wall on top of that so the weight is supported off the wall and not on the thin layer of cement. BTW, are you sure it's straight portland cement. Usually it's mixed with sand at least. Without it, it's very weak and brittle. The sand and aggregrate is what gives it strength - the cement merely binds the aggregrates together. If it is only portland, I would insist the contractor replace it with concrete. You can get a mix with pea gravel for aggregrate. It's small enough to allow a thin layer. Even then you wouldn't be able to set the wall on it though.

knightfly29 March 2006#199
When adding the sheetrock between the joists should I be taping the joins before adding green glue and the second layer? Depends on how quick you work; if you're quick and can get the second layer up before caulk hardens, it should work just as well to caulk the first layer's cracks, then push the second layer into the caulk HARD and fasten. If you're doing GG between, it might be better to mud the joints/cracks for the first layer, being careful NOT to put too much mud (taping knife, strike it off smooth) THEN the GG and second layer, nails to hold (like Rod's book shows) and finally cleats and caulk. Also - did you guys do this work solo or with a second pair of hands? I am assuming that if you work solo one would need to work with pretty small peices. Is this okay or won't smaller peices = more joins = less isolation? If you need to work with smaller pieces, I would fill cracks with MUD (more mass) then sand smooth, second layer, etc... - You should still offset joints in this process same as full-wall sheets. Also - got a call into the pee pee brigade today. Apparantly the Portland is mixed with a light gravel agregate. The layer is about 2 - 2 1/2 inches thick, followed by 3 - 3 1/2 inches or rocks and then the pipe. I think you're underestimating what this can support - look at the sketch below; if the trench is filled with river run rock (round, not crushed with sharp edges) then it needs NO COMPACTION; it will self-level, with nowhere else to go, and form something almost like a "keyed arch" similar to old Roman construction. In any case, both sides of the pipe will be VERY solid. Note in the sketch that I've drawn the portland as filling in around the top layer of rock; this is what happens, so ALL the portland, even tho it's not as strong as actual concrete, will be hard enough and strong enough to support a wall. If you use a wider plate for the wall frame, and use sill seal under it as I drew, you will have plenty of support for a 2-layer gyp wall. We're talking about 50 pounds per linear foot of 7 foot tall wall here, spread out over 7.25 inches wide, for a load of around half a pound per square inch... So all ya gotta do is MISS the pipe with a few lag anchors into the portland, and you should be good to go... Steve
