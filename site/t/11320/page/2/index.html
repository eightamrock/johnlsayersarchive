Soundman202031 October 2008#27
I think you need to read Rod's book to understand the concepts. The diagram you posted is correct: It shows three "inner" rooms next to each other, inside one "outer room". In your case, the existing structure all around is the "outer" leaf. You might need to beef that up with extra mass and extra structural integrity, but you do NOT want ANY part of that outer leaf to touch any part of the "inner" rooms. Neither walls nor ceilings should have any contact at all (between outer and inner), and if possible even the floors should be separated for absolute best isolation. However, it seems that many people do not separate their floors, and they still get good levels of isolation. Think if it this way: you currently have a box with six sides: floor, roof and four walls. That is your outer leaf. Period. All of your other rooms are built INSIDE that shell, and do not touch it anywhere nor do the touch each other (except via the floor, as noted above). Therefore, each of your three rooms is a totally separate "box", with sealed walls around it, and a sealed ceiling on top of it. Period. That's all there is to it. You don't need to build the outer leaf: you already have it. You might need to add more mass to it, but you don't need to build it again, since it already exists. All that you need to do is to build your inner leaf (inner three rooms) as you show in the diagram you posted, with an individual ceiling on top of each one, not touching the other ceilings. You need to make certain that there is no other leaf in there anywhere, such as for example a drop ceiling located between the existing outer leaf ceiling, and the new inner leaf ceiling that you are going to build for each room. The new ceiling for each room rests on top of the new walls for each room, but there is NO connection AT ALL between the three new ceilings (inner leaf) and the existing ceiling (outer leaf). So, basically, you do need to get rid of that drop ceiling: If you leave it in place and build another ceiling below it, then you have three leaves. You cannot use it by itself as your inner leafe, for two reasons: 1) it won't support the weight, and 2) it would create flanking paths between the rooms. It has to go. It is also wasting space: Once you take it out, you will have gained the space above it, so you can now build your new inner leaf ceilings higher, which is good. But the key point is that you want two leaves, and ONLY two. Mass - Air - Mass. Nothing more, nothing less. No drop ceilings in between the outer leaf and the inner leaf, for example. And you should try to keep the two masses of the two leaves as closely balanced as possible (meaning that if you have 3 layers of drywall on your outer leaf, then you should also use three on your inner leaf). But once you get Rod's book, I'm sure you'll understand it much better. He explains it quite clearly.

Height is your friend! If you are going to have 5 or 6 feet of extra space available by ripping out the drop ceiling, then surely you should USE that space! Make your inner rooms 5 or 6 feet higher... More space is good, acoustically. Use every last inch that you can find. If you don't like the idea aesthetically of 12 foot ceilings, you could always still build them that way, use the space for acoustic treatment, then hang some acoustically transparent "ceiling" at whatever height you think looks good. That will lower your ceiling visually, but still give you the extra acoustic space above.
