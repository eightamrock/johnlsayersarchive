Soundman20203 January 2010#6
The brown is for the slat diffusers.
Do you mean "slot resonators"? I don't think you'd want to put diffusers at your first reflection points! In fact, I don't think you'd want diffusers at all in such a small control room! Normally, you'd put thick absorption there, at least 6" thick.
I know how to build soffit mounts
Well, I guess you are going to only mix while standing up, and must be rather tall? If not, then I'm curious as to why you have the acoustic axes of your speakers at roughly 1.8 m above the floor, yet they are not tilted. I didn't do the math, but it looks to me like even if they were tilted to the maximum recommended EBU angle, then they'd still be aimed too high to intercept your ears and also hit the 25cm intersection point. They also seem to be aimed a bit too far forward to place your ears at the 38% point. Is there a reason for that? In other words, there seem to be serious geometry issues with your speaker and mix position layout. The speakers should be at 1.2 m, or can be slightly higher if need be, tilted down by up to 10 degrees. The acoustic axes should aim at your ears, and intersect at an angle of 60 to 90 degrees at a point roughly 25cm behind your ears. Your ears should be roughly at 38% of the room depth (inner-leaf front wall to inner-leaf rear wall). None of that is written in stone, and can be varied slightly as needed, within reason, and while understanding what the consequences will be.
I know how to build soffit mounts ... The yellow is is OC703 to absorb the reflections off of the computer monitors
so if you know how to build soffit mounts, then why are you covering the entire infinite baffle surface with 703? That looks more like a LEDE room, a concept that was abandoned years ago in favor of the RFZ room, which is what most people build today. You might want to re-think those soffits, since they are unworkable as you have them now. The infinite baffle must be an sold, massive, dense surface. It cannot be a soft surface, if you expect it to do its job. You also need to make sure that your speakers have bass roll-off controls that allows them to be used flush-mounted, or you need to use a parametric equalizer to do the job. You adjust it while carefully measuring the response, Hz by Hz, across the entire low end.
I also plan to add absorption panels to the rear sides to pick up first reflections.
The rear sides are way, way behind your first reflection points! Either that, or your model is HUGELY out of proportion.
I'm not sure what to think of the closet space,
Probably could just treat the doors with absorption. Attach reasonably thick absorbers to them. But won't the closet itself act as some kind of resonator? Or you could build a plug, or even a rolling gobo. several options.
Keep in mind that these mockups are not to scale. Any suggestions?
Well, I hate to be harsh, but if they are not to scale, then why did you post them? How can you expect people to offer useful advice if your model isn't even accurate: My suggestion would be to make your model accurate, address the issues I mentioned above, then post it again.
I listen at 95db usually, c weighting.
That's really loud! Twice as loud in fact (subjectively), as the normal maximum recommended level, which is 85 dB. At 95 dB your absolute maximum exposure time is 2 hours per day. If you go just a fraction louder to 97 dB, the maximum exposure time drops to 90 minutes.) If you go beyond those maximum exposure times, you run a very high risk of suffering permanent irreversible hearing damage, tinnitus, and a host of other hearing problems. At 85 dB you can listen safely 8 hours a day, but most professional engineers that I know of listen at even lower levels most of the time, and only turn it up to 85 to check the mix every now and then. Listening 8 hours a day at 95 dB, and you'll be partially deaf in a month. Irreversibly so.
As far as the back wall is concerned I kind of figured I will have to build the room to figure out what to do about the back wall,
I don't understand why! It should be easy to figure out what you should do on the back wall. That kind of goes without saying, if you want a room that sounds good and produces mixes that translate well: Bass traps in the corners, absorption or hangers for most of the rest, and perhaps some slot resonators: How thick / deep the absorption / hangers are depends on several factors, not the least of which is: how much space can you afford to sacrifice?
Obviously this site is about learning everything by reading it and no grains of wisdom for people on a time crunch.

By overwhelming I mean "I don't have the time to read and figure it all out in this short of time."
Once again I hate to be so blunt, but if that's the case then you probably shouldn't even be building it! You only have four options for building a studio: 1) spend the time to learn and design it right and build it right by yourself: 2) Don't spend that time, and end up building it wrong (that's guaranteed): 3) Pay a professional designer/builder (like John) to do it for you in the time available: 4) don't build at all. Option 1 takes somewhere from a few months to a few years, is relatively inexpensive, and usually results in success. Option 2 takes anywhere from a few hours to a few weeks, is much cheaper, and always results in failure. Option 3 takes a few weeks to a few months, is more expensive, and practically always results in success. Option 4 is instant, and saves you a stack of time, money and frustration, but doesn't get you a studio at all. The choice is up to you. There are no other options. I hope you don't feel insulted, or offended, because my intention is not to insult or offend. So if you want my help, I can help you in certain parts of your design, but not all by any means. But you have to do the basic leg work yourself, such as only posting accurate models, not confusing terms, and clarifying what you don't know, what you think you know, and what you do know. - Stuart -
