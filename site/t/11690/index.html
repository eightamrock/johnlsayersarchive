doublehelix13 February 2009#4
A couple of comments: It doesn't matter where you live or how cool the basement is under normal conditions, you absolutely *have* to have ventilation and fresh air!!! Air-tight rooms will also get hot very quickly, especially with gear and people in there. Lack of oxygen also makes people cranky and get tired easily, and you will have a difficult time making good music in an otherwise awesome build out. You must provide HVAC and fresh air, you will be so sorry later if you do not. You are going to all of this work, don't ruin it by skimping here. All I can say is you have been warned!!! :lol: Next, I am going to scold you a bit here (sorry!)... you should have thought about this earlier rather than waiting until you are all framed in and insulated. Your job will be more difficult now, but it is still do-able, so you are not sunk yet! HVAC is one of the most important things that you have to include in your planning, it impacts your ability to work *and* your ability to isolate sound. It is critical, yet it appears to me that you have left this to the end and have not put much thought into it. All I can say is that when you are done, you are going to wish that this was the FIRST thing you thought about, rather than the LAST. Poor planning here can ruin an otherwise great studio build. It is possible for you to cut into the existing air ducts, but that is not advisable. You really should be tying into the main trunk (usually a big rectangle-like metal "tube") where the smaller round ducts tie into. Cutting into smaller ducts is going to make them much less efficient at the far end. Once again, if you had been planning better earlier on, this would have been easier... Also remember that you not only need outlet vents, but you also need cold air returns in EACH room. Otherwise, it is like trying to blow air into a coke bottle, it just will not work. For example, in my control room, I have 2 exhaust vents, and 1 return vent. This is CRITICAL!!! Regarding sound transmission... Sound will travel through these ducts, without doubt, and it will be even worse if you are tying into the small round ducts rather than into the main trunk line as I described above. The best way to avoid this is to install a small, dedicated, split HVAC system dedicated just for your studio. Sounds like that is not going to happen at this point, especially if you are asking these questions so late in your build. One way to help *reduce* sound transmission through the ducts is to build duct silencers. Search this forum, there are several examples around here. These will help some, but will never truly isolate sound. Your sound isolation is only as good as your weakest link, and even if you build the most awesome wall/ceiling/window/door isolation in the world, if your HVAC leaks sound, you might have wasted a lot of money in the rest of the build out since sound is just going to POUR out of your HVAC vents. They are just like sound tunnels, and since you asked, insulating them does not help, it is still a hollow tube where sound will travel. Where air goes, so goes sound. Try to include as many right-angle bends as you can in the duct work, this will help some, and look into the silencers as I mentioned. Another issue to consider: When you tap into the main HVAC system, you are also now a slave to how someone else in the main part of the house has the thermostat set! This is especially bad in the winter when someone upstairs has the heater going, and you are suffocating down in the basement due to bodies and equipment. Trust me on this one!!! There are formulas around that are used to calculate how much HVAC you will need based on size, the amount of equipment, and the number of bodies that will be in the rooms, etc., and I can pretty much guarantee that you will NOT have enough if you are tapping into the main system!!! Check out this book, it will help you out immensely in this area: "Home Recording Studio Build it Like the Pros" By Rod Gervais ISBN: 1-59863-034-2 Amazon Link for Rod's book Good luck, you have some very difficult decisions to make, ones that should have been made a long time ago (sorry if I seem harsh!).
bassman15 February 2009#5
the one tip I could add to this is when tapping in to your existing HVAC, do so way back at the air handler unit and run metal lined duct to each room, send and return. Once inside the room, continue the duct around the ceiling at least one 90 turn and then soffit that in with drywall or you can create a frame around it, fill with rockwool and cover in fabric to create bass trapping as a part of the duct design. Bringing that duct around inside your room will help room-to-room isolation and quiet down the air noise a bit. -ashley

It would be worth it to make sure the system has the capacity. What I have often seen is that residential units are waay oversized. Oversized units do not work well since their duty cycle will be shortened and they will not de-humidify well. Contractors sometimes try to sell more equipment that the house needs. Sometimes clients think "bigger is better" and upsize the unit without understanding the reality of it all. Rod's book has really useful info on this. Doing the load calc is worth the hour or so with a calculator that it requires. It would be worth it to have a qualified HVAC guy do the calc for your whole house and see if the unit is properly sized. You might get lucky and find there is extra capacity to handle the studio! :yahoo: Beware that what cools the house to a certain degree might not be right for the studio so you might need some dampers or zoned thermostats to deal with that. HVAC is rough. If you want it right, its gonna cost ya.... -ashley

bassman21 February 2009#12
Third leaf effect is minimal and what we are trying to do is minimize the effect of the direct penetration of the room. Putting the air duct right at the penetration will leave you with weak points at the edge of the duct hole. Continuing the hard duct inside the room in a drywalled soffit around at least one corner provide better isolation and quieter air handling noise. At least that's the idea. It also allows you to raise a good portion of your ceiling height since most of the duct is incorporated inside the room. You can also bring in the supply and return at the same point and then route them to better locations in the room for optimal air flow and coverage. -ashley
