Carpfttx, The offer still stands and you'll always be welcome at the studio. As to being a "real facility," the jury's still out. :-) You'd be surprised what a little paint and some stained wood can do! :-) I think the door you are talking about was posted by Knightfly and the gist of the idea was to use a HOLLOW core door (i.e., real cheap) and then bond (w/ glue and screw) a layer of 3/4" MDF to each side. This gives you a M-A-M structure which he felt might be a dB or 2 better than a solid core door (and a lot cheaper too). This is how I'm building the doors in my studio that are NOT solid core. You should remember though that you should NOT use this where you have have double doors (like the passage between my big room and my vocal booth) because you end up with m-a-m-a-m-a-m (a quad leaf). Hope that helps. len

knightfly25 June 2006#82
Ya found the door thingy; now here's the deal on kraft paper, vapor, moisture, zen, and the art of saying NOTHING with the most words :? If you're talking about a wall that has no appreciable temperature difference between one side and the other, it's a non-problem; temperature DIFFERENCE is what drives vapor/moisture, and it will drive it from the HOT side to the COLD side; so Kraft paper (a VARIABLE-permeability material) needs to be placed as the first item behind the drywall on the HOT side, so vapor can't be driven INTO the wall - in addition, there needs to be NO such vapor barrier on the OTHER, COOLER side of said wall; this way, any moisture that DOES manage to get inside the wall still has an ESCAPE route and the wall will dry out. OUTSIDE walls - vapor barrier goes OUTSIDE the studs when there will be more cooling time inside; vapor barrier goes INSIDE the studs when there will be more HEATING time inside (as in, number of heating or cooling days per year) Acoustically, any vapor barrier that's part of the insulation WITHIN a wall needs to be against one or the other inner layer of wallboard, but NOT half-way BETWEEN the two - for a double-framed wall, this means that (for example) if your space is built in a bigger space, and that bigger space will NOT be kept at the same temperature as the INNER space, then you need to follow the upper paragraphs and put your Kraft facing toward the side that will be HOTTER more of the time. IF, however, the inner and outer space will both be controlled by the same heating/cooling system, then it doesn't matter as long as the paper is against ONE or the OTHER inside layer of wallboard. For OUTSIDE walls; follow the "barrier goes on the HOT side" rule, and ONLY on one side. RC - unless you have limited space and are using the same frames for inner wall layers as you are for OUTER wall layers, don't use RC at all. Just use 2x4 stud frames (two of them) spaced an inch or more apart, fill the ENTIRE cavity with "fluffy stuff" (if you can't get un-faced, get THICK, and follow above rules) - if you'll support your inner ceiling on your inner wall, be sure and DOUBLE the caps on the inner wall frames for better support. 2x4's on 24" centers gives better wall performance than on 16" centers - OFFSETTING the inner frame from the outer one by a foot MAY improve wall performance a dB or two (no tests to back this) but will make it a PAIN to walk around before all the wallboard is up. IF you can only put one layer on one side of your CR wall, I'd put the lighter one INSIDE; no difference in isolation between the two choices, but you will see a slight but noticeable improvement in low bass control INSIDE the CR PROVIDED you've slightly over-stuffed your insulation to prevent RINGING - this, because drywall on studs is your basic "low bass trap" centered somewhere around 60 hZ; the slight overstuff of insulation will broaden this peak (good) and damp any ringing (also good) Did I forget anything? (Hell, I ALWAYS forget something; it's what I do :roll: ) ... Steve
