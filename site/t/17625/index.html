The big red and white things behind the couch are hanging broadband panels.. I have them at 9" deep right now.
The won't be very effective there, in the middle of the room. Place them close to a wall and you get a free 6 dB boost in effectiveness. Place them close to two walls (eg, a corner) and you get a free 12 dB boost. Place them next to three surfaces (eg, two walls and a ceiling) and you get a free 18 dB boost. But place them in the middle of the room, far from all surfaces, and you get zero boost.
QUESTION: What angle should I splay the window at?
Not sure what you mean by "splay". That word normally refers to movement about the vertical axis, such that the window remains vertical, but the rear edge is move outwards (away from the room, into the wall). But your SketchUp doesn't show that: It seems to show the glass tilted outwards at the bottom (not vertical) but parallel to the other wall. That isn't necessary. It's one of those common myths, like carpets and egg crates: you do not need to angle your glass vertically for acoustically reasons. Doing so does NOT prevent reflections from happening, or at least, not at any reasonable angle. Try ray-tracing in the vertical plane, and you'll see that you need a huge angle before you can aim reflections away usefully. At small angles, all you succeed in doing, is changing the point on the glass where the reflection occurs: it just happens at a slightly higher point on the glass. That said, you might need to tilt your glass if the lighting in the room is badly designed, and you are getting glare on the glass. But a better solution is to design the lighting correctly!
There are of course two, the other side is the live room. Angle them both down?
Keep them both vertical, unless you have lighting issues that cannot be solved any other way. You could splay the entire side wall, windows included, to help solve your first reflection problem. That would work. If you angle them, you reduce the size of the air gap between them, thus reducing your isolation ("soundproofing"). You need to keep the air gap as large as you possibly can for maximum isolation, so any tilt on the glass is a bad idea, from that point of view.

but really?? If I have a 7'X4' 6" thick rear hanging broadband absorber, a corner absorber in one back corner, a couch in the back, and the hangers under the soffit, that's not enough low end absorbtion? not only that, but the absorbtion in the front of the control room will help with low end too.
I think the point Glenn was making is that you don't have enough absorption in general and bass trapping in particular, in your room. Despite all that you mention above, the answer to the question that you ask is: No, that is not enough. Theoretically, for a room with those dimensions, you need 436 sabins of absorption to achieve ITU/EBU recommendations. In other words, you need the equivalent of 436 square feet of open window. You only have a total of 968 square feet of surface area (considering all the walls, and the floor, and the ceiling), so you need the equivalent of nearly 50% of your entire interior room surface area being as absorbent as an open window. That's hard to achieve, considering the layout you show. The floor alone is 249 square feet, leaving you just 719 square feet of wall and ceiling space. A large part of what is left is taken up by windows, doors, the soffits, and other things, leaving you precious little space for absorption, and especially for low frequency absorption (bass traps). Thus, Glenn's recommendation for making good use of the ceiling/wall corners, where you get a "free" boost of 12 dB in effectiveness for bass trapping (theoretically). And once again, those absorbers hung in open air are not very efficient: They need to be close to a surface for maximum efficiency, and yes there is a complex relationship between distance/frequency/effectiveness/standing waves/etc.

Yes, you really do!!! :) Proper duct liner is designed to go inside ducts, not outside them, and you do need it, both in the ducts that pass through your room as well as those that you will design as part of your own HVAC system. However, you should also be aware that adding duct liner inside an existing duct reduces the cross sectional area to some extent, thus forcing an increase in air velocity, which in turn means more noise... :) Catch-22!
