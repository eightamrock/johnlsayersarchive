sharward28 September 2005#4
My-oh-my -- someone's sitting on your toilet already! :lol: Greetings and welcome, Deven (and Monica)! :) My first reaction to your plan was that this is more than a hobby space. This is a business. You plan to have bands over, presumably for a fee, and bands are made up of people who are customers. The same is true of your wife's occupation: students are customers. A quick Google search turned up this handy document about the City of Portland's home occupation (business) rules. Assuming you live in Portland's city limits, review this document carefully to ensure that you can legally run these businesses. You'd fall into the category of "Type B." I don't know if the garage conversion would be considered to be an accessory dwelling unit or not (the inclusion of a bathroom might make it so, even if you don't want it to be!) -- if so, then a Type B business would prohibited. Assuming that the city will allow you to build what you want to build, you may need to merge your and your wife's businesses together as one entity. That's because you can't have more than one Type B businesses on the property. If you treat both occupations as one business, that might fly. Lest you think all of this isn't worth worrying about, consider that just one complaint from one neighbor (who may not even live in your neighborhood yet) could trigger an investigation that could shut you down. :shock: You don't want to be living your dream just under the radar -- do you? :roll: In short, do all of the research that is necessary to confirm that you won't run into any legal troubles later that would devastate the sizable investment you plan to make. Find out what you can do and what you can't, and what compromises you may be forced to make in order to fulfill this dream. --Keith :mrgreen:

Started to reply to your post in Construction, followed your link here, so here goes; first of all, if you need "ammunition" for approvals, this link http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4400 May help convince neighbors/bureaucrats that good isolation is very attainable when done correctly. I have some concerns about that in regard to your existing roof construction, saw the pix of it on this thread; would need to know what you have in mind for existing, as well as the new construction. Things like # of layers, what material, where it will go, and layer by layer description of what the sound would have to pass through traveling from the outside world to your inner space. That truss layout looks doable, but it's good that you've not yet had an actual truss designer involved yet; when you do, specify that you need at LEAST 10 PSF dead load capability, more if possible. And before you even do THAT, I need to know your answers to the previous paragraph on layers/leaves, etc - your outer framing should also be capable of higher than normal PSF loading, and The hardest part may be determining a method of good sound isolation without causing moisture retention problems. These people have an amazing amount of info re this - here's just one page of their site http://www.buildingscience.com/resource ... efault.htm In fact, the last link on the page is one I've not yet seen/read, just did a quick browse thru it http://www.buildingscience.com/resource ... tilate.pdf I'm still working on a usable compromise between mold vs. isolation - I live about 90 miles south of you (East of Albany) so I'm familiar with climate problems in Oregon, and this isn't something you should gloss over. More when I get time... Steve
