You probably don't want to hear this, but you are setting yourself up for disappointment if you plan to track live acoustic drums late at night on the second floor of a house in a residential neighborhood, on a very limited budget. Drums are the hardest of all instruments to isolate anyway, even under the best of conditions (ground floor, slab on grade, etc.), but second floor makes it harder still. Drums put out a huge amount of energy in low frequencies, and those are the toughest ones to isolate. Drums are also the loudest of all instruments. There are things you can do to attenuate them somewhat, but each additional decibel of isolation costs more than the last one as you try to get more and more. dB is a logarithmic scale anyway, so the math is not heartening. A typical house wall isolates to around 30 dB, realistically. Improving that to 40 dB is relatively simple, sinve you only have to block ten times as much power. But making it 50 dB means blocking one hundred times as much power, and making it 60 dB means blocking one those times as much. 70 dB is ten thousand times as much.... I think you see the issue here. Drums can easily put out 115 dB, and more. Most residential noise regs insist on levels below 45 dB at night: Do the math, and see how much isolation you need in order to get 115 down to 45. That's a tall order even for room built on a really good concrete slab on the ground. But for a second-story room, on a typical joist-and-plywood floor, that's a really tough proposition. So I would first measure how loud your typical session is, using a sound level meter, then check that against how quiet you need to be, legally. Subtract B from A, and the answer is how many dB of isolation you need. Based on that, you can look at the documentation to find a construction method that will get you there, but there aren't many possibilities for such high levels of isolation on the second floor, and they all cost money to implement. You still need ducts, especially in a studio. Bass trapping on the side walls is pretty ineffective, considering that the modal nodes are in the corners, and the nulls are on the walls. If you wanted to trap along the walls, then it would have to be pressure based devices, such as membrane traps. Also, "thin" and "bass trap" do not go together.... The frequencies where room modes are problematic correspond to very long wavelengths, on the order of tens of feet, so bass trapping has to be substantial.

For three leaf, the optimum technique is to have equal sized air gaps, and with most of the mass on the middle leaf, such that 2 x M1 = M2 = 2 x M3. So you need about twice as much mass, or twice the air gap, or a combination of both, to get the same isolation. It does not matter which side you put the stud on: MSM is governed only by the mass of the leaves and the distance between them, so the decision on building inside-out or conventionally is purely related to room design concept, use of space, and treatment.

I'd really suggest that you re-think that: If you don't run conduit for your cables they you are pretty much screwed when you need to run more cables in the future, or change something, or repair something. Conduit is dirt cheap, compared with the cost of tearing holes in the wall a few months down the line to add something you forgot... or worse still, doing without something that you need! This is probably the second biggest issue I have run unto with my post production customers over the years: they ALWAYS need to add something else as needs change, and without conduit they end up spending big money to do that. For the sites that I design, without fail I specify everything in conduit, and over-sized conduit. It just makes things so much easier.
As far as the conduit goes I'm going to hire someone to install the hvac system, as well as an electrician to put all the lighting and outlets needed for my inner wall of the LR, but for my CR the house's standard outlets do just fine through a few battery backups and power conditioners.
The conduit is not for the HVAC, or for wiring within the room: it is for getting the cables into the rooms in the first place, and also running them between the rooms. All of the wiring within the room is surface-mount anyway, and a structured cabling system is best for that (if you are building conventionally), or just plain wiring (if you are building inside out). You cannot do conventional sockets, switches and light fittings in a studio, for obvious reasons: surface mount is your only option.
