The drywall is 5/8".
That's good! It would be sad if it was 1/2", but 5/8" is great. So more tips: When you get down to the first layer of drywall, make sure that all the joints between sheets are sealed. Use acoustic caulk if you can get, or if not then any good quality caulk that remains flexible even when it is fully dry. Also caulk underneath the drywall (where it meets the floor) and at the top (where it meets the ceiling). And when you put the second layer on, stagger the joints between panels, so that nothing lines up form layer to layer (in other words, joints in the first layer are nowhere near joints in the second layer). And caulk the second layer like the first. The idea is to make that wall totally airtight. - Stuart -

"How to "Stagger-Stud" and soundproof existing stud walls using Sound Isolation Padding Tape: Assuming the wallboard has been removed and the studs are exposed- Apply 2 layers of the green 1/8"X1-7/16" tape to every other stud. This will build out the stud by about a 1/4". The tape has a peel-off backing and may be cut with scissors. Place your wall panels over the studs and fasten them only to the studs which have the tape. If the other wall is open do this to it also, alternating the studs, that is, do not apply tape to studs which have tape on them already on the other side. This system breaks the sound path through the studding and is very effective and inexpensive for reducing sound transfer through the wall. If you don't do anything else, do this! It can double the sound reduction of your wall, especially at speech frequencies. Ted White17 June 2010#32
Damping requires surface area. Like the surface area between two sheets of drywall. Damping cannot take place in any real sense with the small surface area on the edge of a stud or joist. The assembly you describe is an attempt to create a decoupled system, however you'll be driving screws through the tape, which couples the system effectively. So no decoupling takes place, and no damping.

Having worked with this foam in a lab environment several years ago, I was not at all impressed with its ability to accomplish decoupling via a pseudo-staggering. The final compressed thickness is so small that you risk the untreated studs warping even slightly and providing an intermittent contact which you'll hear. Your comparison to wood furring applied to the studs is different, in that a much more sizeable and definitive gap would be created. So I would much prefer your wood furring suggestion, if applied horizontally 24" OC (to your excellent building code point). The foam is simply not worth the cost and is a liability in this application in my opinion, even if the studs were 12" OC

The only serious development of the method I am aware of is that of Ben Sharp. Adding to your last quoted line above, the improvements in TL were in the mid and upper frequencies. The region that is not of concern to us in studio isolation. Note to others reading the thread. In isolation of music performance spaces, the most difficult range to isolate is the low frequencies. If a partition is designed to adequately isolate the low frequencies, then the amound of isolate at higher frequensies is significantly greater than what is absolutely required. This is also the reason for one of the few true generalizations in acoustics, being "if you design for adequate low frequency isolation, the rest of frequencies will look after themselves."
