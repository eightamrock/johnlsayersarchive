The metal profiles I was writing about are called z-channels in america, I think. Basically metal channels that are attached to the framing. The gypsum then is attached to that channel, so that it's not touching the wood framing. It's supposed to decrease sound leaking through the wall.
You seem to be confusing "Z-channel" with "resilient channel" (abbreviated "RC"). It is not the same thing. Unless you specifically get proper RC, then it isn't resilient and wont help at all, acoustically. And you don't need it anyway, in your case, since you are already building separate stud frames for your leaves. RC does not "decrease sound leaking through the wall". What it does is to decouple the drywall from the frame, so that vibration cannot be transmitted from the drywall on one side of a wall through the frame to drywall on the other side. But you are already fully decoupling your two sides, since you are building separate frames, so you do not need RC. It will not help at all. It will just waste your money. Forget it.
The Layer "Röhren" are some pipes that come through the north wall. I laid out the locker so that the space where the pipes pass through is IN the locker.
But there is also a white rectangular thing on that layer that passes through the rear of the control room, touching all of the leaves and thereby short-circuiting all of your isolation! That's the one I'm talking about. Is that an HVAC duct? Whatever it is, it needs to be decoupled form the leaves and encased in its own "leaf", to prevent sound transmission.
Now for soffit mounting: At first I was determined to do it, but then i came across this article: http://www.mercenary.com/howdoitknbmo.html It just sounds a little overwhelming with trial and error to do it really right (at least the way they write). I would still love to have them soffit mounted but that article really turned me off :?
Like a lot of stuff on the internet, there is some truth in that article, mixed with a lot of hype. The basic concept of soffit mounting is simple: you build an "infinite baffle", which is just a fancy name for a very large flat, rigid, massive, surface that extends the front panel of your speaker in all directions, as much as you can. That's it. Not complex. What it does is to make the speaker driver radiate all of its sound in only one direction: straight ahead. High frequencies do that anyway, but low frequencies don't. Low frequencies (bass) try to "wrap around" behind the back of a speaker, where they muddy up the sound and cause all kinds of strange issues. All that the "soffit" does is to stop the bass sound from "wrapping around" behind the speaker, and force it to go where it should be going anyway. Simple concept. What it does for you is to tighten up your bass, making it much clearer, more focused, and allowing you to hear details that would be lost otherwise. That's the basic idea, and it isn't hard to understand, or to do properly. All that you need is some kind of large flat, heavy panel, such as plywood, MDF, HDF, or even concrete, and you make a hole in the middle of it, just a little bit larger than the front face of your speaker. Then you position your speaker so that the front face of the speaker is flush with the front surface of the "panel". You fill the area behind the panel with something acoustically absorbent, such as pink fiberglass insulation, or mineral wool, or old socks ( :) ). You mount it so that everything is decoupled mechanically, and you are done! Easy. The only other thing you need to take into account is that you have to "roll off" the bass response of the speaker itself by 6 dB, since it will be radiating into "half-space", instead of "full-space". That's not a big problem, because many studio speakers have built-in controls to do exactly that. There is very often a switch or potentiometer on the back of the speaker that you simply set to "-6 db", and you are done! It's not nearly as bad as that article makes out.

That's one of the "wrong" things. :) You should not have any of the building structure inside your inner leaf, nor touching it. If you do, then any vibration at all in the entire building will be transmitted to your walls, and make them vibrate like the cone of a loudspeaker... Those beams and pillars can be in the air gap, but the cannot be part of your structure, and you cannot use them to support the ceiling or walls. So you should build your rear inner-leaf just inside of those beams/pillars, on its own frame, and not touching. If you still want to use the depth of that space between those beams for bass trapping (as you show on your model, and which is a good idea), then you can build your inner-leaf structure so that it fits around the beams, but without touching them. the same is true at the front of the room: you need to disconnect those beams from both the inner and outer leaves (because the "outer" leaf of the control room at that point is the "inner" leaf of the live room).
The stuff in the "Röhren" layer is not going to affect any leafs, so I wonder what there still is wrong in my plan
