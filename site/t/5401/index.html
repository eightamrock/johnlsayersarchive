knightfly9 January 2006#6
OK, we're getting closer; in that same link, there's a comment about acoustics being THREE-dimensional - I can give you a better idea of what you may need to do if you include the Height of your room as well as any odd shapes, like alcoves, columns, etc - if possible, a sketch would be best (or picture) - About traps - The term "bass trap" has been thrashed to death in this type forum, and seldom is it used correctly - a true "bass trap" would ONLY trap bass - examples would be the panel type traps like those on Ethan's site (the DIY ones, not the ones sold on the Realtraps site) - A panel trap works on a combination of panel resonance and the internal insulation, where the panel vibrates at resonant frequency and tries to move the air INSIDE the trap - the insulation mounted inside (not touching the front panel) tries to STOP the moving air, in the process turning some of the sound energy into heat as it tries to get through the small fissures/cracks between the glasswool or mineral wool fibers of the insulation. More energy is lost in flexing the front panel, so these traps are a relatively narrow band trap typically within an octave either side of resonance. They can be built to trap frequencies down to around 50 hZ with the right dimensions. Another type of resonant trap is the Helmholtz resonator, also known in one popular form as a "slat" or "slot" resonator. Types of helholtz resonators include "perforated panel" resonator, slat and slot resonators (one and the same thing), and "barrel" type resonators, not usually built that much. Helmholtz traps reportedly don't work well below about 100-150 hZ, but I'm not sure that couldn't be gotten around by proper placement and design. All resonant traps need to be placed at points of maximum sound PRESSURE in order to work best - this is NOT true of absorbent type traps. The point of maximum sound pressure at ALL frequencies in any room will be its tri-corners, because that's where all the boundaries come together, and a boundary forces a sound wave to STOP and turn around - therefore, it is a point of maximum sound PRESSURE and minimum sound VELOCITY. This means that resonant traps belong against walls and at specific points ALONG those walls depending on which frequency you're trying to treat. It also means that, since these traps are frequency-SPECIFIC, they are NOT a good "first thing" to do unless your room has absolutely HORRIBLE Length/width/height ratios that cause gross unevenness in frequency response. Which brings us to what a lot of people INCORRECTLY call bass traps - the BROADBAND absorber. I've even heard the term, "broadband bass trap" - if it's BROADBAND, that means it absorbs EVERYTHING, not just bass... Absorptive traps (broadband) - are typically made from 3 PCF (sometimes denser) fiberglass, mineral, or rockwool - higher densities tend to NOT give as even absorption as 3 PCF, although for corner traps up to maybe 6 PCF can work - this is partly because people are after more bass absorption usually when making corner traps, and because the trap surface ends up closer to perpendicular to sound waves when it's across a corner, so "grazing effect" isn't as noticeable (that's where a sound hits the panel at an angle OTHER than perpenducular - higher density materials cause more of this indirect sound to bounce off rather than be absorbed) I know corner traps take up space, but they are one of the best "first" things you can do to a room, for reasons mentioned above. Next, in roughly equal importance, would be sidewall absorbers (first reflection points), "clouds", or ceilng absorbers, and rear wall (deep) absorbers. Typical side/ceiling traps are 2 to 4" of absorbent placed an equal distance away from the wall, with NO BACKING - this mounting improves the bandwidth giving more even sounding treatment, without the additional cost of twice the material thickness. In a room that's less than about 18 feet long, diffusion is an "iffy" proposition in most cases - I've only heard a few people claim otherwise, and have seen/heard no proof that it can work. Carpeting in a room is rarely a good idea, unless you take the time to calculate each and every square foot of every kind of material in the room so you get an overall, even absorption coefficient at all frequencies - usually it's simpler to leave the floor hard (wood, concrete, vinyl, etc) and absorb the ceiling. You want some sort of absorption on ONE of each parallel pair of surfaces, or you'll get flutter echo. Some links - http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=4581 http://www.recording.org/ftopict-26889.html HTH... Steve
