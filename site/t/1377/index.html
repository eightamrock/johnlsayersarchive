dymaxian25 February 2004#6
Ok, maybe i went off the deep end with this... Might want to open the PDF in another window so you can switch back and forth easier. With a room that size, absorption is going to be your best bet. So what I'm showing is 2x4 studs except for the back wall, which I made out of 2x8s. That deep stud space in the back will give you a real deep broadband absorber in the back. I dont think you'll be able to do this for $400, but you can get started and take it in stages. Stage 1: get the framing up, and put only one layer of drywall on the outside (you can add another later if you decide you want better isolation, but you mentioned that's not so much of a concern). I'd build the door myself, too- just make it out of 1x4 framing, put 5/8" drywall (maybe add a sheet of 1/2" plywood to the back for durability), stuff the inside with blanket insulation and cover it with fabric- maybe put a layer of 703 under the fabric too, up to you. Every little bit will help, even on the door (it's 1/3 of that wall!) Then, on the inside, stuff all the stud spaces you see with 4" fiberglass blanket insulation. Cover that with fabric, stapled to the studs. This'll make all your walls pretty absorbent, and your room will sound pretty dead, but it will suck up all but the really low stuff, so it'll get you by until you can do more. Stage 2: If you can afford to put 2" of rigid fiberboard insulation on the front and back walls during Stage 1, do so. Otherwise, that'd be the next step. Cover it up again with fabric. Stage 3: Refit the side walls. Get the fabric out of the way (hold onto it, you'll be putting it back in a bit) and then nail a 1x8 alongside each of the wall studs (or a 1x6 spaced 2" out- the idea is to have a 4" or so offset from the face of the studwall). Here you can attach 1" rigid fiberboard under the fabric under wood slats, all at an angle, so the walls would be more live without creating flutter echoes. I'd personally use 1x2 slats spaced 2" apart, but you might want to see if anyone else on the board has a suggestion- for a room this small, you might want to adjust the absorption vs. liveness... If you can get away with it *snicker* you can angle the side walls out just a little bit to keep those walls from being parallel... if you don't think your wife will mind (or notice) this'll help considerably, and it won't make the room all that much bigger. If you're building this in the basement, and you don't have much $ to put into it, I'd think you could just build right up to the floor joists above. You could try to angle the ceiling, but I can almost guarantee that'll blow your budget. Best to just build up to the ceiling, drywall flat to the floor joists and hang a 703 "cloud" to keep flutter echoes down... I think you'll find that isolation will become more important to you down the road. In the basement, if you're anywhere near the furnace and water heater, you'll be cursing every time they kick on. If your laundry center is downstairs, you'll be unplugging the washer when you want to mix. But like I said, you can start off with a single layer of drywall and add more if you need to. Good luck! Kase www.minemusic.net

I'm going to be alternating between 1x2 and 1x4 slats on my walls. But they're pretty cheap, so that shouldn't pose too much of a problem. If you're not doing this yourself, then you'll probably pay your carpenter more for the labor on that part than anything else- it's kinda tedious... Biggest thing for you in that size of a room is to get that rear wall absorber. Kase www.minemusic.net

knightfly28 February 2004#15
If you can get 2" 703, I would stick with that for nearly ALL your inside treatment. That small a room will have a LOT of low mid problems, so you would likely get better results by spacing some of the 703 off the wall by 6-8 inches where you can. The lighter, standard insulation has nowhere near the density of 703 and will take out too much air without removing any of the "boxiness" of a small room. In that small an area, you may want to experiment with complete absorption and then play some commercial CD's in the room, adding some electronic reverb til it sounds right - this would become a permanent part of your monitoring chain, so you would need at least two different reverbs - one for the permanent monitor system, and one to add to your actual mixes. Then, you'd need to record the mix WITHOUT the "permanent" reverb, and "dial it in" by listening on other systems/places til you get your mixes to "travel" well. Here's the "disclaimer" part - I've NOT actually tried that technique, so I can't swear that it would work - my main point is, that small an area needs to be really dead to get rid of all the modal problems it causes... Steve
