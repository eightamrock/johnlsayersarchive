Dolphran - welcome to the site and hello from the neighborhood. The first thing a mod will comment on this thread is the image size. http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?t=7572 I'm not an expert in this field. I've been working on a similar basement room as yours (mine is entirely below grade though) and I have a moderate amount of experience with home remodelling. On the baseplate/dricore thing - Reference their FAQ
list. Stud walls can be set right on top of your new DRIcore subfloor. Install your 2X4 walls directly on top of the DRIcore panels, measuring for a “snug” fit between the panels and ceiling. Once the walls are framed and in position, simply screw the bottom plates of the 2X4 wall to the DRIcore panels, using 2" wood screws. To finish the anchoring process, we suggest pinning the bottom plate to the concrete floor using either 3" concrete nails or self-tapping concrete fasteners. Only minimal fastening to the concrete is required. For example, on an 8 foot length of wall, you would require only 2 fastening points (one at both ends). Again, only minimal fastening to concrete is required. Too many fastening points breaking through the polyethylene moisture barrier may defeat the moisture resistant benefit of the DRIcore subfloor system. NOTE: If you are planning to use metal studs in place of wood, the base track for the studs should be affixed to the DRIcore panels with wood screws. Metal stud base plates should be anchored to panels every 2’ when installing over DRIcore panels. As above, 2 fasteners should penetrate the panel and go into concrete, securing the wall into place.
Personally I'd prefer to use pressure treated plates. I have doubts about the weight capacity of the wafer board and moisture barrier these are constructed with. Remember - with sound isolation construction, we are putting a bit more mass on the wall and ceiling assemblies. If your concrete is not level - and I mean .5 or .25'' of variance you'd be dealing with pressure points under those heavy walls. And in the spots where you do nail through to keep the wall from kicking out or in - if moisture gets through by wicking or flooding through, you're compromising crappy lumber around this point. whatever wafer board those things are made from. Other weight factors - Your concern is with noise travelling up above? If you go the route of an independantly framed ceiling, you'll be adding MORE weight to your wall. Your best and most direct resource is Rod's book on amazon. Search for "Rod Gervais" and you'll find it. It's a perminent fixture next to the hopper in our place (sorry rod - it's a place I routinely read things...) right next to the Sports Illustrated Red Sox World Series Champions 2004 commemorative edition... But I digress. Other Thoughts Your all covered up with plastic there with the existing framework. If you're concerned with noise to and from the above space, think of these: Please indicate what the first floor is constructed with. What thickness of subfloor and what is the finished floor above. What are the joists made from? That center beam? You'll need to box that in as well. Are you dealing with a support column? Utility piping & wiring? Does it run perpendicular to the floor joists? Do you need to be able to have access to areas like this? How high is the ceiling? Code in most new england states will ask for a minimum of 7' for rec room use. The door is not soundproof because sound proof is an impossibility. What's the manufacturer or model for it? More importantly - what is the seal for it like? The defacto rubber seal method is covered all over this forum and in rods book. Basement water? Have you ever had a problem? Is this past experience why you are interested in having that sort of flooring installed? The best investment we made for our basement was to have basement tech of NH install a parameter drainage system. As you know, we get whacked twice a year with some heavy flow - perhaps the grade of your home helps out in this situation... On flooring in basements - others may have a preference related to sound isolation, but I know how wet we can get in NE and I opt for Tile or a laminate that can withstand mosture. I avoid wood products because wood will bloat and get brittle through time. Treating a room after these surfaces are established is a good plan from what I've read so far.

Cram24 March 2008#19
The optimum and most effective setup would be for you to use that layer of drywall as the second leaf with no suspended ceiling stucture below that. - If you're worrying about parallel surface reflection - treat it with a hung trap or cloud on the ceiling. - It will give you more head room. - Make sure you research the different methods of constructing a ceiling: using islation clips with furring/hat channels or Isolation clips that suspend an independant frame. Or even an independantly (not touching) ceiling frame which rests upon walls built with headers. If you for some reason are stuck on the idea of a suspended ceiling. Make sure you read performance data for the material in question. Read the effectiveness for the frequency isolation of the sounds you're planning to isolate. The answer to your question of hurting the isolation- basically, having multiple leaves will create frequency ranges which are nearly boosted or that they resonate between the three leaves.
