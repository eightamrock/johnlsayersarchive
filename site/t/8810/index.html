Eric_Desart22 May 2007#2
If these mattresses are open cell polyether foam (typical for cheaper mattresses) then they do work, but it's a matter of quality. The typical density and type of foam makes that they have a low gas-flow resistance, which causes relative poor absorption compared to traditional acoustic foam (or mineral wool). This low gas-flow resistance, disadvantage for acoustic use, is useful in mattresses since it makes the foam breath easier. The type of polyether foam, versus the often used polyester for acoustics, also has advantages for mattresses since it's better moist resistant and more elastic (+ cheaper). Hence use as mattresses and acoustic absorption calls technically for different properties to be optimized. While seemingly comparable to laymen, they really are designed with a different purpose in mind. You can use them, if you accept the (significant? - depends on exact type) poorer absorption properties. But check the fire properties of this foam. And this also is only valid if your mattresses are what I think they are. Foam is a generic name covering lots of different types and products. Mostly/often, when budget to buy more suitable absorption is not an issue, one advices against the use of this mattress or upholstery foam for acoustic absorption.

recorestudio wrote:

No recore, it won't help. What I described with this low gas-flow resistance, means in fact that the existing mini holes in this foam as is, are too large already (this is a bit a poetic license). You can blow too easily through that foam. You can not improve that foam itself with whatever technique. It is what it is.
