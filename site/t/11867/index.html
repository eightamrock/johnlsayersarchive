I showed that section of the book to the contractor and we are good to go. Regarding the three leaf issues. What do you mean?
On the side of your vocal booth, facing the passage (I think it is a passage? Or maybe a storage room?) you have a wall that has three leaves in it. That will reduce the isolation properties of that wall at low frequencies. Your walls should have only two leaves. Never one, never three, never more: Only two: Mass-Spring-Mass. A couple of layers of drywall/MDF/plywood/whatever attached to one stud frame on one side: that is your first "Mass". Then comes an air gap filled with insulation. That is your "Spring". Then comes another couple of layers of drywall/MDF/plywood/whatever attached to a second stud frame. That's your second "Mass". All together you have Mass-Spring-Mass. That's what you want for a wall. If you add another bunch of drywall/etc with another air gap, then your isolation goes DOWN, not up. You can lose isolation in the low frequencies like that, unless you add a LOT more mass to compensate. It would be far easier to deal with that third leaf now on paper, than to try to do something about it during your actual construction.

Soundman20204 April 2009#15
I would like to do a double framed wall with staggered studs, and double drywall on the outside faces. This seems to be whats recommended here and best bang for the buck....no?
Maybe it's just a semantics thing? To me "double framed wall" and "staggered studs" are two different things. "Double framed" means that you have to entirely separate stud frames, with nothing joining them (except the floor), and the studs are not staggered. Each frame has its own sole plate (bottom plate), its own top plates, adn the studs all line up with each other. "Staggered studs" means that you have one single sole plate that is wider than the studs, and a single set of top plates of the same width as the sole plate. The studs join the plates (obviously!) and are set on alternating faces of the plates. For example, a staggered stud wall might have 2x6 plates, with 2x4 studs on it, where the odd-numbered studs will line up with one edge of the plates and will carry one bunch of drywall, while the even numbered studs will line up with the other edge of the plates, and will carry the drywall for the other side of the wall. Wow! That sounded complicated! reading it back, I'm not even sure if I understand myself what I just wrote!! :) So with a staggered stud wall, the top and bottom plates are actually common to both sides of the wall, and they will transmit sound to some extent. A staggered stud wall is better than a single stud frame wall, but not as good as a double stud frame wall. Go for double stud if you have the space, or staggered stud if space is really tight. So are you planning on staggered stud walls, or double stud walls? Double are better, but take up a bit more space. If they are double, then they don't need to be staggered (I guess you could stagger them, but it would not serve any useful acoustic purpose, and would take up even more space).
(16" OC or 24" OC not sure)
Go for 24" OC if you can: It brings down the resonant frequency of your wall slightly, which is a good thing. But check on your ceiling construction too: Your inner leaf ceiling will need plenty of support, and if you need to go to 16"OC for your ceiling joists, then you should probably go to 16"OC for your studs to, so that you have a stud exactly under the end of each joist. If you can do your ceiling safely with 24"OC, then great! Do 24"OC for the walls too, once again with a stud directly under each end of each joist. - Stuart -

Soundman20206 April 2009#22
im pretty sure its steel framed with one layer of drywall.
You mean one layer on EACH SIDE of the steel frame, or one layer on ONE SIDE ONLY, with the other side open to the world? If it is one layer on each side, then you will need to take of the layer from the other side. You could also use that drywall (take it off carefully!) to beef up the other side, in between the studs, for extra isolation.
How should i have my corners so the leafs are isolated?
Like Ian said: "Box in a box". Make sure that NO part of the inner-leaf wall touches the outer-leaf wall. Two separate, unconnected walls. So the walls inside your vocal booth ONLY touch each other, and they support the inner ceiling, which ONLY touches the inner walls. None of that has any mechanical connection with the outer wall. If there is any connection at all (even one single nail) then you are "short-circuiting" your isolation, which will reduce your transmission loss considerably. - Stuart -
