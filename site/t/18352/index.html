Any hard reflective surface will work fine. If you don't like concrete, then a really good option often used in studios, is laminate flooring, properly laid over a suitable underlay. Other options are ceramic or stone tiles, and linoleum. There are many attractive, good quality surfaces these days, so whichever you happen to like most will be fine.
The walls in the vocal booth will be inside out, to create as dead a space as possible. I am still unsure what treatment to use on the walls of the live room though.
There are many possibilities: it's a live room, so you probably want it to have some sort of "character" or "vibe" that makes it attractive to your target clientele. For example, if you are going to be recording classical music, opera singers, church choirs or similar genres, then you'd probably want it to be more reflective, "echoey" even, with a much longer reverb time than if you wanted to record contemporary rock or pop groups, which would probably want a "deader". Jazz would be different again, with a more diffuse sound. So design it for the type of clientele you want to attract. And if you don't know yet, or if you want it to be good for all possibilities, then make the acoustics variable: folding or sliding devices that can be opened or closed or moved to reveal more or less areas of absorption, reflection, or diffusion, as needed for each session.
I will be recording a lot of acoustic music, but will also be using the space for rehearsals with live drums and amped guitars at some point. I can't see us exceeding 90db though.
:shock: Rehearsal with live drums and guitar cabs is going to be way, way louder than that! At least one hundred times louder, and probably a thousand (in terms of sound power). In other words, in the range 110 dB to 120 dB. I have never met a drummer who can keep it down to 90 dB during rehearsal. Maybe with soft brushes on a very quiet romantic ballad, but not on a typical rock song. So I think you are way underestimating your sound levels. I would suggest that you buy a sound level meter, set it to "C" weighting and slow response, the measure a typical session that you expect would be representative of your what is going to happen in your room.
If anyone would like to weigh in on the designs below, ... Double stud walls, with 2x15mm plasterboard ... outer wall.
That's a three leaf wall. That will not give you optimum isolation for low frequency sounds, such as drums, bass, keyboards, etc. If you want optimum isolation, then you should use only 2-leaf walls. Also, you seem to have the condenser/compressor unit of a mini-split system in between your middle-leaf wall and your outer-leaf wall: How does that vent to the outside world? Those things need a huge amount of outside air flow to work, so there must be some pretty major venting in there that you don't show. It would be good if you could show that in your model, and also your calculations, so we can check that you really are allowing enough external airflow through that unit. Is there a reason why you didn't just put that outside, where it would normally go in a typical installation? Having it indoors like that really complicates things unnecessarily. You also seem to be wasting a LOT of space to accommodate that, and there's also the issue of where the condensate line will drain to: is there a floor drain at that location, such as an old bathroom drain? If not, then where will the water go' You also seem to be showing only one mini-split system, yet you mentioned two rooms: "live room" and "vocal both". How are you going to cool and dehumidify the vocal booth? It's a much smaller room, so it needs (proportionally) more ventilation, cooling and dehumidifying, to keep conditions usable. You also don't show the fresh air ventilation system, or the stale air extraction system, which are key parts of any HVAC design. How are you going to get fresh air into each of the rooms, and how are you going to exhaust stale air from them to the outside world? Here too, it would be good if you could shoe the entire system (along with the calculations), to make sure that there are no red flags with that. HVAC is generally the most overlooked part of all home-studio design. Finally, you don't show insulation anywhere in your model, but that is also critical, both for isolation and for treatment. Once again, it would be good if you could show it, so we can confirm if your plan is covering all bases, in that respect. And photos of the actual building would help too! :) - Stuart -

Johnnie9 August 2013#9
You're not forgotten! I'm personally in the "build it slowly and cheap" camp, so I know exactly what you mean about problems with HVAC. When I was reading through Rod Gervais Book, he had a suggestion for an "exchange chamber" approach to HVAC issues which was good for me. I see that you have a kitchen/open space too. Have you considered an exchange chamber approach? It seems well suited for the "do it yourself" types if a mini-split is stretching the budget too far. Basically, the concept is to have all your air treatment happening in the exchange chamber (and equipment that is sized to handle the total space) And then you run ducts to/from your spaces. All the fans/equipment/noise is occurring in the exchange chamber (and you put silencers on the penetrations into the live space) The obvious downside, is that zone control is out, but it strikes me as a pretty good system to use in a studio with relatively few rooms, that has a space that would be appropriate to use an exchange chamber.
