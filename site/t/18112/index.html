I thought it would give the whole bit a very "artsy" look, from what i read (on this forum no less) people OPT to go for non parallel whenever given the opportunity. Just figured i'd jump on the band wagon.
For a live room (tracking room), "artsy" is just fine. Many live rooms are designed with a specific "character" in mind, both acoustically and aesthetically. But for a control room, the acoustics just MUST be as neutral as possible. It must not "sound good": rather it must not "sound" anything. That's the entire point of a control room: its a place that tells the absolute truth about what the mics picked up, so you can make mix decisions based on what is actually recorded and coming out your speakers, not based on what the room did with that after it left the speaker. So there's a process and a philosophy that goes into designing a control room. Yes, the walls can be angled if that's the philosophy you are following. For rooms based on the RFZ or similar philosophy, that's a given: you will have to angle the walls to achieve the design goal of not having any reflections reaching the engineers head within the Haas time, and until they are sufficiently attenuated. There's no other way of doing that. But if you are following some other design philosophy, then splaying the walls might not be part of that: You still might want to splay them to deal with flutter echo, but flutter can be dealt with in other ways. Or you might want to splay them just because it looks cool, and that's fine too! As long as you take that into account when designing the rest of the room. so you can splay your walls if you want to, but you have to know WHY you want to, and then splay them at the correct angles... There are studios with splayed walls, and there are studios with purely rectangular shapes: both can be made to work just fine, provided that all the factors are taken into account, and the room is treated suitably. And yes, most people do opt for splayed walls in their control rooms, because most people are aiming for RFZ these days, or at least heading in that direction as far as possible.

You lose a few inches on room width, but that might or might not be a good thing, depending on your room ratio.... Which kind of leads to the subject of room ratios and modal response! :) I'm sure you know that sounds travels as waves in air, and for each tone (frequency) there is corresponding wavelength, the distance between any two complete "cycles" that the wave goes through. So if a wave happens to fit in exactly between two walls, it can bounce back and forth between them, reinforcing itself. Since it is in phase with itself on every bounce, it "builds up" energy, and becomes what is know as a "standing wave", which is sort of the same as a room mode. The interesting thing about standing waves is that when you cut off the sound that caused them, they still carry on bouncing around, making the room "ring" at that specific frequency. Since mode, or standing wave, sort of "stores energy", it takes a while for that energy to dissipate and decay, so the note carries on playing in the room for a long time after you stopped playing it. That "long time" could be several hundred milliseconds, or even more than a second in extreme cases. But that only happens at the exact frequency where a note happens to match one of the dimensions of the room. For all other notes, nothing unusual happens. You stop playing, the note quits instantly. So obviously modes (standing waves) are a problem. If the room carries on playing a note that your gear already stopped playing, then you are not hearing the truth! The room is "coloring" the sound, by reinforcing some notes and not others. Even worse, depending on where you are sitting in the room, you might be in a "peak" or a "null" of that standing wave, so either you hear it louder than it should be, or quieter than it should be! So your room can be distorting the truth in both time and frequency, and doing so selectively for different notes, and different locations inside the room. Clearly, this is not good. And there is NOTHING you can do to eliminate the modes, since they are directly related to the dimensions of the room. However, what you CAN do, is to choose the dimensions of your room such that the modes are spread across the audio spectrum evenly, not all bunched up in one place. After all, if there is one mode in your room for each note on the keyboard, then all the notes would sound exactly the same, since they would all be affected in the same way, so the room would no longer be changing some notes while not touching others. So if you choose the dimensions carefully, you can find a set of dimensions where the modes are spread out as evenly as possible. This is known as finding a good "room ratio", and the ratio is generally expressed as H : W : L (height to width to length), and in general the height is assumed to be one "unit", so the ratio appears as 1 : 1.4 : 1.9, for example. In that case, the room is 1.9 times longer than it is heigh, and 1.4 times wider than it is high. There are a large number of known good ratios that scientists have already figure out, so all you need to do is to plug in your numbers using one of the good room mode calculators on the forum, then fiddle with those numbers to see if you can improve things. You don't need to go nuts about this process, but you do need to ensure that your room ratio is NOT one of the bad ones, and is close to one of the good ones. I have not taken the time to check your ratio, but to my eye that room looks rather long for the height. I would check, carefully.

There is no acoustic reason to tilt your glass. That's another myth! By tilting it, you no longer have the maximum possible space between the two panes, so that REDUCES your isolation! If you tilt the glass on one side of a wall, then at some points it is closer to the glass on the other side, and at other points it is further away. Isolation is proportional to the distance between the two layers, so by decreasing the distance along one edge, you have decreased the isolation. That then becomes the weakest link in your room. You get maximum isolation when the two panes of glass are parallel. You don't even need to tilt the glass to "avoid reflections" from singers into mics: another myth. You often see diagrams on the internet showing how the sound from the singer's voice will bounce off the glass and back into the mic if the glass is not tilted, and another diagram showing how it no longer does that when the glass is tilted, as it bounces in a different direction. So you think you just saw "proof" that you must angle your glass? Nope. That would be fine, if singers only sang in straight lines, spitting out the sound like bullets that only go directly forwards... unfortunately, that is not the way sound works at all: Rather, it comes out of the singers mouth as an expanding sphere, traveling in all directions. So even if the glass is angled, you changed nothing. The sound just bounces off a different point on the glass now, and still gets back to the mic... However, there may be another reason for tilting glass, and this one is valid, but has nothing to do with acoustics: light glare. There might be lights that are positioned so that their reflected glare gets in your eyes, and you can't see through the glass. That would be a valid reason for wanting to tilt your glass. But then again, it would also be a valid reason for moving the light.... :) Truth is, if you plan your room carefully, taking ALL aspects into account, then you can plan your lighting so that it wont cause glare, thus allowing you to have your glass vertical, and maximize isolation.

Questions: How do i go about making a "room within a room" but using the existing ceiling?
Well, here we get to the part that you won't like. Remember I said that an isolation wall is a tuned system? Well, in order to isolate well across the entire spectrum, it can ONLY have two leaves and one air gap in it. If you add more leaves and air gaps, it can actually make the isolation WORSE, not better. This is not intuitive at first, but very true. A "leaf" is just a large flat massive surface, and it can be made up of one or more layers of drywall, plywood; OSB, MDF, fiber cement, or other things. But the sum total of all the layers that are on top of each other, constitutes one leaf. So you need a leaf, then some air, then another leaf, and nothing else. That's what makes isolation work. You'll frequently see this referred to as a MAM wall, or an MSM wall. These mean "Mass-Air-Mass" or "Mass-Spring-Mass". MSM is more accurate, since that's the principle of physics on which it is based. Air is the spring here. Yes, you don't normally think of air as being "springy", but it sure is for sound waves! The problem here is that your ceiling is probably ALREADY an MSM system. In other words, there is a drywall ceiling up there, then joists above that, then a sub-floor on top of the joists. Drywall is mass. sub-floor is mass. The air gap between the joists is "spring": So you already have a tuned MSM system up there, except that it is tuned all wrong, coupled, and not very good at isolating. So here's the issue: if you put another leaf next to that, you now have a three-leaf system, MAMAM (or MSMSM). Remember I said before that a three-leaf system will give you worse isolation than a two-leaf, all other factors being equal? Well, that's the problem you have. If you leave the existing ceiling in place, then you will have a three-leaf isolation system, and it won't isolate very well for low frequencies. So the solution is simple: take off the drywall up there. Yeah, I know you wouldn't like that.... :) But that is what you need to do. Take it off, and in fact of you do ti carefully, you can re-use it to "beef up" the sub-floor above. You can take that "mass" and add it to the existing "mass" of your sub-floor, which will greatly improve the isolation. So you cut the old drywall into strips that fit in between the joists up there, press it tight against the sub-floor, hold it in place with cleats, then seal around the edges with acoustic caulk. Doing that will make a BIG difference. So, that takes care of the outer-leaf of your ceiling, now for the inner-leaf: You simply support it on top of the wall frames! In other words, you add a new set of joists on top of your wall framing, and attach your new inner-leaf drywall to that. There are several ways of doing this, and you might not need to lose any height at all, if you do it carefully. One more "You won't like this but have to do it": the same thing applies to your walls. If your existing walls are simple studs with drywall on both sides, the the drywall on your side has to come off. And here to you can re-use it to beef up the other side.
Is it necessary to offset or stagger my studs when double walling?
No, since you are building your inner-leaf framing as a separate frame, decoupled from the existing walls. You only need to use staggered studs if you are building both leaves at once and cannot do separate framing, but you are not in that situation. Your wall will be fully decoupled as it is, so no further decoupling is needed.
Where should i move my furnace to,
Up, about one inch or so: Just enough to get isolation springs under it... :) Seriously, that's what I would do. Just mount it on an isolated base, so the noise it makes is not transmitted into the floor, then also decouple the ducts form the furnace with resilient sleeves, so that the vibrations of the furnace are not transmitted into the ducts themselves. Done like that, the furnace will still make as much noise as it does right now, bit with a major difference: it will all be airborne noise which is easy to stop, and no structure-borne noise, which is impossible to stop... Then analyze the noise spectrum that it is producing, and measure the level. That's what you'll need to figure into your MSM calculations, to make it inaudible inside your studio.
and should i split the HVAC routing a lot, or try to build a dampener?
Maybe either, maybe neither, maybe both: HVAC is a big part of studio design, so figuring how to tie in to your HVAC system is going to take a while. But the basic concept is that at every point where an HVAC duct goes through a leaf, you need a silencer box. That keeps the noise out while letting the air flow. Easy to build yourself.
