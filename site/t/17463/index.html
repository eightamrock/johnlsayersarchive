Soundman202014 March 2012#4
The problem with any air gap on the other side of any large and reasonably rigid, reasonably massive surface is that it creates a resonant system that will resonate in sympathy with certain notes, thus sucking energy out of the room for those. That's the basis of how a panel trap works (membrane trap). So if you place your plywood over a wooden frame, basically you are creating a drum head for your floor: It will "ring" at it's own natural resonant frequency, and all related harmonics. It's not a good idea to build a room on top of a drum head! :)
do you think it could lead to a moisture problem under the new plywood?
There would only be a moisture problem if you already have a moisture problem!
The problem with laying plywood directly on the carpet is that it will still give a bit, so adjacent pieces won't be level.
Why? What is under the carpet that might give? If it's a concrete floor, then there's no reason why it would give. If it is a wooden floor of some type, supported on joists that give, then it will give anyway, regardless of how you put the plywood deck on top. If you have a concrete subfloor, and you build the deck itself correctly, then there won't be any problem with it giving. We are talking about two layers of 3/4" plywood here, laid crosswise with respect to each other, and screwed together, so there would have to be one very large load to make that lot give! But the big question is: Why not just take out the carpet? If it was installed properly, then it should be possible to take it out without causing too much damage to it. - Stuart -

To be fair, what your remedy is has nothing to do with the topic you started. It is a pretty easy control device to place a hard barrier (plywood or MDF or OSB) underneath the microphone to remove the mid range absorption of the carpet. It is something different altogether to ask "Does anyone have experience putting a hard floor over carpet?" The short answer is this. When you take carpet and encapsulate it with upper and lower hard boundaries, you trap this material, now if it has been there for some time, the ability to trap contaminants is high. The acoustic side is that you reduce the TL of this floor because you introduce a small air space, and the flanking you introduce is not going to help you.

Soundman202029 March 2012#19
My understanding is that the floor should serve as the one large reflective area because you have to have one like that. If you make the ceiling or any wall the reflective surface, then the floor has to be absorptive, but you can't walk on that, right?
There's actually quite a few reasons why it's better to have a reflective floor and absorptive ceiling, including the ones you mentioned, but for me, the most important reason is the psycho-acoustic one: All your life, wherever you go, your head is always the same distance form the floor, so your brain gets used to the sound of reflections coming off the floor. Distances to walls and ceiling change, by the day, hour, minute and second, as you move around, but the distance to the floor is pretty much constant, and so is the "sound" of the floor. Your brain uses it as a sort of reference. If you make the floor very absorptive and the ceiling or one of the wall reflective in your studio, then your brain has to deal with a new and different reference: The distance and timing are no longer what your brain expects, and it just doesn't sound quite right in there. Very subtly perhaps, but the effect is there. Your aural reference is flipped, and the room doesn't "feel" right. For critical listening, that's not a good situation. If you try to track in there, then of course the mic is picking up the same slightly "wrong" sound of the room, and it will be imprinted into the recordings, just as surely as room reverb gets imprinted. And once its in, it's realy hard to get it out! Like too much salt in your soup, there's not much you can do about having too much room sound in your recordings. You could probably position the mic carefully and overcome this to a certain extent, but why make your life harder than it needs to be? Micing is already hard enough without adding another twist! This is also one of the reasons why rooms with very low ceilings sound strange: your ears and brain are getting conflicting signals, and the floor seems closer than it should be, so your brain is mildly derailed by the strange experience. And of course, if you try to mic drums in a room with a low ceiling, it sounds like crap, as you get all kinds of weird artifacts from having the mics so close to the ceiling and so far from the floor. Like I said, all of this is subtle, not really noticed consciously, but it it there, nevertheless, and is a little bit disconcerting, Trying to make mix decisions in a room that sounds inverted, or lopsided, is not going to bring out the best in you. You might tend to compensate, subconsciously, for a problem that isn't really there, or to not compensate for one that is. That's one of the reasons why the general recommendation is to have a hard, reflective floor: to reinforce the effect of the "floor constant", and make it easy on your brain. If you were to also have a hard, reflective ceiling, then you run the risk of having a modal issue in the vertical axis, or flutter echo problems, so the associated general recommendation is to have a "soft" ceiling opposite your "hard" floor. And the soft ceiling also helps the psycho-acoustic thing too: Putting thick, deep absorption on it, makes it "disappear" somewhat, acoustically, so the room can sound higher than it really is, which once again helps to reinforce the "hard floor" sound.
But since my recording needs are for limited amounts of time, I wondered if I could leave the ceiling reflective and put my bass trap panels on the carpeted floor with just enough space between a few to be able to walk where I need.
As far as pure sound waves and acoustics are concerned, yes, you could do that. The acoustics would be the same as if you did it the other way around, with the absorption on the ceiling.. Sound waves don't care which way is "up" or "down"! So from the pure acoustically approach, that would work. HOWEVER, even though the sound waves don't care about up and down, our brain does, and that's where you'll most likely run into the psycho-acoustic sensations mentioned above. So your idea ain't dumb at all, if the only issue were acoustics. Unfortunately, our brains care too much about "up" and "down"...
Add traps according to measurement results, then measure again.
When you do those measurements, you MUST get the mic back to the exact same position in the room every single time. So figure out a way to mark or measure that position very accurately. If you put the mic in a different location, then you cannot compare that reading with others: they will be invalid as they show the response for two different positions, and two different patterns. So make sure that the mic is always in the same place, give or take a half inch or so in any direction. Also, the changes between readings as you add things and move the around probably won't be as big as you might think on the graphs. Don't expect to see huge differences, as the changes are more likely to be small than large. And make your first measurement in the empty room, with nothing in it at all, so you have a valid "baseline" against which you can compare all the changes.
At this point, I would expect bass more balanced through the room, but high frequencies to be sucked out by all the absorption, right?
Yes on the "sucked out highs", but not necessarily on the lows. There will be a difference, for sure, but if you have a major modal issue then a couple of 4" traps is not going to make a huge difference. Some difference, yes, but not earth-shattering. And depending on which mode(s) is (are) causing issues, you might not see any change at all! For example, if you have a modal problem on the side-to-side axis of the room, but you put the absorption on the back wall and the ceiling (which is a good plan!), then you won't see much change at all, since the absorption is not very much in the path of the "problem". If you were to then move some of it to one of the side walls, then you'd see a difference. In other words, it helps if the absorption is in the path that the wave is traveling! And if you want to put the highs back in the room, then just cover your 703 panels with plastic sheeting, or something thin like that. That will reflect the highs back, but still allow the lows through. Please post your graphs here, as I think it would be very instructive to see how things change in your room as you play around with 703 panels and plywood! It would be neat to see some real-world data on how these things actually affect a room. - Stuart -
