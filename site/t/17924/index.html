xSpace31 October 2012#3
" 16x16 (just over 16 length, and just under 16 width)." Any room that is square has two things in common. It has two pair of parallel walls that are matched at the same frequency due to the same wall length. That equals double your opportunity for flutter echo which is the result of sound bouncing off of hard parallel surfaces. A rectangular room has always been a better room. "would rather carpet the ceiling)" I would lose this idea as well. To carpet the ceiling only transfers the unknown and selective absorption known to be produced by carpet, from the floor to the ceiling. dismiss the idea completely, the idea of carpet on the...well, anywhere. "Questions Im stuck on; should i make two walls angle instead of 90 degree corners, " Depends. A complementing 6 degree angle of walls in the control room (both walls on either side of the listeners ears being canted at 6 degrees) can add diffusion and reduce flutter where as the tracking room wants to be non-parallel which is different. "is it ok to use metal frame for walls, and or ceiling instead of wood" Structural studs not a typical light gauge metal. Otherwise the walls and/or ceiling will fail/collapse due to gravity. "whats the best flooring to use on cement or leave cement with fresh paint" Personal preference, one or the other. Either one is a hard surface.

Soundman20202 November 2012#6
new question as searching around that 38$% rule means a 16foot room would be best at 6 feet wide? hmmm best??
You seem to have misunderstood the so-called "38% rule". It has nothing at all to do with room width. All it tells you is the supposed best location for the mix position within the room. So if your room is 16 feet long, then the mix position should be at 6'1" from the front wall and 9'11" from the rear wall. Theoretically, that is the point in the room where the modes are least objectionable. But it is not related in any way to room width: the mix position must always be on the center-line of the room. In any case, 38% is not really a rule at all: just a guideline. A good place to start wen searching for the best mix position.
thanks for the quick help, I've not realized that a closely square room would cause issues
You should probably do some searching on the forum for "room ratio", and learn about why a square room is bad. Using a good ratio for your room is a basic key to success. With a bad ratio, your room will never sound good, no matter what you do to it. With a good ratio you have a much better chance of getting it to sound good.
I think drywall is better, some people are telling me drop ceiling tiles would be a good idea.
Yes, drywall would be better. In fact, it is your only real option if you hope to have a good studio with decent isolation. Stay away from those people who are telling you that ceiling tiles would be better: they obviously have no understanding at all of the principles of acoustics.
but end up with more questions than I already had because its the internet and there's more than one way to do things.
Yes, there may well be "more than one way" of doing things, but the problem is that most of those ways are wrong, and very few are right. That's the problem with the internet: any idiot can say anything they feel like, even if they don't have a clue and have never studied anything at all about acoustics. Such as the people who were trying to tell you that carpet is a great idea on the ceiling, or that drop-ceilings are a great way of isolating a room.
so, 10x16' room is better than a 16x16' room?
Yes it is better, but it still might not be good. For example, if the ceiling ended up at either 8 feet high or 10 feet high, then it would be bad.
GENIE CLIPS are worth it?
Depends on what your overall plans are. If yo are going to build proper decoupled walls then you don't need clips.
new question, *bed for those long hours in the studio producing is doable? / could double as a bass trap? lol
Probably a sofa that converts in to a be would be a better idea for a studio. And no, most likely a bed would not double as a bass trap: mattress foam is usually closed-cell, so it has very little in useful acoustic properties. - Stuart -
