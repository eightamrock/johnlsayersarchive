Well, the amount of work to get the slant to work out was astronomical!!! The main reason I slanted the glass was to minimize the light reflections (the "mirror effect") of the parallel glass, and to be honest, I am not sure that it made that much of a difference! I can still see myself pretty well in the glass, and a perfect image of the wall behind me as well! So all of that work, and the benefit is not as much as I thought it would be. Plus, as you know, parallel panes will give a bit better isolation since there is a larger average gap between the panes. My "ugly window" at the back of the CR was sooooo easy to build and install since it was parallel to the walls. Piece of cake compared to the main CR windows. Plus, if I *ever* decide to move out of this place, it is going to be harder to convince any new potential clients that slanted glass is a cool thing! It is certainly cool and geeky to us studio nerds, but what if the new owners want this room to be a spare bedroom or a home theater? The slanted glass is probably not something that be appealing to anyone. BTW... I love your signature! Great stuff!!!

I know it has been a while since I have updated this thread, but the last couple of weeks have been pretty crazy... the holidays of course slow things down quite a bit, but I have also had a close relative in the hospital which took up all of last weekend (he is fine now), and then we had some family commitments this weekend, etc., etc., etc. Anyway, I have made *some* progress believe it or not, just not as much as I would have liked (does this sound familiar?). Basically, I have been working on finishing my door frames, and have begun laying the flooring. Here are a few pictures to show the progress so far. The doors I used are of the "pre-hung" variety, which means that I was able to buy a door frame as part of the door assembly. This makes it so much easier for amateurs like me to hang them, but there are a couple of drawbacks. 1) Price: They are obviously more expensive 2) "Standard" door frame width: This is the biggie. The door frames that come standard with these guys are made to fit into standard wall construction, which means single-wall construction (2x4 studs) with a single-layer of 1/2" drywall on each side. This also means that the included frames are too "thin" to fit the wall construction for my project, and they had to be "extended" so that the frames were flush with the sides of the wall on both sides of the door (inside the room and outside the room). I got some S4 grade wood, and extended each door frame. This first shot, shows a picture of the CR-to-airlock door frame. I am standing in the doorway taking a pic of the extended frame. You can see the white primered wood of the pre-hung door, and the plain wood extensions that I made to bring the edge of the frame flush with the drywall. I then puttied and sanded the heck of the frames before painting them all with primer. You can also see the old vinyl seals that came with the doors. I will be adding rubber car trunk (car boot) seals to the doors later.
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.helixrecording.com/Pics/Construction/Dsc01538_SM.jpg
Here we are looking at the edge of the frame, and you can see the gap between the frame edge and the drywall. The door frame was already caulked with acoustic caulk, but I decided to fill in the space between the frame and the drywall with expandable foam. Beware, there are 2 types of this foam, and if you get the wrong type to use in doors or windows, it will expand and warp your door or window frames. There is the "regular" expanding foam, and then there is the type that is specially-designed for use with windows and doors.

With the door frames at a standstill while I wait for the seals to show up, I went ahead and got started on the flooring. I decided to go with engineered hardwood flooring throughout the studio, which is of a glue-down style. It is quite messy, and a lot of work, but I think it looks quite classy. Here I am spreading out the first glob of glue.
External image, not preserved — original: http://www.helixrecording.com/Pics/Construction/Dsc01557_SM.jpg

Here are the first few pieces in place (sort of!). As you can see, I started a few feet out from the wall, and worked towards it. What I did was figure out a 12 board space (at 3" per board), and then gave myself a 3/8" expansion gap at the wall edge, and snapped a chalk like that was 36 3/8" from the wall. I then lined up the first pieces of flooring along that line, and worked my way towards the wall. If everything worked out right with my calculations, I should be able to fit 12 boards between the chalk line and the wall, and still have a 3/8" expansion joint left over. It worked out fine for the most part, but it is difficult dealing with walls that are not 100% square. You can see that I cut some scrap pieces of flooring to use as anchoring cleats for the first row. These were nailed into the subflooring to provide a firm anchor point for the first row to sit against.
