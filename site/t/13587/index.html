xaMdaM8 September 2009#3
jbassino wrote:
As long as it looks straight you should be okay. Anyway, you need to caulk between the stop and frame to fill any possible air gap.
Depending on the thickness/weight/mass of your glass, butyl glazing tape is fine as well... You need to be cautious of what type of caulk you use to seal inside the air cavity. You don't want to use anything that will out-gas and fog the glass on the inside. I used SilenSeal, but I think a butyl caulk such at OSI's SC-175 would be fine (if not better). Then seal the inner stop's glass facing surface with glazier tape. Put glazier tape on the outside stops, and then put your glass in followed by your outer stops/keeper plates. On the tracking room side, I ended up with just under a 1/8" gap on the top stop. Just yesterday, milled a 1/8" spacer on the planer and tacked that in with brads, and then applied the finish trim moulding. When I'm done with staining/finishes, it will be fine, albeit potentially visible... but that one little shim isn't going to distract so much that it's a problem.

xaMdaM8 September 2009#5
Not to worry... not everyone has a perfect framing job.... (Especially me!) Try taking a 12.5 degree angle across a 30 foot span, and then rotate it's vertical plane continuously by varying lengths that no one can actually calculate... You end up very appreciative of square walls... :) What you're really faced with right now is planing and sanding that corner, or shimming everything to that "high" corner... tis' up to you. If you plane and sand the corner, you'll have a bit extra to do to get your finish trim to fit flush on the wall. If you shim out the other 3 corners, you have to deal with where the shim needs to taper to the corner and making the shim the same width of your finish trim. That way it will sit square and you wont have odd little angles on your corners. Either way is a pain in the butt. I chose to sand and plane the high corner because I'm making all of my own trim moulding. Since my trim is simple square stock, it was easier for me to mill the back thickness than it was to make shim stock and futz around with mitered corners.

xaMdaM9 September 2009#13
Carsten, The "way" to do this is to take your trim moulding and set it to the side of your "high" corner. Then mark the outline of the high corner on your trim. Then, using a planer/sander/rabbit bit, etc.... you remove thickness of the trim from underneath the trim until it fits flush and square to the wall. The key is to only remove the width of your frame material... like a a rabbit or dado joint... a notch if you will. Hope that makes sense.
