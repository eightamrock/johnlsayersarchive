Soundman20207 April 2009#2
Hey Myriad! Welcome! Sounds like an interesting project! Starting from scratch, no existing walls, floor or ceilings to tie you down: You can do it pretty much any way you want! Cool! With your layout as it is now, I see a couple of issues that can probably be done more efficiently, and I've got some comments that might help you re-arrange things. Comments in no particular order of importance: Just the order I happened to think of them! Right now, you have no sight lines at all from your mix position into your live room and vocal booth. You usually want to be able to see in there, but your mix position is facing the wrong way for that. You might want to think about playing around with rooms, positions, angles and things. You might already know these, but there are a couple of rules of thumb to help you get your CR in shape (it looks like you already used some of them!): Your mix position should be roughly 38% of the distance from the front wall to the back wall. It's just a starting point, and not written in stone, but 38% is a good location regarding room modes. 50% is the worst possible location. With your head at the 38% position, the imaginary lines that are the acoustic axes of your speakers should intersect about 20 cm behind your head, at an angle of 60 degrees. Your speakers should be about 1.2 m above the floor, which is roughly your ear height when seated, and should be about 1 to 2 m apart, more or less. If you figure this out geometrically, then it will place your head and the speakers at the corners of an equilateral triangle. (you seem to have that already). Once again, those numbers and angles aren't carved in stone: They are just rough guide lines to get you positioned more or less correctly. Your control room (CR) should be perfectly symmetrical (left-right): Your LR can be built any shape you want (within reason!), and can be designed to "color" the sound a bit to your taste, if you prefer, or it can be designed to be neutral acoustically, but not dead sounding. The CR should be as neutral sounding as possible. It will need lots of bass trapping. Large bass traps. They work best in the corners of the room. Don't forget that a rectangular room has 12 corners! You might want to re-think your booth and WC: As it is, everyone has to go through the LR to get to the WC! Not only that, but you only have a single door from the WC into the live room, implying that the isolation won't be very good, so noises from the WC could potentially get into the LR... How about if you make some kind of access airlock (kind of like a miniature lobby) that has access into all three rooms, and put the WC on the OTHER side of that, well away from the rooms? You will need two back-to-back doors between the rooms, for good isolation, or one of Rod's massive "Superdoors" will also do the trick. Your CR dimensions should be calculated to match one of the "good" room ratios. If you want, you can splay the side walls and ceiling of your CR, to help create a good Reflection Free Zone (RFZ) around the mix position. If so, walls should be splayed about 6 degrees or more, and the ceiling should be splayed 10 to 15 degrees, with 12 being optimal. So you would be creating a kind of "funnel", with the speakers at the small end, widening out towards the rear wall. You don't have to splay the walls if you don't want: Many people do, for better acoustics, but many don't. You can still build a good room without splayed walls, and of course it makes the construction a bit harder. Pros and cons. Personal choice. Etc. Finally, you might want to consider flush mounting your speakers ("soffit mounting") to give you tighter bass and better imaging. Those pointers should maybe give you some ideas to get your design going! Also, I suggest that you download SketchUp and spend some time learning to use it. It's a 3D modeling package from Google, and it's FREE! It's probably the only free thing you'll get in your entire studio build, so make the most of it! It takes a while to learn, since it isn't very intuitive, but once you figure out the basics, its a very powerful tool. Most people here use it to design their studios, which makes it easy to exchange models with others, and for others to take a close look at your desigg from all angles, and suggest improvements. Anyway, once again: Welcome! - Stuart -

Not a requirement, no. Some people splay there walls, some don't. It helps with first reflection points and also with flutter echo. It looks cool, too! But it's harder to build.
Can I just adequately treat the room with bass traps, etc?
Yup. But you'll need lots of bass trapping either way. Splaying your walls won't reduce the need for treatment. The real advantages of splaying are helping you to get a better, cleaner image direct from the speakers, without reflections off the walls getting in the way, and without flutter echo messing things up.
Does the room need a QRD on the back wall?
Probably not. The room isn't big enough for a diffusor to be of much help. According to one school of thought, you need about six meters or so between the back of your head and the diffusor in order for it to be effective. Others say that even in a small room they can help. Personally, I don't see the need in a small room. You'll probably need to use most of the rear wall for absorption in any case, so a QRD would just take up some of that space. But that's my opinion. Others may disagree!
How does a rectangular room have 12 corners? I get 8.

Soundman202014 April 2009#11
When building the main building, should I first do double walls (2 leaf) all the way around the perimeter of the inside of the building before I begin to construct the other walls that will create the CR, etc?
No. Your outer wall is ONE leaf. Make it as massive as you can afford. Brick would be good. Concrete block would be good. Or stud frame with a couple of layers of OSB, plywood, etc. on the outside, followed by siding of some kind for your exterior surface. But it must be built as only one leaf. Each room is also built as only one leaf. That plus the outer core of the building gives you two leaves. You want ONLY two leaves, not three, not four. Just count the layers between you sitting at the mix position, and the outside world, or between you and any other room. If you get more or less than a total of two leaves, then something is wrong. Ditto for someone standing in the live room: two leaves to the outside world, or two leaves to any other room. Ditto for someone standing in the vocal booth: two leaves to outside, or to another room.
How sturdy will the inside rooms be if they are decoupled from the main structure?
You might need to brace them. There are several methods for doing that. Diagonal bracing is one. Sway braces is another. If you are going to use plywood or MDF as part of your leaf construction, then you don't need those. Even the drywall alone might be enough, although personally I'm not to keen on using drywall as a structural element!
Do I use 5/8" drywall on EVERYTHING that needs drywall?
Unless you can get something even thicker, then yes! :) Seriously, the more mass you can put in, the better your isolation will be. Thicker drywall should have a lower resonant frequency than thin.
For the "inside-out" celing in the CR, would that extend into the live room, as well?
Well. you wouldn't "extend" the same physical ceiling to the live room! But you could "extend" the same principle. Each room has it's own separate ceiling. Either or both could be built inside-out. Your choice!
If I were to put rigid fiberglass up in the "inside-out" ceiling, how would I get it to stay so I can then trim and cover it? With what material?
Package strapping, wire mesh, twine, etc. stapled across the joists.
On the top side of the "inside-out" ceiling, do I double up on the drywall with Green Glue in between?
If you have the budget, and want maximum isolation, then yes! But at the least, go with two layers of drywall.
On the walls that connect the live room to the hall way and restroom, do I still need to decouple those walls as well?
Decouple in what sense? As in build a two-leaf wall? If that's what you mean, then yes: Any wall that has the LR, CR or booth on the other side needs to be part of a two-leaf system. But walls between, for example, your kitchen and bathroom, or hall and bathroom, can just be normal stud walls, if you want. - Stuart -

stuntbutt15 April 2009#17
You don't want three leafs in your iso booths, or anywhere else. It looks like your drawing shows triple leafs in a few spots. Working on a layout of decoupled rooms is counterintuitive because we are used to thinking of walls as simple partitions. Isolated rooms are independent freestanding structures that happen to be close to each other. Here's an overly simplistic exercise: Draw a rectangle as your outer leaf. Now draw two smaller rectangles inside that rectangle. Now you have a control room and live room. An iso booth can be it's own rectangle or a partitioned area of an existing rectangle depending on how much isolation you need. Arrange the rectangles so that the area left forms hallways, lounge, etc. Obviously rooms don't have to be rectangles, just an exercise.

Soundman202015 April 2009#22
He asked if, instead of fiberglass insulation in the walls, could they blow in a cellulose insulation
Is that closed-cell or open-cell? If it is closed cell, then forget it. It has no acoustical benefits at all, and even worse, if it is rigid it will probably end up coupling your walls again! You can ONLY use open-cell insulation in your walls, NEVER closed cell. It needs to have the correct gas flow resistance properties, similar to that of mineral wool or fiberglass, and similar density (roughly 48 kg/m3). I would imagine that the stuff your builder is proposing is closed cell insulation. It sounds like he is looking for ways to cut corners. If he tells you it is open-cell, then ask for the manufacturer's spec sheet on the stuff.
As oppose to fiberglass, which would leave some gaps.
You WANT those gaps! The myriad of tiny air spaces between the fibers of rock wool or fiberglass insulation is exactly what you need! Those air-gaps-and-fibers create the exact acoustic properties that you WANT in your wall! That is how it damps your wall: sound waves penetrate through those air gaps, cause the fibers to vibrate minutely, and they in turn absorb the vibration by turning it into low-grade heat. That's how damping works. Yes, you do want an airtight seal on the SURFACES of your inner and outer leaf, but the cavity in between must be filled with OPEN CELL insulation, full of millions of tiny fibers and tiny air gaps. That is why closed-cell foam is useless. It sounds like your builder does not understand acoustics at all.
he also asked about blowing in a thin layer of foam inside the walls before the insulation goes in.
Why? What does he want to accomplish with that? Is that intended to seal the cavity air-tight? That is not a substitute for carefully sealing and caulking each layer of drywall as it goes up. Once again, it sounds like he is looking for shortcuts. Once again, I'd be concerned about that stuff maybe being too rigid, and possibly forming bridges between your leaves way inside the wall, where you can't see it, or creating flanking paths in other ways. - Stuart -
