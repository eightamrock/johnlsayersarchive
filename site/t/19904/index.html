Soundman202029 March 2015#2
Hi there "wofftech", and Welcome! :)
I quickly realised that the condition of the concrete foundation was rubbish so I ripped out the concrete slab and put a new one in.
Great! so you have your studio floor issue resolved already: concrete is about the best possible floor you could hope for, and is also great for isolation. Image-3 shows what seems to be a stud frame built just inside the skin of the building, and following images show that stuffed with insulation, the drywalled. So you already have a two-leaf isolation system in place... Building anything within that, would make it a three-leaf isolation system, which implies poor isolation in the low frequencies... unless you can spare a lot of extra space for the third leaf, and afford the extra mass that you will need to compensate for the 3rd leaf effect. It's a pity you didn't find the forum before you did all that!
drywall (13mm),
:shock: That's not going to be good for decent isolation. You don't say how much isolation you need (in decibels), but since that 13mm drywall is your middle leaf, and optimal isolation in a 3-leaf system is achieved only with half of the total mass on the middle leaf, that doesn't bode well for high isolation...
He suggested having a slightly smaller room to improve the room acoustics.
That's unusual advice! In general, larger rooms have better acoustics, and smaller rooms have worse acoustics. Unless there was an overriding reason for making the room smaller (for example: if it would have been a cube or square otherwise, or have a really bad ratio), then the general recommendation is to make it as big as possible. Do you know WHY your friend suggested making it smaller?
5.65x3.6x as high as I can make it.
for those dimensions, 2.5 to 2.6m is about optimal. Definitely no more than 2.65, nor less than about 2.45. At 2.6 H, you could take the length up to about 6.1 if you felt like it. And with those two held constant (2.6 and 6.1) you could take the width up to about 4.20. There are better and worse combinations in that range, but overall there's some good ones, and plenty of options to play with. Assuming this is for a control room, that is? Or a one-room studio that is basically a control room with built-in tracking/rehearsal area?
Drums,
Acoustic or electronic? Assuming acoustic, that implies a lot of isolation.... but we'd need to knwo how much, to help you design the isolation system.
3) I have young children so isolation is as much for my own family as it is for neighbors.
How far away is your family? How far away are your neighbors? What are the legal requirements for noise in your area? How many decibels of isolation do you need?
we came up with the idea of modules - framed. We came up with the idea of skinning the whole room with two layers of 19mm tongue and grove. ... The idea uses 3.5x90mm structural timber.
30 kg/m2 dead load, spanning 3.6m, on 2x4 lumber? Hmmmm... did you check that with a structural engineer? What deflection did you want for that, and what spacing are you using for the joists? To be very honest, that doesn't sound safe! I doubt you could do that with anything less than 2x8 joists, or maybe 2x6s if you could get good lumber (oak, hemlock, spruce, etc.) and were prepared to live with large deflection. But on ordinary pine or fir, I don't see that passing inspection on 2x4s. Where I live, it would have to be at least 2x8s 40cm OC, and L/480, minimum. You really should double-check that with your engineer. Also, that low mass ceiling with the light-weight middle leaf and thin flexible outer leaf, does not sound like a good combination for low frequency isolation. Are you sure that's what you need? You did mention "drums", "neighbors" and "family"...
Essentially, each module would be lifted up, bolted together and sealed.
The idea is OK, but the joist sizes, mass, 3-leaf system, and isolation are not very convincing. I don't see you getting much more than 40 dB of isolation overall, and substantially less in the low end.
Each module would then be filled with Tontine acoustisorb 3.
Yes, but that's underneath, which is inside the room, so that is treatment, not isolation. You also need damping (insulation) on top of that, to deal with the multiple resonance that will be going on inside the MSM cavity...
With two layers of MDF, this will be heavy. I suspect I will need to design the walls in sections.
Or use wall jacks... Not sure if you have the space for that, but if you work carefully you should be able to do it.
1) I notice that not too many people use two layers of mdf. Are there problems with using two layers of mdf rather than some other combination?
The issue is mainly cost: In most places around the world, drywall (plasterboard) is a lot cheaper than MDF, and the mass (surface density) is similar, so using drywall tends to be cheaper. MDF isn't great structurally either. So another option might be to put a layer of OSB on first as your base layer (against the studs) then a layer of 16mm fire-rated drywall for the second layer.
2) Any suggestions / concerns with the ceiling design?
Structural. See above comments...
3) Are there problems with using a combination of inside out and normal wall construction?
Nope! None at all. As long as you are certain that about your mass continuity all around the room, and that you have figured out how to get good seals where the two meet, then there's no problem with doing it that way. Interesting project! I'm looking forward to following along. - Stuart -

Soundman202013 May 2015#5
The idea would revolve around putting up a panel, then a beam, screwing the beam to the panel, seal, move on.
That should work, yes. Just make sure that the beam is dimensions correctly for the span! Do get a certified structural engineer to sign of on that.
The big concern I have is sealing between the panel frame and the 15cm beam. Because there is on overlap with the boards anymore, I would think this would be quite weak re sealing. Any advice would be appreciated.
Acoustic caulk, applied liberally. Lots of it! Apply in every possible place you can think, of during the building of the modules, also as each one is installed, and once again after they are all in place.
I need to get fresh air in and out... After reading the posts... I'm guessing a couple of fans, and silencer?
Yep. You only really need one fan per room: since the room is basically an air-tight container with a inlet duct and and exhaust duct, if you on the exhaust duct that that automatically also sucks fresh air in through the intake duct. - Stuart -
