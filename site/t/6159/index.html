golok16 May 2006#16
Old Dog, There would be a collaboration between the Studio designer and the Architect OR Certified Interior Designer (CID). Yes, the architects job would be to comply with all codes. For example, wall connection to the underside of the ceiling/roof. Spacing of studs, sizes of studs depending on the wall height, ADA (such as mounting heights, path of travel from parking to studio and egress). Since you mentioned this is going to be in a church, which is consider and assembly occupancy and now you want to introduce and different occupany (the studio) you might need an occupancy seperation wall(just need to know how to detail it) . Also, PARKING stalls and BATHROOM Fixture calcs. Since you are building a studio, the building department is going to view that as more people (then what the bldg. was originally designed for) in the entire building. And guess what? more people means more parking stalls and more toilet fixtures is needed. But maybe your church might have enough already. Also, if your studio is going to be in the basement or the second floor, yes, you will need access for the disable. a wheelchair lift or an elevator. Oh, and a big thing is the EXITS. If your studio is greater than 3000s.f. you'll need two exits which need to be apart more than half the greatest diagonal of your studio. One exit would lead directly to the outside and or a fire rated space. Fire sprinklers play an important part as far as the traveling distance to the exit itself. You can also play around on how you describe the studio to the building officials. For example you might tell them its for the church band employees onlyand no new outside people will use the facility. What you dont want to do is increase the occupancy load. But your not done yet, you'll probably need to submit to the fire department. And if on the drawings it mentions absorbtion material (flamable material) you might need to give them specs on the material your using for the acoustics. The fire department would need to know what kind of fumes would be release if you studio goes up in flames. Since this is a TYPE V (Type five) construction, your city might have a type V sheet. A type v sheet gives STANDARD details and span limits. If you live in a house constructed out of 2X4, thats type v. try not to introduce beams or columns, if you do, you'll need a structural engineer. But you might need one though(struct. eng), if you want A/C in the studio. A mechnical engineer might introduce a new a/c unit on the roof and the structural eng. would need to verify in the roof can handle the additional weight. Best of luck old dog!!!! golok

Bryant27 May 2006#21
OD, The budget for design can certainly vary from project to project but in my experience 1/3 to 1/2 total project cost would be a bit high. Contract documents and design documents are different beasties usually. "Plans and Specifications" are CONTRACT documents. Typical ramifications...... Poor plans and specs = poorly built + unforseen costly over runs. In construction its usually not a matter of "if" things aren't built according to plans and specifications, its often a matter of "when" things aren't built to plans and specifications. Good plans and specifications are a reference to the "terms" of the contract; before, during, and after the work is done. If you have a good "Contract" where the "terms" are clearly spelled out you have a much better likelyhood of the work being done correctly. By correctly I mean both in accordance with building codes AND so the space functions in accordance with your design intent. Also consider if it has to be done two or three times, you'll only have to pay for it once if the contract documents support you. (How much do lawyers cost?) Another thing to consider: On larger commercial projects it is typical for owners to contract with the architect through all the stages/phases of the construction process from design to close out. A good architect's involvment during the entire construction process could wind up saving your tail feathers in the long run. Even so, you might have the option of contracting with the architect up to the completion of a particular stage/phase. So, 1/3 to 1/2...... I suppose its relative to the cost of materials, labor and the Peoples Republic of California's take. This is a great thread and some great questions are being asked in it. I for one did learn, pretty much the hard way, that many of the decoupling techniques, most notably floated rooms, are difficult for a DIY person or team to get a blessing on from local building departments here in Quakeifornia. At one point in my project planning stage, I considered a floated concrete slab using Mason Industries' jack up floor system and specially designed seismic snubbers. You can see the details of that (since abandoned) plan halfway down page 13 of my monster thread. It may seem like overkill, but to float a slab in this state, this is the level one probably must achieve in order to have authorization to build. I'm glad I didn't proceed with that plan for a lot of reasons, not the least of which is an extremely high water table in my area -- about 12-14 inches in dry weather, and more like 9-10" after heavy rains. Bottom line, the requirements for fastening to slabs are very strict around here -- and that's just one example. Good luck with your continued planning. You are to be commended for getting all this stuff figured out before you even think about putting hammer to nail. 8) --Keith :mrgreen:
