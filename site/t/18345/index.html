I already said "welcome" in your other thread, but I'll say it again here: Welcome!
20'(L) x 15.5'(W) x 11'(H)
That's a pretty decent size for a control room. Nice!
Our building is located on a very busy street with frequent trucks passing so STC is a consideration,
I think you mean "isolation", not "STC". STC is not a good way of measuring isolation for studios, since it does not consider low frequencies at all. There's two and a half octaves at the bottom end of the scale that STC totally ignores, and those are the two and a half that are the most difficult to isolates. So it's much better to talk in terms of TL (Transmission Loss), or just plain "isolation", to avoid confusion.
sorry, I don't have an spl meter
Better get one soon, then! You'll need it frequently in your build, and especially in the stage of treating the rooms after they are built. Without a sound level meter, you really don't have much to go on in deciding how to design and build your studio.
Most of the exterior walls of the building are cinder-block,
That's reasonably good, but do make sure you seal it well before you start building anything. Even better would be to put a layers of plaster on it.
but the front of the building is almost all glass.
Ouch. How thick? Fixed or operable? Well sealed?
Floor is concrete with a layer of commercial carpet.
The carpet can go: that's useless for acoustics. The concrete is excellent, though.
without floating the floor.
Good. You do NOT need to do that.
I feel like its overkill four our purposes.
Not only overkill, unnecessary and expensive. Floating a floor correctly would eat up pretty much all of your budget, and floating it badly (as per the usual YouTube idiot videos) is not only pointless, but also detrimental to isolation and acoustics.
Instead we will frame up the floor and fill it with sand.
Why? If you already have a great concrete slab, why would you want to put a sand-filled deck on it? For what purpose?
What do you think?
I think that's a bad idea... :)
The side if the room that faces the tracking room has a 64"x24" window in it.
That is going to be expensive! Two panes of thick laminate glass that big, is going to take a huge chunk out of your budget. Do you really need something that big?
My main concern is if I should build the room to be set up with the monitors firing down the long side of the room or the narrow side.
Down the long axis, always. The only time you could reasonably have the firing across the room, is if the room is big enough to allow that: Yours isn't.
I am leaning towards LEDE treatment with an emphasis on the DE
LEDE is a concept from 30 years ago that has long since passed from favor as a good design for control rooms. LEDE rooms turned out to be uncomfortable to work in, fatiguing, and even unpleasant. It has been replaced by the far more useful RFZ and CID concepts, which are somewhat similar, and are often considered to be extensions of LEDE, but without the drawbacks. Also LEDE is not just about treatment: it is about the entire room design concept, of which treatment is just a part.
with an emphasis on the DE
Dead is not good for a control room. That's part of the reason why LEDE was abandoned: the dead sound was unnatural. AES, ITU and EBU specs for control rooms all agree pretty much on the decay characteristics, and they are not close to "dead". I would suggest using one of those as the basis for your design.
the space I have allows for one main tracking room, an ISO booth (both already under construction)
How are those being built? Also "room-in-a-room", I hope! What materials and techniques are you using? It is unusual to build part of a studio before the whole thing is designed, since you never know what you might need to change in the LR and booth until the CR design is complete. Some things you didn't mention that are also very important: Existing ceiling. Existing roof. Plan for doors. Plan for HVAC. Plan for electrical. Those are often overlooked in the early stages of design, so now would be a good time to start planning them. HAV especially: it's not easy to do HVAC for studios. It would also help us to understand better what you are doing if you could post photos of the space you have, and what is in it already. - Stuart -

Soundman202029 May 2013#5
Wes seems pretty firm on the dimensions, I can build the ceiling at 8 ft, but I have 11 ft, should I raise the ceiling height or stick with his math?
Wes is very big on tuning room dimensions to exact relationships on the musical scale: pretty much everything he does in a room is tuned musically, sort of like a series of related chords. He's been very successful following that design philosophy. However, there are many other very successful designers that do not follow this philosophy, since there really isn't much research on how the musical tuning of a room relates to its actual effectiveness as a studio: Is a room tuned to a major fifth any more successful over its life than one that is not tuned to anything in particular? Is a musically tuned room more usable? Do the mixes translate better? Does it make more money? Nobody has studied that, as far as I know, so nobody knows for sure. And there are very large number of successful big-name studios out there whose control rooms don't meet this tuning. So it seems that even if it does help, the effect might not be that big. With no research, it's hard to say if it works or not. On the other hand, there is a large amount of available research on room volumes, floor areas, room ratios, dimensions, shapes, frequency response, time-domain response, usability, and subjective analysis by experts, all of which indicates that higher ceilings and greater room volume are practically always desirable and useful. So if you have the option to increase your ceiling height from 8 feet to 11 feet, I would most definitely say:Go for it! Make the room as big as possible, as high as possible, then check your ratios, volume, area and dimensions, and adjust as necessary to get the best relationship. Maybe even tweak an inch here or there to get closer to Wes's philosophy, if you so desire. Personally, I think there are many other aspects to a room, other than just ratios, that can be "make or break" factors, so I wouldn't be too concerned if you come up with an otherwise good design but that doesn't follow Wes's tuning exactly. My $ 0.02. - Stuart -
