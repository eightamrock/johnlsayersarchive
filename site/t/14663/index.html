Soundman20203 August 2010#2
but we will need to support the walls structurally.
Is there any reason why you cannot build a proper complete MSM structure (walls and ceiling as one unit)? Or a reason why you cannot sheath the inner-leaf framing with plywood, to give it structural integrity, if for some reason you really have to hang the ceiling separately? You could use sway braces, but to my way of thinking it makes more sense to make the inner-leaf as a single one-piece self-supporting structure. (Unless of course code requires sway braces...) More details on your situation would help to clarify. And please also read and follow the the forum rules. - Stuart -

Soundman20204 August 2010#4
The separate ceilings were a suggestion by my designer, and I didn't really question him on that.
You'd get better isolation by resting the ceiling joists directly on the inner-leaf walls, and thus totally decoupling it from whatever is above it. Plus, that would help to give the walls some extra integrity, too, as the ceiling structure would tie the tops together. It also makes it easier to get a good long-lasting seal between the ceiling and walls. But again, if you post more details on your design plans, folks here can take a look at it and suggest ways of improving it. There may well be other issues that need attention. - Stuart -

plus6vu4 August 2010#7
I doubt that this will be much help, but this is what I ended up using in a similar situation. What happened to me was the ceiling framing was inadequate for the ceiling load, at least as far as I was concerned. So I used these http://www.mason-industries.com/masonin ... h610v8.pdf from Mason industries. Mine are type 30n. These can come with a variety of hanging options from simple threaded studs to eye-bolts (which I opted for). So far only half of the ceiling is up, but so far so good. The guy at Mason did all of the calculation based on my information and floor plan and supplied a diagram to work from. Not all of the hangers are the same rating as some hold more load depending on their location, like how close they are to the edge of the ceiling. I am thinking this is a better solution than using a "one size fits all" clip, but I'll stand corrected by someone in-the-know. They aren't cheap though. Price depends on the weight rating of the hanger. For me, it was a wash between several options and these seemed to best fit what I wanted to do. I know that you have a consultant, but keep in mind that ceilings are heavy and you have to make sure that your roof can hold the load. If you have snow, consider that weight too. Hope this helps. EDIT: After rereading your initial post, I realize that you have no ceiling framing, so my suggestion won't work for you. Not only would you need framing, but you would need at least a foot between the ceiling and roof joists for your hangers. Sorry. :oops:
xSpace7 August 2010#8
"In both cases you would not need to lose any more than the thickness of the drywall, plus maybe a slight extra margin for safety." With the walls framed like they are, with the angle, it would be impossible to do this. The joists will have to go from front to rear, rather than following the existing rafter placement. So the height that will be lost will be dictated by how far that span actually is which will determine what thickness of joist will be used. The actual lose of headroom could be anywhere from 7 inches to 12 inches, possibly more.
