Soundman202010 October 2015#3
The most noticeable problem is boominess.
Right. There really isn't much you can do about that, either. That's the problem with small rooms: you are up against the laws of physics. In order to fix it, you do need deep absorption, but the amount of absorption you need won't fit in the room: there would be no space left for you in there. And even if it could fit, the room would sound so dead and life-less as to be very uncomfortable, and probably unusable. That's not to say that you can't do something, and at least improve it! You sure can. But it is never going to sound fantastic, simply because of the size.
I record vocals and acoustic guitar in the booth
You could probably make it reasonably good for vocals, but I don't hold out a lot of hope for the acoustic guitar.
I've read that's a common problem with small rooms, and bass traps in the four vertical corners are generally the best starter solution.
There isn't enough room in there for the type of corner trap that works well. The recommended size is 36" across the diagonal, making them over 2 feet deep along the edges. Obviously, you can't even fit such a trap into your room.
I've read that the OC 703 style ones need to have a sizable air gap to really reach the low end.
OC-703 is excellent, and probably your best bet. However, it is a myth that you need it to be extremely thick with a very large air gap: That only applies to normally incident sound (sound waves that hit the panel exactly head on), but for sound waves that hit at any other angle, then automatically "see" a much greater depth of absorption in front of them. The higher the angle of incidence, the greater the depth. So in reality, for randomly incident sound, absorption does still happen at much lower frequencies than are predicted by the equations for normally incident sound alone, and even with relatively thin absorption and a relative thin air gap behind it, you still get reasonable absorption down to useful frequencies.
I considered throwing 1'-wide 3/4" plywood or MDF (having a hard time finding NRC ratings on these materials) in each corner to get rid of the right angles.
That would do nothing useful. The sound waves you are dealing with in the modal range for your room, have wavelengths in the range 4 feet to 14 feet, so they won't even notice an object that is only one foot wide. So that would not help at all to reflect those waves. Even worse, you don't even want to be reflecting them in the first place! You want to be either absorbing them or diffusing them. There isn't enough space in there to build diffusers large enough, so your only option is absorption.
What to do?
I would start by just getting a couple of panels of 4" thick OC-703, and putting them on adjacent walls. It doesn't really matter which two adjacent walls, but it would probably be easier to put one on the wall opposite the door, and two on one of the side walls. Also put one on the ceiling. If you have any left over, then put a double thickness right in the corner, or better still angle a piece diagonally across that corner, if you can afford the space to do that. That's about all you can do at low cost. As I mentioned, it's not going to make your booth sound like Abbey Road! But it will help a bit to take the edge off the boominess. However, it will also sound pretty dead in there, since you are over-treating it, but there's not other real solution. If you find that the high end of the spectrum is disgustingly dead, you could try wrapping some or all of the panels loosely with plastic. Use reasonably thick plastic, not the thin stuff that garbage bags are made of. Experiment with different thicknesses and different amounts of coverage, to see if you get a useful benefit from that. And please do let us know how it goes! - Stuart -
