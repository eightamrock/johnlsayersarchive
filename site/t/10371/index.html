DanDan24 March 2008#1
Hi, I am a fledgling acoustician. I am struggling with an apparent conflict of acoustic interests:- Warmth vs Accuracy in the Mix Room. As a recording engineer I have noted that very highly absorbent rooms, e.g. vocal booths, yield a very clear, uncoloured, perhaps even sparkly sound. I am thinking of acoustic guitar and vocals here. Conversely, reflective rooms are suited to fiddles, drums, etc. which benefit from the warmth added by the room. Many Control Rooms I have worked in were highly trapped. The sound from the speakers was in general, controlled and very clear. Too clear for my taste. Simply too much treble. In this situation it is very hard to make a mix that sounds normal in the outside world. I have treated my current control room extensively, actually as an IOA Diploma project. The room is small, 4.4 M long, concrete floor and walls, with a chimney breast in the middle. The room from hell! The treatment consisted of my own version of StudioTips Superchunks plus many HF MiniTraps and two LF MiniTrap panels. The attached Sketchup file has the ceiling cloud removed for visibility. This cloud consists of 4 HF MiniTraps. After treatment this room is now very similar to some of the better ones I have worked in, very dead, very clear sound. In practice it works very well. However to make this happen I have chosen Ribbon tweeter monitors and turned them down and applied a roll off. Room measurements show extremely short RT in the mid, strangely rising from 2-8K. I have rejected some very capable monitors, PMC IB1 and K and H 300, because of the sparkly nature of this room. I would much rather complete the job, get the room balanced. however I am stumped as to how? How to warm it up without losing the incredible imaging and mixing accuracy? Best Regards, Dan FitzGerald www.soundsound.ie

AVare24 March 2008#3
The most important thing is how well your mixes translate to end user systems. The last thing user systems are is consistent. One system has a peak in a frequency range where another has a dip, etc. The goal with accurate monitor systems is that you can hear what is actually in the recording. A story by Bob Katz about CD players may provide some insight into the difference between consumer and professional applications. Bob was using a very high end audiophile CD player for quality control on his projects. He heard some digital errors on one of his project's releases when played on a consumer player. He took the CD to his mastering studio and played it on the high end audiophile CD player and no errors were heard. He checked on several other decks, and sure enough, the errors showed up. The high end audiophile player was correcting errors to make sound more pleasing, but less accurate than what was actually on the CD. He switched to a more accurate CD player. More pleasing does not necessarily mean better for professional audio. To put in other words, accurate is absolute, pleasing isn't. Accurately, and pleasing, Andre

dahnielson29 March 2008#11
Well, the solution is in your head. Just having a good room and good monitors isn't enough. One have to listen and learn how they sound compared to other environments and speakers. A good practice is to mix something, burn it and play it back in different systems and rooms/environments. A good mix is an average of the target playback-systems/environments. The lesser number of targets the more precise the average will be (e.g. headphone only target -> binaural only mix), the more targets the more compromises and less precise average. My 2c.
