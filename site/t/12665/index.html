Soundman20202 July 2009#10
Brick (outside) -> (original) frame with insulation -> air -> frame with insulation -> double gyprock (different thicknesses with green glue in between).
Yup! That's the way to go. Although you don't really need different thicknesses of drywall: The slight advantage you get from the differing resonant frequencies is offset by the slight loss in mass. Just go for two sheets of 5/8 on the inner frame, and you'll be fine.
Or should I also put gyprock on the original leaf as well:
If you mean "right up against the brick, in direct contact with it", then not, its not worth it. Brick is about 3 times more dense than drywall, so you'd need to layer up drywall until it was three times the thickness of your brick wall, just to double the mass of the wall. That's an awful lot of drywall! One or two sheets isn't going to make any appreciable difference. What WILL make a big difference is to render that brick wall (stucco it, plaster it, etc.) and then paint it, to make certain that it is absolutely airtight. An airtight seal is your friend. But if you mean "in the other side of the outer frame, with an air gap", then the answer is MOST DEFINITELY NO! That would make a three-leaf system, which is a really bad idea. It will make your isolation worse, not better, at the low end of the spectrum.
I have read that the second option would cost more but not give a justifiable increase in reduction. Is this right?
Yup! Very right. And could make it worse, if you do it as a three-leaf.
Also, should I get the builder to construct an "inside" frame that would make the room a slightly irregular shape - not rectangle?
Which room? I see two. What is the purpose of each room? If you plan to use one as a control room, then you want to make it perfectly symmetrical, not irregular, but splaying the walls and ceiling to reduce flutter echo and help control first reflections is an option that is often recommended here. If you want to do that, you need to splay each side wall 6 degrees, and angle the ceiling up 12 degrees. The front of the room (where the speakers are) is at the narrowest point. The walls and ceiling widen out from that point backwards. Your "music" room seems to be big enough that you could afford the space to do that. But if you only want a practice room, then by all means make it as irregular as you feel like. Or not. Either will work. There are pros and cons both ways. - Stuart -

So you are going to be LOUD! Undoubtedly! You are probably looking at levels around 115 to 120 dB SPL in the live room, maybe even more. If you want good isolation, then most definitely! You simply cannot get good isolation from single-leaf construction, nor from the typical construction used in houses, and certainly not enough to tame an enthusiastic rock band.
I asked my builder to look into glass for the windows and he came up with this:
Looks like good laminate glass to me. But check those specs, and compare them to your reality. The very best they have to offer requires one pane of 12.5 mm, then a 16 mm air gap, then a second pane of 8.5mm: That is advertised as taking a level of 75 dB on one side, and reducing it to about 35 dB on the other side. So they are talking about roughly 40 dB TL for their best system. But you don't have a level of 75 dB to deal with: you will have a level of around 120 dB to deal with, when your band is jamming away merrily. 40 dB of reduction will get that down to 80 dB. 80 dB is loud. 80 dB is the level of really loud conversation, or maybe a motorbike passing a few meters away, or a circular saw running (but not cutting). That's what you'll be getting through the glass, and therefore that is the best you can hope for with your current plan...

However, it will be well insulated ...
That's actually irrelevant! Insulation is not the issue: isolation is. Contrary to popular belief, insulation does not stop sound. Insulation is good at keeping heat inside the room, but lousy at keeping sound inside the room. Only mass does that. There are two totally different and incompatible issues going on here. One is to isolate your room, the other is to treat it. Isolation means keeping all of the sound energy in the room, not letting any out, and the only way to do that is by building a large, solid, rigid, massive airtight shell inside of another large, solid, rigid, massive airtight shell. There are no shortcuts. You cannot bypass the laws of physics by hoping that they won't apply to you: they will. That is issue #1: keeping the sound in. Accomplished with "isolation". Issue #2 is the exact opposite: getting rid of the sound that you kept in :shock: . You don't want it bouncing around inside the room forever! That's called "reverb", and you don't want much of it in your room, but by forcing the sound to stay inside, you got it for sure! So now you have to treat the room acoustically, to deal with that energy that you did not let escape. That treatment usually involves insulation. But don't get confused here: insulation does not STOP sound. It just ABSORBS it. Just like a sponge does not stop the flow of water, but nevertheless absorbs water. Kind of the same idea here. So don't confuse the two concepts. Isolation stops sound, insulation does not. Insulation helps absorb sound as part of the treatment, but does not stop it. Isolation is accomplished with mass and airtight seals, treatment is accomplished by either converting the sound energy to heat (absorption), or diffusing it evenly back into the room. Isolation and treatment are two different concepts.
However, it will be well insulated and tbh I'm not too worried about sound escaping as I'm not planning on monitoring too loudly or too late at night.
Monitoring is not going to be the issue. Bleeding is. If you don't have good isolation between your CR and LR, you won't be able to hear on the speakers what is happening with the mics. You won't be able to position your mics well at all, since when you go into the CR to listen to them on the speakers, you will also hear the direct sound from the instrument coming through the walls and ceiling. Not only that, but any sound you make in the CR will still be heard in the LR, where it will be picked up by the mics.: Stirring your coffee, coughing, talking, listening to other tracks while you record, equipment noise, moving your chair around, dropping your pen on the floor... all of that will get into the mics in the LR, if you don't have good isolation between the CR and the LR. I'm talking from firsthand experience here: I just finished an album where all of the drum tracks were contaminated with the sound of birds: There were birds roosting nosily just outside the hall (not studio) where the drums were tracked, and every single mic on every single track of the album had bird song all over the drums and bass. It took me WEEKS of careful editing to get rid of that. You'd think that birds would not be a problem on drums tracks, but I can assure that they most certainly are!
Also, above will be a kitchen ...
So you will also have plumbing noises, refrigerator noises, and people noises leaking down from the top, as well as your own noises leaking in from the side? :)
(might get them to do the ceiling properly to keep kitchen noise out)
Ceiling alone wont help. A "room within a room" is exactly what the name says. If you only do the ceiling, then you didn't do anything. Unless you do ALL SIX sides of the room, then you ave not isolated it. You can't just do two or three sides and hope that sound won't find its way through the other sides, because it will. The laws of physics and Murphy's law guarantee that it will. The problem here is called "flanking". Any time that sound can get around your isolation by another path, it will. So if you build a great 2-leaf MSM ceiling with STC-65 rating, then sound will just flank around it through the walls, and you won't have isolation: Your room is ONLY as good as the weakest link, If that weak link is a door, or a window, or a wall that isn't there, then that's how good your entire room is. Think of it like an aquarium. You have to have a floor and all four sides in place in order for the aquarium to hold water. take out any one of those five, or even put a hole in it, and you no longer have an aquarium. same with your room. You need ALL sides in place in order for it to isolate. It is an all-or-nothing proposition. Sure, you can build them one at a time as funds permit, but it won't actually work properly' until you finish the last one.
Brick (outside) -> Plaster (or some kind of render) over the inside of the brick (painted) -> frame with insulation -> air -> frame with insulation -> double gyprock. I think thats right.
Yup, that's the basic idea, although you probably don't need the "frame with insulation" up against the brick wall. You need to fill the entire air gap with insulation, so the frame is just a waste of wood. You only need the frame for the inner leaf. Of course, you DO need two frames between your rooms, but only one on the outside walls. - Stuart -
