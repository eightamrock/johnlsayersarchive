Soundman202019 October 2011#5
I decided on the 5/8 + 1/2 idea because in my research, the numbers I saw showed very little difference between the two. Might be that, in the real world, there IS a difference.
Yes there is. The difference is that the 1/2" stuff is too thin, too flexible, and too low in mass to be recommendable for isolation. What many people don't realize is that all of the layers of mass in a wall act both independently and together. Yes, a layer of 5/8 plus a layer of 1/2 is just mass, from the point of view of MSM, and the equation for that only sees a total "leaf" of mass, regardless of how it is made up. Some people think that there won't be a difference, because its only a 1/8" difference, but in reality, there is a difference. Going from 1/2 to 5/8 adds about 30% extra mass (per layer). Not an enormous amount but enough to make a difference. Take a look at IR-761, and you might be surprised. But that's not all. Apart from adding mass to the MSM, the 1/2" drywall also acts by itself, and therein lies another big issue. 1/2" is thin, flexible, and low mass, when what you really want is thick, rigid and high mass. Stiffness is important in the region below resonance, for example. And yes, it is true that there is a slight advantage in the region of the coincidence dip for the wall (by having two different materials the dip is broader and less pronounced), as well as a slight advantage for refraction, but in reality neither of these "slight advantages" even comes close to outweighing the much larger advantage of thickness, rigidity and extra mass. And as it turns out, all of the gains from 5/8 over 1/2 are in the low end of the spectrum, where you need it most. :) - Stuart -

Soundman202019 October 2011#8
If you want high isolation and still want thin walls, then you need greater density. Replace one layer of drywall with something far more dense, such as fiber-cement sheeting, which is probably nearly twice as dense. So you could go with 5/8" drywall plus 1/2" fiber-cement, and that would actually be even better than 2 sheets of 5/8! Add Green Glue in between, and you'd have an excellent wall in little space. Or you could add a layer of lead sheeting to the wall: that is REALLY massive. A little pricey, though. Or you can resort to adding drywall between the studs, inside the wall, for more mass (if you build inside-out). Or a combination of the above. Plenty of ways of getting good isolation in a thin wall. The only caveat: it won't be as cheap as just using drywall. - Stuart -

Soundman202027 October 2011#23
Damping between studs and drywall doesn't accomplish much, since the stud isn't where the problems are. The entire wall is a tuned system, that resonates at a frequency set by the mass on each side and the size of the air gap. The insulation that you put in the air gap is the damping, and it works on the multiple resonant issues in the cavity itself, just like the shock absorbers in your car work on the multiple resonances set up by the wheels on the road. Putting something between the stud and the drywall would be kind of like putting a piece of rubber between your car wheel nuts and the bolts they go on, then tightening the bolts fully: It won't accomplish very much! The shock absorbers are already doing all of the damping that is needed. The little bit of rubber under the wheel nut might make you feel better, but doesn't actually accomplish much at all, in terms of improving the ride. If you really want to stretch the car wheel analogy to the limit, then I guess you could say that Green Glue works something like the air in the tyres, but nothing like like the suspension springs, and nothing like the shock absorbers (and certainly nothing like a bit of rubber behind the wheel nut!). Sort of.... Each part accomplishes a specific purpose, and they all work together to smooth the ride overall, so you don't feel the bumps. It is the same basic principle with your wall: each part has a job to do, and they all work together, but adding a bit of rubber in the wrong place will not help much. So, damping between the studs and the drywall isn't notably useful, since the entire wall is vibrating at different resonant frequencies anyway, including the studs, and that is damped by the insulation in the cavity, not by bits of rubber on the studs. Since the entire wall vibrates, including the studs, that rubber would not actually have anything to damp! I'm not saying it won't do anything at all: just that it won't do anything worthwhile, since it won't be acting on what needs to be acted on: air-borne resonance in the cavity. - Stuart -
