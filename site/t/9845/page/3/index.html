After framing the doors, there were some huge gaps. We inserted backing rods and shot foam up against 'em, then we trimmed the foam and applied SHEETROCK brand acoustical sealant. Industrial-sized tubes cost $5. This door frame top took in about a full tube on each side. The sealant stands up really well. It’s easy to sculpt a layer of it that’s over an inch thick.
External image, not preserved — original: http://aycu19.webshots.com/image/42938/2006231689694288597_rs.jpg
Foam application gets out of control pretty easily. On the other side of this wall it’s not so bad because the backer rod barrier stops the foam from coming out the other side. The backer rod was pushed in about ½ inch on the other side, to allow space for sealant on that side. After this foam hardened, I took a box cutter and carefully trimmed the excess around both edges, and then I used a paintbrush to clean out stray chunks of foam and drywall dust. This is time consuming. It was a day’s work to trim foam, carve/clean out the areas and tape 'em up, and then apply the sealant, for both sides of two door frames.
External image, not preserved — original: http://aycu37.webshots.com/image/44796/2000045122563339490_rs.jpg
I shot almost a whole tube of sealant into this space. The backer rod is very soft. It’s easy to ram the sealant tube nozzle right through it so that sealant flows through to get behind it. By the time I was done sealing this particular space, the backer rod was just mush.
External image, not preserved — original: http://aycu23.webshots.com/image/45462/2000886123898254734_rs.jpg
External image, not preserved — original: http://aycu26.webshots.com/image/41425/2000436377424203252_rs.jpg
Trimming all the foam from the edges really helps give you a clean-looking seal. Occasionally the box cutter would bite into the drywall and shave some off, leaving a nice smooth face for the sealant to run up against. This is what it looked like after I pulled the tape. What I learned real fast is, tape both sides and then when you're done sealing a crack, pull the tape off RIGHT AWAY. The wetter the sealant is when you pull the tape, the cleaner your edge will be. On the top-of-door-frame pic here, you can see a piece of mangled backer rod jutting out, forced forward by all the sealant that I pumped in behind it. To me it really didn’t matter where the backer rod ended up, as long as I was certain that I was pumping in all the sealant that the space could possibly take.

Vertustudio18 February 2008#57
My entire south wall has the same structure with regard to rock and insulation: QuietRock 525, Type X, cellulose insulation 'mattress' between staggered studs, Type X. However, the control room portion of the south wall has Green Glue between the 525 and Type X sheets, in fact, I used the recommended dose of 3 tubes (that's about $90) per 4X8 sheet. The iso room has none or maybe just a little bit of leftovers from the one case I bought. So, I can make a pretty good assessment of how good Green Glue is because I have this basis of comparison. My conclusion is that Green Glue works great and is a good value. It might even be a better value than QR 525 - it's worth considering trading out QuietRock for Type X/Green Glue if you must choose between one or the other. My recommendation is, if you use Green Glue, don't skimp - go with the three tubes per 4X8. The effect is that panel resonance is significantly dampened, producing a far more 'thuddy' wall. QuietRock. I got mine for $80, and I bought 20 sheets. I don't have a basis of comparison in my studio to assess its effectiveness. South wall has one layer out of three, party wall has two layers out of four, west wall has one layer out of two, and the ceiling and north wall have none. East wall is cinder block. QuietRock 525 has a layer of regular drywall bound to a layer of 'ceramic' infused composite material. It's heavier and harder to cut. I suspect that this '1 sheet of 525 = 7 sheets of Type X' is dubious, especially at low frequencies. Fact is, I don't know. Anyway...if I had to do it again, and bearing in mind that three tubes of Green Glue is about the same price as one sheet of QR 525, I'm not even sure what I'd do because I can't really judge the effectiveness of 525 *in my particular space,* with *my particular issues* regarding sound coming in from the outside. I'd definitely use Green Glue over 525 on ceilings if footfall noise were an issue. I'm so glad I used it in front of the furnace where low frequency hum is the only issue I had left after I put in the cellulose, which takes care of mid-to-high frequencies *easily.* Blown-in cellulose insulation is the bomb. :D One thing for sure: Green Glue works well! Vertustudio18 February 2008#59
$80 / Sheet for the QR 525. Doubtless, 525 gets less effective the lower you go in frequency. But adding layers of drywall sacrifices space and costs money to put up. 525 has more mass. Whether it's worth the money or not depends on how much value you place on the actual qualities that QR offers, whatever they may be. There are better ways to spend money than to spend it on 525, I agree. Regarding the cellulose, it's a no-brainer. Direct comparisons with fiberglass are just shocking. Most of the sound that enters nine solid inches of that stuff never comes out the other side. Look back a few pages at the control room with just studs and insulation. Every set of opposing surfaces in the room were different except for the north and south walls, which were both insulated. At this stage, that was one of the nicest sounding spaces I'd ever been in, albeit just a tad on the absorptive side. If I wasn't concerned about soundproofing and equity, I would have just nailed up fabric-wrapped perforated particle board over the studs and called it a day. In retrospect, I wish I would have done just that on the north wall (both rooms), which are backed by cinder block and received only one layer of type X right over the studs and cellulose. This would have settled any treatment issues on that wall, but the fabric costs would have run a bit high. There's enough air between the studs; the insulation is very active. When you pound on the wall beneath the control room window, you can look through from the top and see the insulation dancing around in there, doing its thing. It smokes fiberglass. The only downside I can see here is that this insulation is going to settle much like cereal in a box. If you were totally anal about it, you could wait a while and then drill some small holes near the ceilings and have the insulation guy come back and spray more in there. Doubt I'll be doing that! Window glass installed, comprising two slanted ¼” laminated panes. This was tricky. The first batch of glass was defective; one pane had scratches on it, the other was not cut to spec; both panes had to be reordered, and we didn’t see the scratch on the first pane until after it was installed. We had to take it down and strip the silicone off the rails, and suddenly the build project took on the vibe of a restoration project. We tried to get a perfectly clean install, but alas, a small smudge and a few particles got trapped inside. These are only visible when the light hits the panes a certain way. When I set up these shots, I backlit them with a set of three lights right up against the wall on the other side, pointed along the *length* of the glass; only then did I see the smudge. There are also a couple of pencil-tipped sized defects between the laminations (NOT the surface) on one side, again, barely noticeable. My advice; inspect the glass very carefully before installing it (d’oh). When inspecting your own glass, hit it with artificial light from as many angles as possible before you lock both panes in. This way you’ll stand a better chance of catching all the smudges. Anyway, I’m really happy with this install. It’s clean and you have to be really looking in order to find the imperfections. I’ll have to be cool with this until some enraged rapper throws a mic through the glass, then I can start over. But wait…this is supposed to be a personal studio – no clients. Heh. Well, the way that all the costs are spiraling here, I better start looking for some clients! I’ve been in studios where some piece of molding or something is dangling out between the panes…total Jethro. At least I don’t have to worry about that happening, what with my Gorilla-Glued trim and all…sall good! I gotta laugh. I’m ordering panels and treatments and such over the phone, and discussing this build and so forth, and the guy on the other side of the call will say, “This will turn out OK, but in the future…” And I just interrupt him and say, “There IS no future, man.” Mo’ Later,

This week I’m getting room treatments made and putting them up. Here’s a shot of the iso room. My goal here is to have the vast majority of treatments be easily removable (iso room only). My wife designed this trapeze system that allows 2” panels to be placed anywhere on the ceiling. It’s made from PVC pipe and end caps, drywall anchors, sewn fabric strips, and screw eyes. After this picture was taken, we took everything back down and shortened the fabric strips, so now there’s a 1.5 inch gap between the ceiling and the top of a 2” panel. The big reason why I did this is because I have a low ceiling, and I want to be able to throw up a panel quickly anytime over any overhead mic placed anywhere in the room. I don’t want to be chained to having a permanent absorptive surface across the entire ceiling. Now, I can sling 2X4 panels of 703 (or anything light) easily, anywhere, anytime. The panel you see in this shot is a piece of 703 in a Chameleon Traps bag. These are pretty cool if you can afford ‘em, but I’d never use them in a permanent installation. They just don’t look ‘permanent’ enough. They’re great for slinging around though. $30 per bag is a reasonable price, considering how much this studio gear costs in general. Mo’ Later,
