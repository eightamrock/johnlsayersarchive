Soundman20206 July 2009#3
My first thought is that there is no mass in that stuff, so if that's all you have between the frame and the jamb, then your isolation is shot. You need to keep the mass solid all the way around the room, with no gaps. The drywall must go right up to the jamb, not just the frame, creating a complete unbroken solid massive surface. My second thought is that if your door is very heavy (which it should be!), then just shimming the jamb to the frame and filling the gap with polyurethane foam, is NOT going to give the door the support it needs. The jamb needs good, solid support all around, especially on the hinge side. That whole thing is going to sag and eventually come apart, totally wrecking your isolation. Frankly, I think your constructor does not understand at all what you are trying to accomplish, and he screwed up there. If that were my room, I think I'd make him take it out again and do it right. But that's just me. Maybe others might have better opinions on what to do there. - Stuart -

stuntbutt6 July 2009#6
Looks like a standard door installation to me. A rough opening for a door isn't always plumb or straight and you want your door jam to be both. That is why rough openings are oversized and the difference is bridged with shims which can be finely adjusted. If it were me, I would make some door trim from 3/4" MDF to get some mass over the gap and caulk it airtight.

Agreed. If the framing isn't plumb enough on at least the hinge side (the side I work hardest at making plumb) then common install is wooden shims. Fire rated and solid core doors can pull a jamb out of shape with little trouble just because the door is so heavy and puts so much tension on the framing/wall at the opening. I like to get it as tight as possible on the hinge side to alleviate potential pinching or if it gets hinge bound over time, from shifting. This could be any heavy door install, whether in an acoustic environment or a commercial environment, if it is of a wooden jamb. After setting the door, stuff insulation into the cavity and add the extra mass into the trim, like Stunt recommended with the MDF.
