xSpace23 October 2012#2
I wouldn't attache anything hard to the concrete and then build on it, especially if it were filled of insulation. Now if you laid sleepers (furring) on the slab and used sand...this sand has mass and you can route conduit, cables, etc. A damped floor can be constructed using 703 (owens corning) rigid insulation panels laid flat on the slab and then cover with 2 layers of 3/4 inch plywood, running one layer and then the next layer perpendicular to the first layer.

xSpace23 October 2012#4
"Is this correct that the sleepers should ideally be resting on insulation/rubber/sand" No A sleeper is attached to the main substrate. It is a hard connection between foundation and a systematic path to attach the flooring. Used extensively for solid hard wood flooring and creates the "echo" or hollow sound most noted with this type of application. What you are trying to avoid is this hollow sound AND the possibility of creating a diaphragm which will work in the same way a drum head does. Still the sleepers are hard connected to the substrate (A substance or layer that underlies something, or on which some process occurs, e.g. hardwood flooring installation.) and the sand goes into the cavities created by this material installation process.

I'm not sure why you would want to nail laminate floor into the underlying concrete... :) But point taken on the cost issue: leveling cement ain't cheap. And if the OP doesn't like laminate, then it's a moot point, I guess. Which leads back to the original question... You CAN build a raised floor like you proposed originall, yes, but as Brien already pointed out, there is the possibility of resonance if you don't either damp the cavity properly or fill it. As with any surface next to an air gap, it will resonate at a certain frequency. So either you need to tune that to a useful frequency for the room (hard to do), or tune it so low that it is out of the range of interest, or damp it heavily, or fill the cavity with something solid. - Stuart -
xSpace26 October 2012#9
Anyone that is "learning" will conclude that to simply get a material with a name that reveals both what it does and is also a marketing device will assume that this material will in fact self level. Not only does it not self level it can create more irregularities in a substrate than may have existed. Assuming we have an unbuilt project, which is the case here, and we throw in "self leveling" high strength concrete onto the existing floor the next phase will be to construct walls and that leads to a hard fastener in the rat sill of the wall assemblies.
