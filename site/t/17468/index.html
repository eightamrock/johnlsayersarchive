Hi Eric, and welcome! :) You ask a lot of interesting questions. Many of them have been answered before in different ways on the forum, but I'll give you a brief summary answer here in any case, since there's a lot of them in your post, and it might be useful to others in the future:
I’m not sure if the walls finished over the foundation walls are insulated. If they are not, do I need to tear them down, insulate, and put drywall back up?
Basic answer: Probably, but "it depends". You didn't say how much isolation you need, in terms of real-world decibels, so no-one can say if you need to do that or not. But assuming that you need to isolates something like acoustic drums, or you live in a typical city or suburban house with other people, then yes, you will need insulation in your walls. If all you need to isolate is voice-overs (ordinary speech) and you live alone in a silent concrete bunker, then most likely it isn't necessary. However (and more on this below), if those "finished walls" are built as normal stud-framed walls with drywall on both sides, and you want to build a proper two-leaf MSM isolation system, then you have no choice: one side of those walls must come off, and stay off, leaving the studs exposed, and if there is no insulation, then you will need to install some.
I know they have not sealed in between the floor plate and the concrete floor.. So do I need to just tear down and rebuild this wall anyway?
You don't need to tear it down just for that reason, but you do need to get to that joint and seal it. Sealing is critically important for good isolation. No seal = no isolation.
Remember, in this case, I am referring to the finished walls over the foundation, not the double studded two leaf walls I will build for the interior walls.
This is what I mentioned above, about the "more on this below": You already have a wall with at least one leaf on it, possibly two leaves. So why would you ALSO need an ADDITIONAL double-studded two leaf wall? You only need an additional SINGLE stud frame with a leaf on ONE side. And if the existing "finish walls" already have drywall on both sides, then you have to REMOVE the drywall from one side, in order to end up with a grand total of two leaves, only two leaves, and nothing but two leaves. If you build a double-studded wall next to your exiting finish wall, then you have a FOUR leaf wall! :shock:
Is it true that when constructing a double studded wall, that using different stud spacings and leaf masses for the two different walls is the right thing to do?
No it is not true, and it would be pretty darn hard to do for anything but a very short wall. If you have different stud spacing on either side, it wont be long until the studs start coinciding... What you should do is choose either 16" OC or 24" OC spacing for your new wall. But you DON'T need to build a double studded wall in any case! Sea above...
If it is necessary, what is the best way to seal between the bottom stud plate and the floor to isolate?
Acoustic sealant, or common flexible caulk. Or if the floor is very uneven, then perhaps a rubber strip under the bottom plate.
Is it necessary to seal between two layers of drywall on the same leaf using a product like green glue or a regular bathroom/kitchen caulk?
Caulk, no, Green Glue, yes. But Green Glue is NOT a sealant, and nor is it glue. It is a visco-elastic polymer that acts as constrained layer damping between the two layers. Very different thing, acoustically. I have an appointment outside right now, so I'll get back to you on the rest later, but I did want to give you the above to get you started. - Stuart -

I am really confused about how to construct my ceiling. First of all, I will unfortunately have air ducts running down the LONG way of my control room (my listening position is faced parallel with the ducts) on the ceiling, one all the way to the left, and one all the way to the right. So I have to drywall around the ducts..but how should I finish the middle of my ceiling?
IF the ducts cannot be moved, then you have two options: Built rectangular soffits around them (perhaps as part of your wall), or angle your ceiling on both sides, to a peak in the middle (gable). Neither is a fantaastic choice, acoustically. Each has it's pros and cons.
I am trying to decide about my control room dimensions and how to calculate what is best.
There are a number of good room mode / room ratio calculators around, many of them linked from the "stickies" right here on this forum. Stay away from the bad ratios, get close to one of the many good ones, and you'll be fine.
But first of all, how does that ceiling situation factor into my proper room dimension calculations?
You'd probably be OK just using an average for the height dimension. The mode calculators will not give you accurate answers if your room is not rectangular, but at least you'll get a ball-park idea.
Just a note, I wanted to make the back wall entirely absorbent with like a foot of insulation. So will that take away any problem making the room this long when the width and height are proportionately much smaller?
If you have the space, I'd use hangers at the back. When calculating ratios and modes, you still measure the room length the the actual physical walls, not just to the face of the treatment.
I want to construct soffit mounts, but what if I get monitors that are a little bigger in the future..can I build the speaker cavity in the soffit mount a little bigger than my current monitors to accommodate a future purchase, or do the monitor cavities need to be a tight fit for monitors?
Barefoot once posted a design here for a soffit mount system where it is easy to replace the speakers in the future. That's also here on the forum. - Stuart -

You already said that, and I already answered: If those walls are stud-framed with surfaces on both sides, then you WILL need to remove the drywall facing the room, and you WILL need to leave that drywall off, regardless of whether or not the wall is already insulated. You WILL need to do this because those existing walls already have two leaves on them, so in order to avoid building a 3-leaf system, you MUST remove one leaf. If you were to do that, then once again you would be be building a 3-leaf or 4-leaf wall, which is a bad idea in general, especially if you need to isolate low frequencies. ... and they are not decoupled or sealed at the floor plate of course.
They don't need to be decoupled, since they are your OUTER leaf, and will not be touching your inner-leaf walls at all. But they do need to be sealed, regardless of whether they are inner or outer leaf: Everything must be sealed! So if you can't see if they are sealed, or can't get to parts that need sealing, then you need to figure out how to do that: sealing is fundamental to isolation. - Stuart -
