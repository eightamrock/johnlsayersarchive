Soundman202012 November 2011#5
So i will basic have build room in room on a floating floor?
Room-in-a-room: Yes. Floating floor: No. You most likely do not need that. If you have a concrete slab floor on the ground, with nothing underneath you, then you are most likely fine. Floating an entire room is complicated, very expensive, and hard to do right. There's about a million ways to do it wrong, and only one way of doing it right. You also do not have enough room height to be able to float. With only 2.78 m, that's already low and you are going to loose a lot of that with the isolation ceiling anyway.
I dont know how fast i can get measurements since i dont have the equipment and knowledge to do this.
All you need is a basic sound level meter, and a full-range loudspeaker that can produce at least 90 dB. Not complicated. All we need to now is how loud you are in there right now, how loud things are around you, and how quite you have to be. All we need is a single number stating how much isolation you need. But you can't guess: If you guess to low, then the studio will end up not isolated properly, and either the neighbors will call the police all the time, or you'll be annoyed all the time by externals noises. And if you guess too high, then the isolation will work great, but it will take up more space than is necessary, and you will have wasted a lot of money on materials you didn't need! So that number is important. Beg, borrow or buy a meter. A cheap one costs about US$ 50 to US$ 100 on Amazon, e-Bay, etc.
I was wondering if there is some kind of comprimise between optimal isolation without loosing too much space.
Yes there is, but them you need some SERIOUS amounts of money! You can have fantastic isolation, lose almost no space, and for very little money! Just pick ONE of the above... :) In other words, the three are mutually exclusive. If you want great isolation, it can be low cost but will use lot's of space, or it can fit into little space but then it will be very expensive, as you need exotic materials and techniques, such as steel plate or sheet lead... Basically, there is only one way to keep sound in or out of your room: Mass. Lots of mass. That means you need very heavy materials. The cheap heavy materials take up a lot of space. The thin heavy materials are very expensive. It's that simple.
Is there any standard i could aim to achieve concernig the decibels?
Well, yes! That's part of the process of deciding how much isolation you need. Look at the standard NC curves to see which one best fits your needs. Then measure the amount of ambient noise around your room, to see how high the level is. Subtract "A" from "B", and you have your answer! That's the MINIMUM isolation that will work for you, to keep OUT sounds that would otherwise disturb you. That's one part of the process. But it says nothing at all about the other part: How loud YOU are INSIDE the room, and what the legal requirements are for noise in your area. For example, if you are going to mix at 100 dB, and the noise regulations say that you cannot make more noise than 30 dB, then you need 70 dB of isolation, which is really hard to do. But if you are going to mix at 85 dB and the noise regulations allow you to go to 55 dB, then you only need 30 dB of isolation, which is really easy to do. Just so you get the picture: isolating to 70 dB is about one thousand times harder than isolating to 30 dB. I mean that very literally. To stop 70 dB of sound, your walls must absorb one thousand times as much energy as for 30 dB. That's why it is important that you get a meter and make some measurements. If you isolate to 70 dB and only need 30, then you spent a huge amount of money that you didn't need to. If you isolate to 30 but really need 70, then you'll be spending a lot of time talking to the cops, and to the judge, and paying fines.... :)
I guess the more the better in my case.
More is always better, but can you afford it? :) Cost goes up really fast when you need very high levels of isolation. Why pay more than you need to? :) - Stuart -

Soundman202025 November 2011#11
Sure! A meter without "A" and "C" weighting is most likely linear, which isn't much use, since the human ear is not linear. The weighting curves adjust the sensitivity of the meter to different frequencies, making it more sensitive to some tones than others. "A" weighting is designed more for industrial measurements of low level noise, and also used by audiologists, who need to relate sound levels to the full range of human hearing, and the damage that can be done by loud noises at all frequencies. The "A" weighting curve is an approximation of the actual sensitivity of the human ear, and gives much higher priority to mid-high and high frequencies, and less to low frequencies. It is also the scale used by most municipal noise ordinances. which is great for us as it gives less "weight" to low frequencies, and studios do tend to produce lots of lows, which are the hardest to isolate. "C" weighting is more realistic for loud sounds in general, such as speakers playing music: it gives more weight to lower frequencies, which is more how our ears work when sounds get very loud: for quite sounds, we tend to hear higher frequencies better than lows (like "A" weighting), but when things get loud, our hearing sensitive flattens out somewhat, and we hear low frequencies similar to highs. So "C" is more useful for understanding how loud we really are, and how well our walls are really isolating, across the full spectrum. It's more realistic for real-world studio measurements. The "slow" and "fast" settings basically make the meter more or less sensitive to peaks: put it on "fast" and it it will tell you more about the sudden brief peaks that might be present in sounds. Put it on "slow" and it averages out the levels over a period of 250 ms. "Slow" is more useful for us usually, as we want to get an idea of the overall level of the music, and the overall level of isolation, but "fast" is sometimes useful for some things, like impact noise and to catch peaks that might not be obvious. Normally, you'll use the meter on "C" weighting, slow response, unless the cops come knocking on your door with a complaint, in which case you switch it to "A", which is what they most likely use, and greatly favors you anyway, since it is much less sensitive to heavy low frequency content, such as drums and bass, escaping from your room! :) There are other scales too, but we don't need them much for studio design or live sound work. Although if your meter has Leq, that could be useful. (It's kind of a very long term average, or "noise dose" type measurement). That's pretty much all you need to know. When you use the meter, hold it far away from your body with your arm almost fully extended, at chest height, and angled upwards at about 30-45 degrees (mic pointing a bit upwards, between the horizon and the ceiling/sky). That is supposed to give you the most accurate readings. If you have a bit extra money to spare and want a meter that does everything, even generating pink noise, showing the level in each of 31 frequency bands, and checking things like phase and RT-60, then get a Phonic PAA-3: I love mine! It's more than most people need (more of a spectrum analyzer than just a sound level meter), but I find it VERY useful. - Stuart -

Soundman20201 December 2011#14
did some research for full range speakers but the speakers i found usually start at 45 - 65 hz.
There's a big difference between starting at 45 Hz and starting at 65 Hz! That's half an octave. But having said that, speakers don't just suddenly cut off to zero at the lowest frequency: the response rolls off gradually. The number is normally the point where it has dropped to 3 dB below the average, but it is still putting out energy below that. But if you can't find a good full-range speaker, then get a mediocre one plus a sub-woofer and a cross over. The sub should take you down to 30 Hz or even lower, and that's probably good enough. - Stuart -

Hi Andrew. First, isolation: Your floor looks like a raised wooden floor, not the concrete slab I had assumed. Can you confirm how that is built? Since this is a ground floor apartment, I expected that the floor would be concrete laid directly on the ground. Is that correct? Second, the windows: I suggest that you remove those existing windows and frames, and replace them with a single piece of thick laminate glass (something like 12mm or so, at least) in new heavy wooden frames, that are well sealed to the walls. Third, the walls: What are they? Brick? Concrete? Drywall on studs? Something else? Are teh interior walls the same as the exterior walls, or are they different? Specifically, the wall with the door in it: Is that brick, or is it drywall on studs? Fourth, the radiators: They have to go. You can't build your "room-in-a-room" with those in the way. Fifth, HVAC. You are going to need some method of getting fresh air into your room and exhausting the stale air: Have you thought about that? You can probably install some form of HVAC ducts in the area where the windows are right now. Sixth, electrical: Where is the distribution panel, or at least the connections for the two circuits that you will be using to supply your studio? Those have to come into the room somewhere. Seventh, ceiling: How is it built? It's an apartment block, so I guess it is concrete? Eighth, budget: You said that you thought budget would not be a problem, but it would be good to know what range of numbers you are thinking about: Some materials are very good but expensive, so if you have the budget then those can be included. If not, then you can only use cheap materials. OK, with all that in place, which is your outer leaf, you need to decide on the shape and dimensions for your inner-leaf, and then for your treatment. Probably your easiest route is to take a look at all of John's designs and other designs on the forum, choose one that is similar to what you need and to close to the size of your room, and the adapt it. Your room is nearly square, so I'd probably do splayed walls if I were designing this room. You have a slight advantage with the room facing the windows, so I'd use that orientation. And I'd try to move the door forward a meter or so, to give you better arrangement at the rear of the room. So draw up a basic layout based on the above, and post it here for more comments! :) - Stuart -
