Hi "seavote", and welcome! :) Lots of questions you have there! I only have a few minutes to answer the most important ones, but the other answers should flow from that, or you cab find them by using the "search" feature of the forum.
dimensions are roughly 10'6" x 16' with a 7' ceiling.
That's a low ceiling, but you already knew that! so probably angling it is not going to be worthwhile: Your idea of using a cloud will work. Angled ceilings are useful, yes (when done properly), but room height is also important, and in your case I reckon height is more useful than angles.
alternate diffusion and absorbtion on walls
Forget diffusion. The room is not big enough to be able to benefit from diffusion. You need a large distance between the diffuser and your head for that, so diffusion is only appropriate for large rooms. Of course, the manufacturers of diffusers don't tell you that...
what is best for the floor rug or wooden flooring?
Forget carpet: it does the exact opposite of what you need for a small room. It absorbs lots of highs, some mids, and no lows at all. What you need is to absorb lots of lows, some mids, and very little highs.
but ive seen an interveiw with thomas jouanjean where he suggest a preference for a wooden floor in a low ceiling studio.
He's right. Wooden floor, or concrete, or ceramic, or anything hard, solid, and reflective is the right choice for a studio floor, provided that the rest of the room is treated correctly. Look at photos of top studios all around the world: How many of the are fully carpeted? NONE! There are several very good acoustic and practical reasons for that.... See above, for part of the acoustic reasons.
also i am decoupling the ceiling from the joist
How?
i was thinking of some collapsible 2-3 foot pyramids i could place around the room . would the gains justify the effort??
Probably not. Look around the forum to find out how studio treatment is normally done. - Stuart -

Soundman202010 October 2012#5
Disappointed that my diffusers won’t help.
That seems to be a common disappointment! :) Many studio builders discover too late that most types of diffuser create artifacts that take a lot of space to merge together and even out again. Some types can work (such as polys and slot walls), but those based on numeric sequences are out, unless they are only tuned to high frequencies.
The room above is a bedroom so I’ll be decoupling with rc-2channel, 24” on center and ½” sheetrock.
Depending on how much isolation you need (in terms of decibels), and at what frequency you need it, that probably won't be enough. 1/2" drywall is too thin, too flexible, and too light weight for attaining high levels of isolation. 5/8" drywall is much better, and you might even need two layers (once again depending on how many dB of isolation you need). Two layers of 5/8" drywall on the ceiling would probably need 16" OC, rather than 24" OC, for safety.
On the floor I’ll use felt paper (moisture barrier)
If you have or suspect that you will have a moisture problem in your concrete, then you must get that fixed first, before you do anything else at all. You cannot solve that with just a layer of roofing felt. You must be certain that there is no issue with damp in the foundations, floor or walls before you start building the room. If not, you'll have to tear the room apart at some point in the future to fix it, and by the time you notice the problem, it will have advanced to the stage where is it a major, expensive, big-time problem.
I believe the block and my new wall will be sufficient sound proofing.
Unfortunately, "believing" something to be true will not make it so! :) The only way to find out for sure is to determine how much isolation you need, in real-world objective terms, stated in decibels. Then you can look at the tables and charts to see what type of construction will provide that level of isolation. Just guessing is not a good idea, and guaranteed to be wrong. You need a proper sound level meter in order to determine two things: "How you are you?" and "How quite do you need to be?". The difference between those two is how much isolation you need.
The 3rd block wall adjoins my tenants’ kitchen. I hoping the block and the new wall will be ok.
Most likely not! It sounds like you are saying that you have a common "party wall" between you and your neighbor, so any vibration or sound that gets into that wall will be transmitted straight into his kitchen. It needs to be isolated properly, and "hoping" won't do that. :)
Since I need to get rid of bass energy as well as decouple the walls from the existing structure
You seem to be confusing two concepts here: Isolation and treatment. Isolation is what you do to stop sound getting in or out of the room. Treatment is what you do inside the room to modify the acoustics so that the room sounds good. They are two very different, and mutually exclusive things. First you isolate the room, and you do that for ALL the frequencies that you want to keep in or out. Then you treat the room based on the results of the isolation, once again for all of the frequencies that are causing problems.
Track will be caulked to floor on some type of absorbent material (suggestions???)
That is not the right way to float a wall. Floating a wall is a big deal, and requires careful calculation of many factors, as well as careful study of the characteristics of the resilient isolating material. And you most likely do not need to do that anyway: Once again, that depends on how much isolation you need, in terms of decibels, and the frequencies that you need it for.
and lightweight ½” sheetrock.
Once again, bad idea if you need a high level of isolation. 1/2" is flexible, thin, and low mass. What you need for isolation is rigidity, thickness and lots of mass.
it was my understanding that a lightweight wall will have more give to it and will resonate and help get rid of some of the low frequency energy
Where will it go? The energy has to go somewhere. Yes, you can make a membrane trap (panel trap) like that, provided that you do it correctly, but that is room treatment that you put INSIDE the finished room, and it cannot be part of the wall. If you build a thin, flexible light-weight wall that is tuned to bass frequencies, then that wall will allow those bass frequencies to get through to the other side, where they will be heard loud and clear. So this would destroy your isolation at those frequencies.
I can punch these into a mode calculator and see what comes up.
Ratio is 1 : 1.38 : 2.3. Not looking very good. The bonello diagram looks like a mountain range, and that ratio fails one of the critical tests used by the BBC to determine if a room is usable. There are several large gaps in the modal response at the very low end, then some close bunches further up. I'd try to change that a little, if possible.
I was planning on floor to ceiling wide range hemlholtz bass traps
Not sure what those would be! Helmholtz traps are tuned to very specific frequencies, and by definition are not broad band. They are also very tricky to tune precisely, and not much use if not tuned precisely. They must be tuned to the exact mode that is causing problems, but you won't know what that is until the room is finished and you have tested it. You could build a broadband slot wall, which actually is a Helmholtz device, and could be tuned to several modes, but that's not an easy proposition to get right for corners. It would be far better to build ordinary broadband bass traps, based on absorption, such as superchunks or even plain old ordinary corner traps.
As many 2” Owens-Corning 603 cloth covered traps with 2” spacing (hopefully 2 per wall) as I can afford.
I think you mean "703" not "603"! :) And 4" of 703 would be much better than 2", as it gets down to lower frequencies, which you definitely will need to cover. - Stuart -
