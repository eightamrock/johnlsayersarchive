Ro20 August 2009#14
Depends... There's different techniques to add mass, let's say GB for instance, to the ceiling. These different techniques depend on the current ceiling. If you have an exterior roof which is your ceiling, you don't ever want to penetrate it from below. You might accidentally cut through the ceiling/roof and damage the roof itself resulting in leaks (water). In that case you have to use nails and insert them in the beams, not the ceiling, to support the mass. Use clads/trims on the last layer of mass to finish it off (and make it carry the whole weight) Second; your current ceiling has to be strong enough to carry the mass. If your ceiling is strong and it's not a roof and you're sure you're not gonna damage it (damn long nails or screws penetrating to the upper part.. yay) you can screw the mass directly onto the ceiling. Use backerrod and sealant to finish it.

Soundman202012 September 2009#22
Personally, I'd go with "Plan B", your second option with the double frames. It will give you better isolation. But there is a problem on your plan: The image labeled "garage inside-out walls.jpg" shows the inner-leaf wall on the left, with the door in it, being mechanically connected to the existing outer-leaf walls. You'll need to separate that, so that no part of the inner leaf touches any part of the outer leaf.
I'm loosing about 60mm in my precious space in width if I build this with inside-out walls,
Acutally, you aren't losing anything! In fact, you are GAINING space with an inside-out wall, which is its main purpose. You use the stud space for acoustic treatment. With your "Plan A"m, you'd still need to add acoustic treatment in the room, and you'd have to hang it on the finished inner-leaf walls, which takes up a lot of depth. With an inside-out wall, the acoustic treatment goes between the studs, saving you space. In fact, if you take a close look you'll find that your room with "Plan B" is slightly larger all around, acoustically speaking, since you don't have the added thickness of the RC. So your inner-leaf drywall is already a bit further out, all around. You gain the thickness of the RC, plus the depth of the studs. Now, your "Plan B" shows your studs as 2x3's, but if you plan to put a ceiling across that room :shock: then I think they should probably be 2x4s. xSpace can give you good advice here, but it seems to me that you'd need 2x4s to support the new inner ceiling structure, not 2x3s. - Stuart -

Soundman202021 September 2009#24
Do you think that 2x3s won't carry that inside ceiling stucture? If so, I'd better be using 2x4s??
Don't trust my guesses! Get a structural engineer to tell you what you need. I'm not one. The reason for my comment is just to point out potential issues that you should be aware of, not really to tell you the dimensions that you should use. You'll be hanging a LOT of mass from those joists (two layers of 16mm drywall is about 12 kg/m2), so you need to have the structure in place to do that safely. Even 2x4's might not cut it, depending on the span, loads, deflection, and type of wood: You might need 2x6s, or even more. You could look at span tables for the types of wood that you plan to use, and work it out from there, but your best bet is to get a structural engineer to look at what you plan to do, and tell you what type of structure you need to support it. It would be pretty sad if you built a great room that sounds fantastic and has brilliant isolation, then have a few hundred kilograms of ceiling all come down on your head one day! :) - Stuart -
