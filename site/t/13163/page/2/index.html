xSpace20 February 2010#44
'Couple things Janne, 2"X4", visually graded #2 lumber(Southern Pine assumed) can span 9"-10" using a 24" layout but is only given a 5 PSF dead load. Since the framing member is part of the equation when trying to establish deadload abilities of lumber, you hit the framing member limit ability(with safety in mind) early. Before I built something in wall I would get the room closer to being finished. It is mentioned that diffusion with a short path, as your 8.85 feet path, is not enough room for the sound to scatter and be beneficial as you might want it to be.

xSpace13 March 2010#49
"There are some rule which says that I can put the vapour barrier in the middle of insulation layers." It might be a rule of man. Think about how you could actually do that, what would it take? If warm air is moving towards the colder air, which is a law of Nature, then this barrier that is correctly placed >behind< the interior hard boundary stops vapor at that point. In some situations, when humidity is allowed to run hot and cold, like in a vacant house, you will find that the sheetrock or whatever is the interior hard boundary was been compromised (allowed to get wet) from this accumulation of vapor that builds up and becomes moisture. Now install that same barrier in between insulation, have the same law of Nature and what happens? The vapor will accumulate on the side of the insulation that is closest to the warm side of the building and wet that insulation. Given the right climate, this insulation will hold that moisture, and have more added to it and eventually develop a mold problem. This happens inside of a wall as you described and you will not know it for a long time. It would have to have some or be a proven method, rather, as a track record. Because these things are different from one climate to another but the procedure is pretty much the same. "But Insulation layer which is facing cold outside must be at least twice as thick as a layer which is facing inside." I would need some hard data on that. This suggests that every place in Finland has a room in a room type construction...and we know that is not a certainty. "Then ceiling. I need a vapour barrier there also," I have seen only a few people do this, ever. I have never encounter this in my line of work. I will add again, that you live in a different region of the world, but I still have issue with this vapor barrier on the ceiling for the same reasons mentioned above. The vapor will hit the hard boundary and then, in your configuration, gravity will take over. Which means it goes right back into the interior boundary. Now, consider, this is a gradual process so it does not happen in a season. So if the house has ability to extract the moisture, the HVAC is doing it's job, problems may not(I have my doubts) exist, maybe ever. Maybe not.
