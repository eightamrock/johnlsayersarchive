britune31 July 2007#2
I am in an almost identical situation as you... The room above the garage. In Rod's book, he states that an elevated wood deck (which we both have) is almost impossible to soundproof. The floor of your bonus room is a giant drum head that will vibrate and resonate sound. To stop acoustic drums and low bass from passing through your floor, it would require WAY more weight in concrete or sand than most rooms can support. If you have ridge vent or soffit vents, it will be very difficult to design a "sealed" room within a room, or two leaf system. If your outer shell is vented, like mine, it is not a sealed leaf. Depending on what you have below your floor, you may need a beam to support more weight to build more in your room. In the end, it will be very difficult to have a high level of soundproofing in your room for something like acoustic drums, or a live band with amplifiers. I'm in the same boat. sharward1 August 2007#11
Don't just do something: stand there! ;-) Seriously... Don't do anything physical yet, including adding insulation, before you take your measurements. The insulation alone won't improve isolation much (if at all) and you very well may (actually, I predict, will) need to remove it in order to beef up your outer leaf. Testing: good. Planning: good. Educating thyself: good. Building before you know what you're building: bad. There may be ways to shore up the ceiling from below inside the garage if necessary: perhaps an(other) glu-lam beam (or two?) running under and perpendicular to the joists. See the "Stickhouse" thread to see a heavy beam being installed in that garage conversion project to shore up a sagging roof. I wouldn't plan it yet: just know that may be one item in a professional structural engineer's bag o' tricks to allow for a heavier build. Keep studying, my friend. Indeed, there's a lot to learn here (and elsewhere) and no shortage of people here to help you get unstuck when you need it. :-) (Actually, there is a shortage dammit! :evil: ;-)) --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward1 August 2007#14
When the outer leaf cannot be completely sealed, and isolation is a top concern, then it usually means the best compromise is a three leaf system where the inner and middle leaves are extra beefy. You need to be careful not to overload the rafters though... A structural engineer needs to be brought in to tell you what you can handle without doing any structural upgrades, as well as what kinds of measures can be taken to raise the loads. Factors taken into consideration on the rafter load include the weight of the roofing material. Is it tile (heavy) or asphault composition (light)? Typically, houses are built without a lot of extra load capacity to save on construction costs... :roll: ...Even if it's asphault comp, and there's some extra load capacity, and you spend it on the studio build, bear in mind that when it comes time to reroof, then you likely won't be able to add a layer of new shingles without stripping the old ones off. My advice is to first make sure there aren't any restrictions at the city planning or homeowners' association levels to worry about (free). Then keep studying, learn as much as you can, then design a plan that will meet your needs, regardless of weight (free). Get that concept signed off by some experts here (free, donations appreciated). Then consult a local structural engineer to produce a report, informing you what you'd need to do to make it happen safely ($hundreds). Then submit everything to your city's building department for a permit ($dozens). When permit issued, build it ($thousands). --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward3 August 2007#17
We may be in a futile exercise if you're saying you know that you cannot afford a three leaf system -- that's the first time anything budget-related has been raised. Designing a three-leaf system (the outermost leaf being unsealed) can be done, albeit not cheaply, by basically surrounding the room with multiple layers of drywall, firmly attached (no resilient channel), then framing the internal walls and ceiling within the existing space. Yes, that does cost money and square footage -- and it may not pass the building permit plan check stage due to the headroom limitations. In fact, I'll repeat, you really should consult with your city's building department to determine what restrictions you have. There are minimum headroom requirements: typically a certain height is required over a certain percentage of floorspace. Since your space is mostly A-shaped, that percentage may be very small, such that you may have to move those walls in to lower the square footage and thus raise the percentage that meets the headroom requirement. Without knowing what is legally allowed and what your budget is, we all (including you ;-)) are kind of going around in circles. :-) Legal and monetary constraints: let's get those defined. Obviously the laws of gravity trump all else, but the answers to the other two will tel us how we can deal with that one. 8) --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward6 August 2007#19
The problem you're going to run into are the breaches in the form of soffit vents, plus general lack of airtightness. A leaf that is not solid in those terms will perform really poorly. A local acoustics engineer I consulted during my design phase once relayed a story in which he was an expert witness in a civil case on the side of a drummer who built a rehearsal space inside his home. The room was very well designed and built, except for overhead, where attic vents were a critical point of isolation failure. He said he remembered it well since it was one of the very few (only perhaps?) cases he actually lost in court. That story was instrumental in scaring me into an "overkill" mode. :roll: You were, I think, wise to elect to finish the room yourself vs. having the builder do it "on the cheap." However, you still have a lot of work ahead of you. The discovery of the trusses is an interesting one. Do you have Rod's book yet? There's a section of his book on that subject you need to read and understand in order to not make a critical/catastrophic failure. FYI, spray-in insulation generally does not help with acoustics and may under some circumstances make things worse. An exception might be using spray-in closed cell foam to merge two leaves together in order to avoid a third leaf, but I don't think that applies in your case as such a third leaf would need to remain vented. --Keith :mrgreen:
