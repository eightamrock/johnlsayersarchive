knightfly14 June 2005#12
"bout damn time... :twisted: Some basics - always design for #2+better, but buy the best you can get (pick through the pile or walk away if they won't let you) - then when installing horizontal members, watch carefully where the knots are on each piece; any knots right at the edge, if they are tight and you must, put the knots UP for joists so the knot will be in COMPRESSION. Looser knots, don't use that piece for horizontals or else put the knot where it will be supported by a wall, etc - never put knots within a couple inches of the end of a board where you will have to nail into the knot - if you're lucky enough to get a nail into it without the nail bending, it will likely split the board. KD lumber doesn't cost much different than green, and it's more likely to be in the shape it will STAY in - green is still drying, and may twist a LOT before it's achieved moisture equilibrium. I've seen pieces allowed to dry un-supported that were twisted about 75 degrees in 12 feet. That's what the wood will TRY to do in your frame... :cry: The downside of KD lumber (sorry, Kiln Dried) is that it's more likely to split when nailing - stay further from edges if you can, drill pilot holes if necessary that are just slightly smaller than the nails you're using. The UP side is, it's a LOT lighter weight (plus the twisty thing) Don't EVER put sole plates in direct contact with concrete, whether they are wood or metal; I always use at least one layer of 30# building felt and sometime two, this acts as a thermal break AND moisture barrier, and I STILL use Pressure Treated wood for sole plates, period. If you MUST drive a nail thru wood within less than about an inch from the end, first place the HEAD of the nail against something heavy and metal (like a vise, or a sledge hammer) and BLUNT the point of the nail by tapping it a few times with your hammer (not a "waffle head" framing hammer, this doesn't work very well for that job) - then, when you drive the nail the blunted end "punches" its way through the wood, actually removing a bit rather than "wedging" the grain aside - the end result is you can usually drive a 16d nail through a 2x4 within 1/2" of its end without splitting. Use this trick for EVERY nail if you go with KD lumber. I know it's slow, but so are "overs"... I don't think nail guns are a very good idea for building studios, UNLESS you have one of the newer ones with an adjustable drive depth; the reason I say this - a nail gun drives the nail REALLY FAST compared to using a hammer, so the inertia of the piece being nailed in place PREVENTS the piece from being pulled up snug against what you're nailing it TO - so framing/siding fastened with a nail gun is not going to be as strong or squeak-free as if done with a hammer in most cases. Senco (maybe others) has a newer line of guns that have an adjustable drive depth; these work OK for projects, PROVIDED that you set the depth so the nail does NOT drive flush with the wood, but sticks out about 1/8" or slightly more - then, after the first couple of nails are driven into a piece, you stop and SINK them with a hammer; this pulls both the nail and the pieces you're fastening up tight against the frame, giving a stronger joint. Once the piece (a sheet of siding, for example) has been fastened this way at corners and center of each side, you can drive the REST of the nails the same way, then go back and SINK them with the hammer. If you put EVERYTHING together with construction adhesive AND nails, this isn't such a big deal - but that's a lot of construction adhesive... Frame assembly - whenever possible, assemble your frame on a flat floor and then tilt it into place. Much easier to get the studs fastened correctly to the sole plate than toenailing each and every nail from above. Make sure that the CENTERS of each stud are at multiples of 24" or 16", NOT their EDGES (except for beginning and end of a wall, of course) - that way, wall paneling will meet in the middle of the stud and both pieces can be toenailed to the stud, without needing double studs at all joints. The way to do this is to lay out caps and plates parallel to each other on sawhorses, with the side the studs will be nailed to UP toward you - match up one end of the two long cap/plate boards, then use a tape measure and mark off your centers as follows - For 16" centers, mark the first stud position 1-1/2" from the end; tape measures for carpentry are marked with both 16" and 24" centers - do NOT mark the caps/plates directly on these marks, instead ADD 3/4" to each one - at these points, draw a line across the board(s) perpendicular to the board (a framing square or saw guide helps here) - continue down the entire length of the board to the end, but place the very LAST mark 1-1/2" in from the END of the board that's the SHORTEST (hopefully they are the same, but in studio building it's not always so) Now, go back and make a big "X" just to the LEFT of each perpendicular line on each board; this is to show that the LINE is where the EDGE of the stud goes, and the "X" shows WHICH SIDE of the line the actual STUD goes. Don't forget that you'll need TWO plates, one for the bottom and one for the top; they should be laid alongside each other for marking, so everything lines up. Then, tilt up each plate so the marks are TOWARD the studs, lay out the studs and fasten each by lining up its edge mark with the body of the stud OVER THE X, and nail as seen in the pic. For 24" centers, the only change would be to use the tape's markers for 24" centers. I'll do a quick sketch in case this wasn't clear for some, then gotta go... Steve

Not sure if screws are legal for framing or not, never checked; but some other problems - standard wood screws, in order to be strong enough, need to be fat (like #10 or #12) - also, the shear point (where the two boards meet) would likely be where the threads are, so it's the SMALLEST diameter of the screw that's holding the shear force - fat screws will probably split green wood, DEFINITELY will split dry wood, the alternative for green might be DECK screws but again not sure about code or strength for shear - and finally, there's pilot holes for every fastener - :roll: ya gotta be German to wanna do anything THAT anal for that large a project :cry: On the nail blunting thing; I've also (couple of times) taken entire strips of nails for the nailer and hit each end with a bench grinder - not quite the same as the hammer flattening thing, but it works.
