knightfly7 February 2006#253
Ramset tools (powder actuated) can be dangerous if not careful as already mentioned; they are faster, they do work, but they can also "spall" your concrete (break out chunks) and it's very difficult to get just the right "load" for each situation. ONce you drive a "nail" partway into too-hard concrete, it's THERE. (There are several loads for each caliber tool - IIRC, the .22 uses yellow, red, green, maybe blue, and purple (the highest pressure one) - For speed, I'd use ramset; for accuracy/studio construction, stay with the hammer drill; you're putting in floor plates, so it's the easiest to get enough pressure on since you'll be pushing DOWN on the drill and can use body weight to advantage. Don't forget the Thermal Break under wall plates... Steve

When I 1st moved into a house in Florida I had a regular drill. It took forever to drill into a wall, I destroyed many drill bits & my 1st cordless drill. 1st Hurricane season hit and the guys in the family teamed up to put up hurricane shutters on 3 houses with plywood and tapcon screws. We started with a regular drill on my house and made way too many holes as we made our 1st mistakes around 1st window. About 1/2 way through my house we borrowed a hammer-drill. After the 1st hole was drilled I was sold. In comparison "it did go through like butter" ... but couldn't buy one that day cause all the hardware stores were sold out of hammer drills and everything else related to hurricane supplies. I advice to never try and drill through concrete with a regular drill or bits, it's painfully slow & not cost effective. BTW, I've put and taken down hurricane shutters 3 times, 2 of the times were in a real hurry as we prepared to evacuate and beat the traffic of millions of other people heading for another state. Now I got a 18V cordless hammer drill that was about $250USD at HD. When drilling into concrete, start slow or you'll miss the mark as I have many times... once you miss and drilled over the pencil mark things worsen... that goes for drilling into stucco, concrete block or anything concrete. The hammer drill can bounce off the concrete, best to start with the hammer off to get dug in. Drywall over concrete is a little easier but once the bounce starts on the concrete the size of the hole on the drywall can get big if not careful. Drilling into a 2x4 with concrete on the other side is the easiest cause the wood guides the drill bit straight into the concrete. Screwing in tapcon screws... of course I want to drive in the screws with a drill... but again got to be careful as the drill doesn't always want to get the screw in flush, it will crap out 25% of the time even on the highest torque and then the screw head can be stripped. Solution is to use a wrench or ratchet on the last part of drive, tightening by hand so you can "feel it" using body weight to not strip the head. Concrete screws & bolts come with or without a masonry drill bit. Always use the right size drill bit or the screw won’t go in or will slip out. I learned that drilling the holes around my 1st window for hurricane shutters. I've screwed so many tapcon screws I thought it worth while to post about it. Marc

Sandersd13 February 2006#274
Just thought I'd add a few thoughts: Your header over door - using a larger than necessary header to avoid cripples is a typical construction technique. Also, if the wall is only a partition wall, i.e. not load bearing, you don't need a header at all. The purpose of the header is to bridge the gap (door opening) and transfer the load from above to the foundation. No load- no structural header required, although you might want to double the top 2x4 for more nailing area for trim and drywall. :D Anchoring walls to foundation - Best = cast-in-place anchor bolts, next best = epoxy grouted anchor bolts, next best = expansion anchor bolts, next best = Tapcon bolts (no offense Marc). I've used all the above plus the powder acuated fastners (PAF's). Here's an issue I've dealt with: new concrete (< 1 year or so) is relatively easy to drill or shoot into. You could get by with a standard hammer drill or PAF's. When the concrete is old, 30 years for example, it doesn't accept the PAF's well, even with the red loads, and drilling it with a hammer drill is an exersize in futility. My recommendations: After you stand the walls, rent a rotary hammer (heavy-duty hammer drill) from HD for $35 (4 hours) and use 1/2" expansion anchors at 4' centers. Epoxy anchors are stronger when exposed to uplift, but in shear the exp anchors are more than adequate, especially in a load bearing wall. (If you want epoxy anchors HD sells them in the Simpson joist hanger section.) Definitely use a rotary hammer like Marc described (pic below). I've drilled thousands of holes with 'em and they're the only way to go. I tried using Tapcons and half the time they twisted in half and snapped before they were seated. They're great in brick and probably cinder blocks, I i've had no luck in old concrete. :evil:
