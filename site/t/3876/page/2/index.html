Dan Fitzpatrick wrote:
. . . yes there will be some bowing toward the center, but some bowing is allowed right? (L/360 is about .43 inches at maximum load)? :?
It's not that bowing isn't allowed -- I realize that some bowing is to be expected. My concern is that the expected and allowed bowing of your new upper joists will put an unknown amount of inconsistent downward pressure on some of your existing joists which you do not know for sure whether or not they can handle. If you raised your new joists up higher so that there would be no contact between upper and lower joists, that would solve that problem. However, it would create a new problem in that you would no longer have the opportunity to apply caulk to the old joists to seal the gaps around the new gypsum panels. It's a "Catch 22" situation.
as long as the weight of the old joists is included as dead weight in my calculation it should be more than ok (since the old joists can easily hold just their own weight)
Again, it's not the dead load of the new joists that I'm concerned about. I realize you can account for the weight of the old joists upon the new ones. My concern is the weight pulling the new joists down and putting pressure on your original joists. Hopefully that's more clear. If not, I'm sure I can find another horse to beat. ;)

So what am i thinking TODAY. as if anyone cares; i find that spewing my thoughts in a public forum helps me focus (sorry!) ... :P I've been giving a lot of thought to my slab, or slabs i should say, because they seem to be a limiting factor now to my design. there are two slabs that make up my planned studio floor. Rod's warnings and other info I have gathered seem to point to 50psf as the "safe" weight to allow for a distributed load. On the other hand it's pretty obvious that a point load of 1000 pounds plus is also allowed, because that's what a car tire does. I'm worried that if i put a wall that weighs 75 lbs per linear foot on the edge of one of these slabs, like i would want to, that it exceeds the allowed weight of the slab and the slab will crack. On the other hand, I have no problem driving my car up onto the edge of the slab in the exact same spot and putting 2000 pounds or so on it. So it seems like it would be ok to do the wall. Floating designs I have examined using a lightweight wood floor can get me down to 150 pounds per puck and if i put that on a 12" by 12" wood block that seems like it should count as 150 psf. that's still high by "official" standards but then i come back to my car tire that is 1000 pounds in less space. my room if i float will be 10000 pounds including 3000 pounds live load, using a multilayer wood and gypsum floor. here's the thing. i really don't want to make a major engineering project out of this, i want to get this thing going. here's what i'm thinking. forget floating, because the expense is too great versus the risk of major problems with slab cracking and settling, and there's no certainty that i need to float. build the first 75 lbs/linear foot wall along my slab edge and see what happens. if it cracks, maybe i'll have to disassemble the wall and demo the slab etc. but i won't be out too much. the expense vs. risk is not so much. but i don't think the risk is so much. if i can park 2000 pounds of car there (front wheels), i should be able to put 700 pounds of wall across roughly the same line. maybe after i've got a sense of how the slab is holding up, (was thinking i could measure the height of the slab before and after building the wall to see if the slab has flexed at all) maybe i'll reconsider floating the inner room. i think my slab will survive. if not, lesson learned the hard way. it's a risk, but i think a reasonable one. tearing out the slab and repouring it seems just too daunting. and perhaps unnecessary. there is risk too in doing something that is not needed isn't there? maybe the risk that the project doesn't get done AT ALL. the question now is, do i do 3 layers of 5/8 gypsum or 4 ... 3 will be less heavy on the floor, and realistically do i think my door and ventilation system will "keep up" with a 4 layer wall. maybe. one thing i realized today. 60 duro rubber is not going to work at all, even though i found a cheap source of 1 and 2" 60 duro neoprene in oakland, i'd have to put 300 pounds per puck or more on that to get the deflection i need for good iso. 130 bucks for 3 square feet of 2" neoprene not bad ... left the name at the office but i'll provide later. 50 or so dollars for the 1" ... at least i think that was neoprene, it may have been rubber. 300 pounds per puck ... can my floor handle that? aaaarghhh. maybe it can. but using the softer KIP pads that can be used at 150 lbs/puck is probably a safer bet if i do decide to float. unfortunately the KIP pad route is more like $400+ versus the $130 for the rubber. maybe i just need to find out how soft a rubber i need for about 20 psi to give me decent deflection. certainly 60 duro is way too hard. and $270 is cheap insurance and relatively small expense compared to the whole project. so why worry about it. sorry to ramble, but maybe someone else will get something out of this, or make a suggestion (other than, SHUT UP) :twisted: anybody have any slab-cracking experiences they want to share? Xalky22 June 2005#49
Heres a very educated guess without seeing your slab. I've been building houses and running construction jobs since I've been old enough to pick up a hammer, and I have an mechanical engineering degree.( i telling you this to qualify my statement as I'm new here.) I think you're intuition is dead on about the weight of a car versus the weight of the wall. The car is exerting about 1000 psf, all in one spot (8x8" area). The load of the wall won't be nearly that much because the load is distributed over the width of the plate times the length of the wall. If you're paranoid about this you can distribute the load more by simply using a wider plate ie: 2x6 instead of 2x4. You can still however, use 2x4s for studs, just put the studs flush to the plate on the finished side of the wall. I don't think this is necessary however.
