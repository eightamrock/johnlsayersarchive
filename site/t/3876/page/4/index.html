There is an interesting discussion here from Paul Woodlock's project. the strategy discussed in this thread is to try to make the MAM resonace as HIGH as possible (usually we try to get it LOW) so that it will be in a frequency that is VERY well isolated by the first two leaves. ALSO: the cool thing about this is that below the MAM frequency two leaves follow mass law, essentially they become one leaf, so the garage door's extra leaf has no effect. if you follow this strategy then you do not want to add much mass to the garage door since that will lower fMAM and also insulation will lower fMAM. you want to dampen the door of course somehow but try to do it without lowering fMAM too much. the strategy of totally integrating the garage door into the outer leaf with zero airgap is the best one from a theoretical point of view, but from a practical point of view it may prove to be a huge pain. Xalky20 July 2005#83
Without hinges and hardware in place the door sections should fit together rather tightly. First bottom panel:Caulk along the floor. big fat beeds to completely fill the gap. Calk along doorstop frame, and screw directly to door opening Frame. Second panel: caulk top of first panel, fat bead or two, to completely fill the gap, caullk again against door frame and screw the panel directly to the frame. Repeat for remaining panels. It'll be no weaker than your corner joints will be on your inner leaf. The key is caulking all the joints Really well. You'll probably need to be 3 guys to lift the panels in place, being careful that the middle of each panel is well supported. I'd use concrete mix, it's cheap and stiff. Once its setup, 3 days or so, those panels will be extremely difficult to break. You might have to beef up your door stops on the outside of the door to provide a good seal. Your inner leaf seal is far more critical than the outer leaf. I think the advantages of doing it this way outweigh the cosequences of a three leaf system. I really think. This would be extremely effective, coupled with the between studs drywall. You could also wrap each stud with strips of drywall to beef up the stud and plate areas. It's a small area... so it shouldn't add much labo in lieu of the extra wall you'd need otherwise. These are just the thoughts of a lowly construction guy, and I could be way off base. Maybe Steve can chime in here. Marcel

I've got a lot to learn about construction, but I'm wondering about the "air permeable paint." That may very well be the way to do it. However, if you're not going to have any other barriers, then maybe plastic sheeting is the way to go. That would protect your drywall from the moisture that comes in through the stucco -- which I think is your greatest concern, since it will have hard contact with the stucco (or paint) and wicking could occur otherwise... And if any airborne moisture (vapor) comes from the room side through the drywall towards the plastic, it can leech back into the room over time. I would think a dehumidifier and a bulletproof A/C solution will be your ticket to reducing the amount of moisture going into the drywall from the room side. And I think Thermafiber SAFB and/or Roxul AFB have awesome water retention characteristics (much better than ordinary fiberglass), which would probably prevent vapor originating in the room from even reaching your outer leaf. Someone with more construction experience, please comment. :)
