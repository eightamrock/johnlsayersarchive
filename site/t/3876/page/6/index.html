knightfly30 August 2005#130
Couple of things - how did you get your new building paper to be continuous without removing the studs, or did you just seal as well as possible at each stud? Also, (no lab tests on this so I may be full of the proverbial, but doubt it) typical framing lumber is approx. 2/3 the density of gypsum - so, when doing the "insert" thang, there will be a point of diminishing return caused by TWO things - one, when the total thickness of gypsum (or similar density mass) reaches approximately 2/3 the depth of your studs, the studs will become the weak point in the mass leaf. Two - as you add gypsum layers INSIDE the frame, you decrease the effective air gap which will RAISE the m-a-m resonance, causing a worsening of LF TL (assuming you don't move the inner frame further away from the original frame to compensate) Yet another thing that concerns me with tight/heavy construction - moisture. I'm sure you've read my comments to Keith about ventilating the space between his inner and outer walls, and why I think this is important - so much so that this is one of the reasons (apart from 7 hZ m-a-m resonance) that I'm planning on at least 24" of space between innerframe and outer concrete shell as I design my new facility - this "moat" will be open to the inside, conditioned/dehumidified space except when in use, and will have constant power ventilation (again, except when in use.) If necessary I'll be able to "tune" the inner walls with more or less pressure on insulation, AFTER it's all built, because there will be enough space to WALK between the leaves. I've seen 'way too many failures in newer homes, most of which are moisture related - the main reasons for this are (1) homes today are required to be VASTLY more energy efficient than they were 100 years ago (or even 10 for that matter) (2) Builders are in much more of a hurry so things are built wet, not fully allowed to dry out, and in a lot of cases moisture is trapped by improper placement/quantity of vapor/moisture/air barriers due to lack of understanding. The old houses are still standing because the LEAK AIR LIKE A SIEVE... This is another area where it takes quite a bit of study to gain an appreciation of the REAL ways of doing things - in some cases the CODES are responsible for wrong construction, so WTF can you do? From my perspective, I would bet that 100 years from NOW there will be more of the homes that are currently ALREADY 100 years old, still standing than homes that are built today. Kinda got a ramble goin' on there, sorry... Steve

Dan Fitzpatrick6 September 2005#141
Pretty decent progress this weekend. I began by sanding down some of the rough parts of the mud layer, and doing a little fill-in on some low areas. But I wasn't too into it, i was just going for the obvious bad spots. Unfortunately that took a long time and wore me out too. I did some acoustic caulking between the paper and the mud too, even though i was not sure it was worth doing. Anyway that pretty much took up my alotted time on Saturday, so I was depressed at having made no visible progress. So I vowed to do better on Sunday. I had been concerned about the next step, which was adding the new layer of building paper on top of the mud layer, and then folding down the original building paper i had cut open. How to make this stuff stick down? I had thought to use spray mount, but that turned out to be a stupid idea. Spray mount might have worked in large quantities, but it isn't the right stuff for such heavy paper. too thin. It would have cost a fortune, and may have screwed up my vapor permeability. So then I decided to try liquid nails, just using a very small amount. That worked great. While it may not be very permeable (who knows) i didn't use that much so the vapor characteristics of my wall system should be ok. Also I decided to do just one layer of building paper. Especially since there is old building paper on top of this, it seemed overkill and also with the extra layers of drywall the wall is going to be pretty thick for vapor to escape through. I set up a sheet of plywood on some sawhorses and started cutting up the building paper, using a drywall square and marking my lines with bits of drywall (since the paper is black, it's hard to see pencil or sharpie etc). I glued on the paper to the mud with liquid nails. Then i acoustic caulked between the mud layer/new paper and the old paper. Then I glued down the flaps of the old paper that had been cut open. Actually i had stapled these open, so i had to spend a bunch of time removing these staples. what a pain that was. Then i acoustic caulked between the paper and the studs. as i went on i decided not to caulk between the mud/new paper and the old paper on a couple rows, it seemed overkill. but maybe i should have. ideally i guess you'd caulk between the mud/new paper and the old paper, and then the old paper and the stud. between the new paper and the old paper, more for watertightness than airtightness. i should probably do this on the remaining sections. i got lazy i guess :lol: then i used some roofing patch between the paper and the diagonal cross brace, because this is a good place for water intrusion since there's no way to elegantly cut this open and get it back with any semblance of integrity. man that stuff stinks for days. maybe i will just use acoustic caulk on this too. it made me a little sick gave me a bit of nausea that i didn't appreciate. maybe the acoustic caulk will be adequate for my purpose since it is not exposed to the elements. anyway it was a bit of work because of the staple removal, but it's buttoning up very nicely and now that all the staples are removed the rest of it should be done in short order. i also cut up my fingers a bit on broken staples and splinters as i smoothed down the paper and caulked. :evil: i really want to keep using that roofing patch (GE Roof Cement/Asphalt Sealant) because it seems like the right way to tie together different pieces of building paper. but man the smell will infiltrate almost the whole house. :evil: :evil: Stick had mentioned in his thread that the Miracle acoustic caulk had shrunk. Mine did as well, leaving a few cracks and pinholes in very thick beads. Not something to get too upset about, but something to keep an eye on. it's easy enough to fill in a few trouble areas, and after doing multiple layers it should be a non issue. On the plus side, it doesn't stink to high heaven, and it cleans up pretty easy with water. It's pretty pleasant to work with. I didn't use any of my brand new 1000 feet of 1/4 inch backer rod though, that will wait for my official first layer of gypsum. It's going to be tricky using 1/4 inch i bet, because i'll have to get those gypsum pieces measured jussssst right. but i'm pretty sure on 1/2 inch gypsum, it's the right size. i'll be sure to report my experience in excruciating detail when the time comes. speaking of materials acquisition, i lucked into a bunch of good stuff from one of our members who is moving across country. he had advertised here about some 703, which i bought from him, but then i discovered he also had 8 1/2 bales of Roxul AFB, which i also bought, and a nearly full case of USG acoustic caulk. i think we were both pretty happy with the deal. overall a good week, and i'm especially glad that the putting back of the building paper plan went pretty smoothly ... that had been worrying me, but no more! the next problem will be dealing with my electric meter box and it's conduit and a gas pipe that is in the way of my beef up. still not sure what to do there.
