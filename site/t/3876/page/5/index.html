jal, if i get your meaning that would make 4 leaves ... you want 2 leaves. so the key is to eliminate that gap, by filling it with something solid and airtight. hanging a limp membrane between leaves is something i've seen tossed around but to my knowledge there is no data to support this practice. in a standard 2 leaf wall it should actually make things worse, by cutting the airgap in half. you want one airgap, as wide as you can afford. dan

well it took me longer than i thought to try out the mortar instead of the stucco. work has suddenly become super busy with 12 hour days and weekend work etc. so no mortar this week. until today :) had maybe 90 minutes to plunge into the mortar scene. it was stucco like, but smoother. i was hoping to be able to say, "smooth like butta," but i guess i haven't quite figured out the proper mix proportions. but it wasn't bad. my tool of choice this time was a yellow plastic 8" taping knife from home depot. its flexibility allowed me to use it nearly flat against the mortar and yet with enough angle that the mortar didn't try to stick to the blade. it was easy to make the surface smooth. it was easy to see the unevenness. i could carve away the occasional bump, and fill in the random indentations. i came away confident in the flatness. ah, sweet flatness. my favorite attribute in an outer leaf interior surface. i found it good to work patiently, wet into wet, not skipping around to fill in odd sections but slowly lay down a new layer from bottom to top. wetter is better. it is good to spray a mist of water on areas that are done, but don't seem to have that glistening smoothness. then you go over them gently with the yellow blade. a glistening smooth surface can be made. before i forget: good idea to put plastic down on the bottom plate and the floor or whatever you might, actually WILL, drop mortar (and stucco) on. because you will be dropping a lot of mortar. but you will get the hang of it. wear a mask while mixing mortar or stucco, a good idea. can be bad for the lungs. IS bad for the lungs. have a hose handy with an attachment that can be turned off or can spray the finest of mists at a moment's notice. still have a way to go. now have only finished one 16" section. but it should get easier and faster. next time, i'll try using mortar only (the first section was stucco first, then mortar. i didn't like the stucco, too chunky). below are a couple of pix. one, shows the mix that i thought was pretty good. you can see how wet it is. the other, shows the sweet, sweet smoothness of the wall. dan

The past two weeks have been busy, but not with studio-building unfortunately. I've been busy helping out with the launch of our company's new magazine, Scrapbook Answers. I know all of you are closet scrapbookers, so go buy a copy when it comes out in 2 weeks! The past couple of weeks have put me in a gloomy mood studio-wise. Having no time to work on the studio makes it seem like it's going to take a million times longer. Go figure! So I've been whining to mikeklooster about how hard it is to smooth out bumpy stucco with mortar. I began to suspect that some of that whining and moaning was due to the fact I hadn't had any time to work. So I found a little window of time to "speed-mortar" a 16" section of my wall. I needed to get something done, to start out the week with some hope of getting some work done before the baby comes (did i mention my wife's having a baby any minute now? THAT will not help the studio progress AT ALL :P ) So I looked at the clock, changed into ratty clothes, turned on the radio, ran the hose under the garage door and started mixin' some mortar. I made it really nice and wet and gooey. If you make it too wet, that's just about wet enough. I went fast. I didn't worry too much about how much was going on the floor, just about the speed. And it DID go quickly, to my surprise. This was my third session at this after all and i WAS getting better! Ah, the steepness of the initial learning curve! Will I ever get over your allure? My strategy this time was to go from bottom to top, as usual, but to be a little sloppy. To go fast, before my wet-down of the old stucco dried too much (although it did anyway, and then i sprayed too much water on). So i got to the top pretty quickly, with a pretty thick buttering, but not smooth. I did this with the trusty 8" yellow drywall knife. Rather than just use the rather weak handle to press the mortar on, i used my other hand against the flat of the blade for extra leverage. This worked well. Then I stepped back and started in again at the bottom with my 7-inch trowel, smoothing out the bumps and looking for holes that needed filling. Using mostly the tip of the trowel, which is rounded. This was a much slower process but somehow satisfying and engrossing. It was a pretty thick layer. I was a bit worried, since it was so thick and also quite wet, that a big chunk might just peel off and land on my head while i was squatting down at the bottom. But there was no sign of movement. So. The final time, after clean up and everything, to get a surface that was pretty darn flat in one 16" x 7 foot section? Forty-five minutes. That's it. Really not very long at all. AND: The first section i did i had to do in two sessions, because i had used stucco and it turned out too rough, and i didn't know what i was doing. This section i did the entire thing, in one session, with mortar, in 45 minutes. and it is just as smooth as the other section (pretty much). So how thick is it? I was trying to lay it on a little thick, just because i thought it would be easier to get smooth. But mainly i was just trying to get it smooth. After i finished i poked it with a nail in a few different places, not super thoroughly but enough, and the depth range of the mortar was 1/16 inch to about 5/16 inch. and this was THICK in my opinion. remember i felt like it was about to peel off and land on my head. so, if anyone thinks they can trowel on a half inch or an inch of mortar or anything else on a vertical surface, i wish them good luck. it just doesn't seem possible. to get the amount of fluidity you need to trowel something on, this same property of fluidity will mean the stuff will just flow down the wall. i think. so to add let's say an inch of stucco/mortar what have you, i think you would really need to do this in about 4 different sessions. at least 3. but i could be wrong. maybe next time, rather than going for speed, i'll go for thickness and see how thickly i can put it on. pix below. the browner colored one is without flash, lit by a halogen lamp, while the other grey ones use the camera's flash. the camera flash seems to "flatten out" the texture so i tried it without. the top one is the finished surface. dan

Dan Fitzpatrick22 August 2005#121
it's hard to say how vapor permeable merkrete is. i couldn't find the data. the reason i wonder is that for example Tyvek is waterproof too more or less but is VERY vapor permeable. so i think they are different things. the main worry about stucco (i think) is that it will crack, and water will get in. stucco is vapor permeable, which is a good thing in a mixed climate, but if it cracks then you maybe have problems, so you need a backup barrier. not a barrier but a retarder, actually. in northern california you want the whole wall to be fairly vapor permeable both ways, but waterproof to the exterior. as far as i understand.

Dan Fitzpatrick29 August 2005#124
This weekend was "back with a vengeance" weekend as I hadn't been able to spend much time on studio building for the past few weeks. Today my wife even took the kids away for the entire day, and I almost gave myself heatstroke trying to make the most of it. On Saturday I finished mortaring all of the lumpy stucco sections. That didn't take long, a few hours. But I only had a relatively small amount to do in the first place, thankfully, only a section of wall about 12 feet long. Thankfully, the rest of the existin structure that I'll be using is wood siding, which is flat. Also on Saturday I began mudding the finished mortared sections, to create an even flatter, sandable surface. I had done two sections earlier, and these were dry. I wetted the mortar before applying the mud, and it went on nicely. Other than the fact that there was a diagonal brace running through my wall complicating matters :evil: the plaster went on easy and pretty flat. Today I finished plastering (mudding) all of the mortared sections, with the exeption of the meter box section, which i had forgotten to do and i mortared the bottom section of that today as well. The top will be a bear to figure out and I haven't yet. Unfortunately I forgot to wet the mortar beforehand today and so I had a hell of a time applying the mud, it was much more difficult. I couldn't figure out why so I kept adding water to thin the mix. Only after I finished the whole job did I remember I should have wet the surface. :oops: I did a little preliminary sanding and the surface is pretty smooth, although not perfect. Maybe i will spot fill some areas during the week, but we will see if i can be bothered. At some point enough is enough. It's pretty smooth. One problem I did have since I only waited less than 24 hours was that some of the mortar did crumble and get into my mud, which was very aggravating. I couldn't wait as I had to get this done today, but I recommend waiting longer ... also maybe try to knock off any loose bits you see before mudding. Much of the rest of the day was spent going to the dump and picking up materials. I finally managed to get the 13 bags of drywall to the dump. That was quite a chore, as some of the bags were so heavy I had trouble lifting them into the van (granted, I was a bit tired from all that mortaring and mudding :wink: ). Then I went to Home Depot and picked up enough OSB and drywall to get the wall finished (well, closer anyway :) ) yes it was a little weird taking all that drywall to the dump and then, on the way home from the dump, stopping at HD to buy more drywall. i have to keep reminding myself, "yes i AM sane, dammit." I've been having the hardest time trying to figure a plan on how to attach the multiple layers of drywall to my wall. holding them in place with cleats didn't seem practical because each layer needs to be caulked so you need to cleat each layer seperately. that just seemed to not be a good way to go. although it would work. but it occured to me that when you cleat a layer into place, where do you caulk? you'd have to caulk between the drywall and the cleat, and then also between the cleat and the stud. twice as much caulk/caulking. unelss you made cleats that were only short sections, so some of the drywall/stud corners were accessible, but this gets so complicated with multiple layers i couldn't make it work. also what if you had so many layers that you ran out of room on your studs to attach cleats. you probably need about an inch for a cleat, so that leaves you 1.5 inches for drywall. which may not be enough. so my plan now is to attach with cleats a layer of 7/16 OSB to my smooth surface and then screw subsequent layers to that. 7/16 doesn't give you a lot of depth for the screw to grip, but it is adequate and then if you are worried about strength you can rig up some kind of cleat or something at the end. unfortunately i had a problem. i had planned to use all 1/2 inch drywall because i'm just one person and i can fling 1/2 inch around easier than 5/8. but, the shortest drywall screws are 1 inch. after a quick test on the actual material, it was determined that 1 inch screws come out the other side of the OSB ... just a little, but i wasn't taking any chances after spending all that time smoothing my walls. the screws might go into my layer of mud but then again the wood was dimpling out and i think it would create gaps. also the screw could run into a bare patch of mortar. no good. :cry: so back to HD for some 5/8 drywall. i was beat, i wasn't happy, but i had the van with the seats out and now was the time to do it. so now i've got 1" screws into 5/8 drywall into 7/16 OSB, which works fine, and i've got 1.5 inch screws for the second layer of 1/2 inch drywall on top of that, whihc also works fine. screws come in 1/2 inch increments so i should be able to add layers to my heart's content without trouble. :wink: i'm still not sure how many layers i'll do. maybe 1 layer 5/8 and 2 layers 1/2. that will give me a 5/8 stucco, 7/16 osb, 5/8 drywall, and 2 layers 1/2 inch drywall. that should be good. but i can always add another layer of 1/2 inch drywall. and adding that extra 1/2 inch will be about the easiest part of building this wall. BY FAR. another problem i ran into was with the backing rod. at my HD it came in 3/8, which seemed about right width wise for a 1/4 inch crack BUT i've suddenly realized that that will leave AT MOST 1/8 inch for caulk and probably less, since it will bulge out when compressed in the crack. will this be enough of a groove for the caulk? even the backing rod package says to leave 1/4 inch groove. another reason to use 5/8 inch gypsum i guess. well, i guess i'll find out soon enough. at least i can use the backing rod i bought in the first layer. then i can figure out if it will work in the thinner layers. i was having a bit of difficulty deciding how to attach my new layers of asphalt paper to the mud layer, and get the old paper to lay down and stick to my new paper. first i thought about using wallpaper paste, which i guessed would keep things vapor permeable. but apparently such thing may no longer exist, only a thing called wallpaper "activator." no matter. i was thinking about buying a gallon of elmers glue, again thinking this is water based, it might be ok. and it is cheap enough to use liberally. then again it might be too runny, i have my doubts about it being able to hold that heavy paper where i want it. but then i saw 3M Super 77 spray adhesive, which i've used before in my work. This seemed like it might work for my purpose. The plan is to use very little, just enough to get the paper to stick in place until the OSB goes up and holds it in place. if i use very little the permeablility of the system shouldn't be affected too much. you can use very little but it's very sticky. Anyway that's about it except i did buy one of those things that automatically disengages the drill when the screw is the proper depth. that should speed things up. and don't laugh, but i bought a hard hat. i bumped my head a few too many times on my garage door track this weekend, and i'm sure it will come in handy as this project continues. :D
