Dan Fitzpatrick12 March 2007#429
wow, i just had the best day of BEEFING UP! and i never thought i'd say that. I HATE WORKING WITH DRYWALL. or at least i did until today. it's all because i discovered a better way of cutting drywall. i have really been hating working with drywall. the method of using the knife and breaking it seemed to be such a pain, at least the way i was doing it. a couple of weeks ago i did a few pieces using a hand jigsaw, or whatever you call it. kind of easier, but certainly not quicker and a lot messier. that got me thinking about putting the question out there on the forums: any tips on cutting drywall? then i got an even better idea (duh), why not search google and find out how the real pros do it? i found this link that made all the difference. particularly the part about using the tape measure as a makeshift rip fence. it sounded hard, but it was actually pretty easy. here's the link reading this led me to change my cutting method to one which is at least 3 or 4 times faster than before. my OLD METHOD of ripping a sheet of drywall went like this: uncap the pen, find the drywall square, measure across to the measurement i want to cut at least 4 times with tape measure, use the drywall square to draw a line with the pen, 4 feet a time (square is 4 feet long). then use the knife to follow the pen line, maybe do it twice and press as hard as possible. after breaking the core of the drywall, make sure to cut entire backside of the fold, hopefully not dropping either piece in the process. Total time: not sure, but maybe 3 minutes. or longer ... too long whatever it was. and a total pain. NEW METHOD: grab tape measure, knife, find measurement on tape measure, cut length of drywall in one motion (NO PEN NEEDED). as long as the paper is cut don't worry about how deep it is. break core, cut backside about 3 feet, or whatever i can reach from the center of the sheet. then try to snap the rest of the paper in one quick motion, sometimes failing that and having to kick the sheet at the seam a few times. DONE. total time, MAYBE a minute. probably more like 45 seconds. How's the accuracy? Actually, after a couple of cuts I was cutting more accurately with this method than the old. basically no difference, except WAY faster. one key to this also is i realized i don't have to use a pen at all, for any cuts. if i have to cut a bit off the end of a ripped piece for example, i just mark my measurement with a quick stab of the knife blade. so without the pen i've cut the need to switch tools (the pen being a tool) and minimized my tools. the cap of the pen was the worst part, what a headache. yes i know, why was i using a pen with a cap. but anyway. i would use the drywall square for these cross cuts. yes the huge one, but it's light so no big deal. also following this same logic, i had to cut six pieces of a board on my chopsaw to custom length. again, not using the pen, i just pulled my tape measure from the edge of the board, to the cut area, brought the blade down on the tape measure (not on, of course) until i had the right measurement, and chopped. eliminating the pen here saved me huge time as well. by the way, i should say i was working with 1/2 inch today. it might be a little different with the type X (with the fibers) but i doubt much different). i know this will be a big help to people who aren't experienced with drywall. as much work as we do with this stuff, we should talk more about how we work with it, because each trick can save huge time over the course of a project.

Alright! Have I got the tip for you today. I shouldn't even be giving this stuff away, I could be making money off these tips. :lol: So, not too long ago somebody (Guitardad maybe) was complaining about how hard USG acoustical sealant was to squeeze out. And i had to agree. I had recently used up the last of my Miracle sealant, which is much less dense and much easier to squeeze out. Although you want your caulk to be more dense, so that's why when I got some more, I made sure to get USG. Man my hand was killing me today. And also I have a sprained wrist that is taking forever to heal (I did it over new years weekend) and that was making it worse. So i had an idea. Years ago I worked as a welder, and there used to be an oven where the welding rod or whatever you call it was kept warm. People used to stick their hot lunches in there too. anyway, that gave me the idea to put my caulk in my oven at about 110 degrees. I knew it would be ok since that's no hotter than it might get in the back of a truck on a hot summer day in Sharward country. It worked a treat as they say across the pond ... after about half an hour it came out as easy as Miracle. Speeded up my work a ton too. I heartily recommend it. Only make sure to put a sign on the oven so nobody turns it up to preheat for dinner! initially i had the idea to put the tubes in hot water, but i quickly realized that the cardboard tubes would get soggy and the whole tube would probably burst apart as soon as i tried to squeeze the caulk. so don't do that. that would be bad. enjoy :twisted:

Dan Fitzpatrick18 April 2007#443
No you have a BIG mistake here. The gypsum layers go right up against the OSB. Yes, that means that you have to rip 14 inch (or whatever it is) pieces of drywall and put them BETWEEN the studs in the studwall. Yes this is a pain. I assumed you already know the basics of this, let me give you the 25 cent version. When I describe the layers as above, I mean that all the metal, XPS, OSB and gypsum layers are all touching each other with hopefully no airgap. This is the outer "leaf" of your construction. The second, or inner "leaf" will be a SEPARATE STUD WALL built inside this outer leaf, 8-12 inches away from the outer leaf and not touching the outer leaf in any way unless you count the connection through the slab floor. If you do it in the way you describe, it will not be soundproof enough. Your only hope of getting the soundproofing to the level you need to have a band playing is to have a true room within a room. Yes this means a separate inner ceiling on the top of your separate inner walls! Don't bother with different thicknesses of drywall, use 5/8 for everything. There was a particular reason i used 1/2 inch on the first layer, which is a long story but i don't believe in the idea of using different thicknesses of drywall in general. You are ok using standard insulation, just use enough to fill up the whole space between the leaves, and make sure any insulation backing is against the leaves, not suspended in between the leaves. I haven't begun building my inner wall yet. When I talk about checking the soundproofness of my outer leaf, it is only to scope out possible weak points in the outer leaf. It certainly is not soundproof enough on its own. Sorry for the confusion. Right now, although my outer leaf is fairly decent, I can clearly hear my neighbor's 57 chevy (loud, bassy engine noise) through the wall (although it is much louder in my house). But once i get my inner leaf done, which will be 3 layers of 5/8, i should not be able to hear it at all.

Dan Fitzpatrick20 April 2007#447
Bootsy, that is pretty much on the right track. One major correction i would make is do not "stuff" the gap with insulation, pack it in full but maybe slightly like 1 inch over full but not "tight" because conceivably insulation that is tight enough, especially something dense like rockwool could transmit some energy to the other side of your wall. so be carefull not to stuff too tightly, ask yourself, is what i am doing going to cause a link between the leaves. some people go so far as to say you should leave a gap of pure air in the middle of your leaves, but i think this is going a bit overboard. also, my osb that is against hte garage door is held up by a 2x4 stud wall with studs 16" OC, actually three separate shorter stud walls because i couldn't lift one whole one by myself. so my "beef up" panels of gypsum are around 8 1/2 feet long and 14 inches wide. yes, that 1/2 of a foot extra was annoying but oh well. as far as not doing a double ceiling, this idea has potential if you really can't afford the ceiling height. going straight up you do have two masses, the ceilign and then the roof. also sound that goes up won't be heard as much by the neighbors. but, you will have a direct link between your band room out to the front and sides of the garage, at the upper part of the wall where the roof and walls meet. so you could get a lot of sound transmission (aka flanking transmission) through that route. flanking isn't as much of a problem through slab floors but along the ceiling joists of your garage ceiling it could be bad. just as an example, a few months back i was testing the solidity of my outer leaf by hitting it with the side of my fist. i immediately heard a rattle from the other side of the garage, which was an old sheet metal pepsi thermometer rattling against the wall where it hung. i then hit the wall much more gently, to see how gently i could hit it and still make the rattle. it was incredible how strong the link was. the thermometer was diagonally across the garage, at least 20 feet away. but the small vibration was going through the structure of the garage and rattling the thermometer. so the lesson is, vibration and therefore sound, travels very easily through a wood structure. it's impossible for me to predict how well this would work. it could work great, or terrible. so, the best thing to do is stick with what you know will work, which is to have two completely separate walls and ceilings. personally i would rather have a very soundproof room with a 7 foot ceiling than a not-very-soundproof room with an 8 foot ceiling.
