The plans are mostly the same as originally posted with a few modifications. I have gone with shimming the walls along the long edge to cope with the slanted flooring like this... All other concerns (dealing with the water pipe/ground wire, ceiling joist load, etc.) have been alleviated. :) Now on to new concerns. :( So far, I have only started removing drywall to expose what new obstacles and headaches are lurking within the walls. I have been carefully removing it by pulling the nails/screws out and trying to salvage most of it to cut up and replace between stud cavities or use as scrap on silencer boxes etc. Sure enough, nails. Lots of em. Along the vertical length of almost half of the studs, there is a series of nails coming through the OSB about a full inch. Very close to the studs where this occurs, mostly within 1 inch of the stud. It looks like there were meant to go into the stud, and just missed all the way up. Much fewer in number, and very close to or coming part way through the studs are longer heavier gauge nails, I assume for the OSB under the siding. Here is a side view of a row of nails showing both sizes. This row is very close up against the stud, other places they are farther out. After doing a search around the forum, it appears the consensus solution is not to grind them off flush, but to push closed cell foam over them to depth as the first layer of added beef-up between the studs. Most of the scenarios to which this solution was applied had very random placed nails all over the cavity space between the studs. Since my nails are all in a fairly neat row up close to a stud, I wonder if this solution would work without eating up more of my air gap than necessary..... Instead of adding a full layer of foam, adding just a strip of it the depth of two layers of drywall about 2 inch's across all the way up the side of the stud to cover the nails. Then adding drywall to continue the layer across the stud bay. Then adding drywall over the foam and drywall layers. Or am I better off doing a full layer of foam all the way across the stud cavity? Anyone have any thoughts? Another problem I encountered while removing drywall along the shared wall with the laundry room was OSB right under the drywall. I expected to just find fluffy insulation leading to the inner drywall. So I've got another careful removal process to deal with before I can get to and remove the insulation and beef up that wall. Any questions, suggestions, comments, criticisms, or feedback would be appreciated. Thnx

Every one involved in building should own a pair of these: http://chicktool.com/tools/nippers/nippers.gif (the tool on the right hand side) Nip off the nails that are up against the stud if they are creating a problem for you. Thanks for the thoughts X A couple questions, I had originally thought about clipping or grinding the nails off flush with the OSB, but thought I should check around and came across this thread. Most of these nails in my wall appear to be holding the siding up on the outside. If I shorten them, won't I be messing with the shearing forces for the siding? Also, if it was one or two nails, I might be more inclined to just clip/grind with little worries, but since I have long rows of nails together, wouldn't I be (in theory) weakening an entire segment of my siding attachment? Shimming..... thankfully I have a built in angle template. The footing under the garage walls (not sure what else to call them) is level. The garage floor slopes away from the house along the length of my room. This method (or the drawing of) received the thumbs up from the gentleman I talked to at my local building office. 1- Put down my floor plate for the new walls (yes, pressure treated) at its proper depth to the existing back wall, but pushed close to the footing along the side wall. 2- Mark where I want to place the shims on the new foot plate. 3- Place the board to be used for the shims (2x4 or similar) in position vertically on the new foot plate. 4- Then using something flat and rigid placed on the foot plate of the existing wall (which is level), extending outward against the side of the vertical shim piece, mark the angle. 5- Then cut off the angle with a miter saw and label the newly cut shim's position. 6- Repeat as necessary. 7- Then attach all the shim pieces and add a top plate, so I would essentially have a short "shim wall" for the length needed, where the foot plate follows the slope of the garage and the top plate remains level. 8- Then I would be able to build the rest of the framing normally on the ground so its nice and plumb and attach my "shim wall" to it, then raise the whole thing into place. Foot plate should match the slope, and the vertical studs and stop plate should be plumb. *key word in all of that being SHOULD :| That is the plan anyway. Its going to be a bit tedious, but if I make a mistake I'm only out a few inches of lumber rather than several feet. When it comes to the door framing, I'm planning to rip a piece lengthwise as you suggest so I have solid wood under the door instead of spaces.
