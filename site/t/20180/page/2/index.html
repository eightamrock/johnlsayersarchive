I'm not a big fan of Newell, to be honest. Some of his rooms look and sound great, but there's also a lot of stuff he says that just doesn't "click" with acoustic reality. When someone says things like that which are far away from what the world's HVAC experts say, you do have to wonder: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_changes_per_hour http://isites.harvard.edu/fs/docs/icb.t ... 1_2004.pdf The second one is pretty much the Bible on this subject: it includes all the data, definitions and equations you could ever hope for, in great detail, right down to the rate that your body consumes oxygen depending on your diet (you need 30% more if you eat only carbohydrates, as compared to only fat). If you go through all the procedure in there, you'll get a very accurate estimate of what you need to do. This is mush easier to understand: http://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/air-c ... d_867.html Long story short: There are actually TWO issues here: one is how much air you need to circulate through the rooms, and the other is how much fresh air you need to add to that to keep people alive and happy (which is the same as how much stale air you need to remove, of course). If you go with a simple ventilation system plus a mini-split in each room, then those two are the exact same thing, since you aren't recirculating any air at all! (Unless you want to make your ventilation system more complex than it needs to be). In this case, the mini-split itself "circulates" the air through the room (sort of), so you don't need to calculate that. But you DO need to calculate the "room changes per hour" figure, AND ALSO take into account room occupancy, in case you need MORE than the amount of air specified by simple room changes per hour (a.k.a. "Air Changes per Hour" [ACH] and "Air Change Rate" [ACR]). But if you go the other way, with a single AHU that services all the rooms through a ducting system, then you have those two different rates to deal with. In that case, then "room changes per hour" is how much air you need to recirculate through the rooms, and then you need to decide on what portion of that is dumped overboard and replaced with fresh air coming from outside, to keep people alive. For example, you might need to circulate 400 m3/hr, but determine that only 20% of that needs to be fresh air, so you'd need to dump 80 m3/hr of stale air while bringing in 80 m3/hr of fresh air to replace it. Or whatever the numbers turn out to be in your case. If I recall correctly, Rod does make this distinction in his book, but Newell does not. ( I may be wrong on that: it's a while since I last read Newell... ) So it's different depending on which plan you choose. One way to look at this is that you need to supply a minimum rate, which is probably also established by your local building code, regardless of how many people are in the room (one study suggests that the absolute minimum should be 3 changes per hour even when there are NO PEOPLE AT ALL in the room), then you need to ADD to that the requirements for each person in the room. But read through the ASHRAE spec: it's heavy going, but it gives you an idea of the complexity behind the calculations and the specifications, and a better understanding of how it all fits together. You can use those equations if you want, but personally I think it's far simpler to just go with simple rules of thumb, such as those in the Toolbox link. (Be thankful you aren't building a commercial kitchen in there: You'd need as much as 60 room changes per hour!). One thing you might want to take into account: furniture, equipment, instruments and people take up some of the room volume... You don't need to take into account the volume that they occupy! Nice! You could spend the time to measure the dimensions of every last thing that you put into the room, and subtract that form the room volume... but there's an easier way: a good rule of thumb is that all of that stuff with take up about 10% to 15% of the room volume, so you can reduce your room volumes and rates by that amount. In other words, the actual volume you need to put into your equations is about 85% of the real room volume.

Re Silencer design - I was thinking of a box around 120cm x 60 cm x 30cm, like the design seen in many threads here. One for the fresh air in and one for the fan powered stale air out, a pair for each of my 3 rooms.
One of the key points about silencer box design is "impedance mismatch". When sound is traveling down a tube, and it reaches the end of the tube and runs into open air, something very interesting happens: part of the sound carries on, expanding suddenly into the open, but part of it is reflected back up the tube where where it just came, this interfering with itself, and causing phase cancellations and other good things that greatly reduce the sound energy inside the tube. The reason why this "reflection" happens is because the acoustic impedance inside the tube and outside in the open air, is very different, and that impedance mismatch causes the reflection to happen. As luck would have it, it turns out that you don't really need to have the tube hitting open air: as long as there is a sudden substantial change in the cross sectional area, you get a nice impedance mismatch. In fact, if the cross section changes by a factor of 2 or more, you get a good effect. So your rule of thumb here should be that the cross section inside your silencer should be at least twice the cross section of the duct that feeds it. If you are using 6" duct, for example, that implies a cross sectional area of about 28 square inches, so the interior of your silencer would need to be at least 56 square inches. In this case, a rectangular section of 6" x 10" would be good. In the metric system, that would be roughly a 15cm diameter duct, feeding into a silencer that measures 15cm x 26cm on the inside. (Don't forget to take into account the thickness of your duct liner!) The length of the silencer box depends on how many baffles you need.

That's not the static pressure that they produce: that's the maximum static pressure they can handle. Static pressure is basically like resistance to airflow. All of the duct-work, dampers, registers, silencers, and everything else in the path of air flow combines to create a certain amount of resistance to the movement of air. The fans in the AHU have to be able to overcome that, in order to move the air. So if your static pressure is 200 Pa, and the static pressure capability of the AHU is only 100 Pa, then you have a problem: the fans will be running up against too much resistance. The air won't move correctly, the fans blades will not be working within their design specifications, the fan motors will overspeed, the whole thing will be noisy and inefficient, and it won't work. The AHU will be overloaded, and its life will be short and noisy. It's like using a car to pull a truck up a hill. On the other hand, if your AHU can handle 100 Pa and the static pressure of your system is only 50 pa, then that's great. No problem. It's like using a truck to pull a car up the hill. So you have to make sure that the static pressure of your system is low enough that the AHU can deal with it. 100 Pa static pressure is only medium. Depending on what static pressure your system has, you might need a unit that can handle a higher static pressure.
