Ro8 June 2009#2
Since you want optimum isolation between the two rooms you want as much space between the sheets of glass as possible. Putting them straight will do just that. So it'll perform slightly better than slanted concerning isolation. Visibility on slanted windows is a bit better (less reflection/mirror-effect). When slanting windows upwards waves might be reflected a bit towards your cloud (absorption). Tho, the effect is minimal. I'd go with straight mounted windows, but it's up to you. :)

rod gervais10 June 2009#8
French - John told you the right of it....... as did RO. Parallel glass is maximum isolation - but you stand the risk of having (visual) reflections affect the comfortable use of the window. Give exactly the same mass - a 2 leaf system will out-perform a 3 leaf system. There is NO truth to the claim that you increase isolation between glazing leafs by splaying the windows. The fact is just the opposite - you loose some isolation because of the loss in the spring system that is created within the free air space....... Rod
Soundman202010 June 2009#9
I'm paraphrasing what Rod Gervais says about slanting glass: basically, it has no acoustical benefits, is detrimental to isolation, and only has visual benefits (the ones John mentioned). To get maximum isolation, you need maximum air gap between the panes. If you angle the glass, then you no longer have maximum isolation, since the two panes of glass will be closer together on one side. Regarding acoustic reflections off the glass, EG a singer's voice reflecting back off the glass into the mic: if you angle the glass then all that you accomplish is that the reflection comes back from a different point on the glass, but it still comes back (unless you set the glass at a really huge angle). Rod makes other very convincing arguments too, but those are the ones I recall off-hand. So like John said: the only reason you need to angle glass is to avoid those annoying visual reflections. Intelligent placing of lights helps a lot with that, too! If the positions of people, lights and windows are figured carefully beforehand, then those reflections can be minimized such that the glass only needs to be angled as little as possible. Like André always says: building a studio right is 90% about designing it right, and only 10% about actual construction. I'm curious about those studios you mentioned with three leaves of glass: They would have to use pretty thick glass in all three panes to counteract the loss of low-end isolation from the three-leaf effect. I wonder why they do that? - Stuart -

rod gervais12 June 2009#17
John Sayers wrote:
Anyone have experience with such a situation.
No.
Neither have I....... but that is a stange statement on the face of it - and Bob is a pretty sharp fellow. The reception area (for a reflected sound) based on the angle of reflection from the window back to the listeners position is a 3 part function - part 1 being the location of the speakers, part 2 being the distance of the listeners ears to the window(along with their height in relation to the speakers) - the 3rd (then) being the angle required for the window to deflect the sound below the listeners ears (preferably into the back of the board so as to not have reflections from the board screw things up). So what would work 5' from the window would not work if your board was tight up to the wall........ likewise - you would require less angle the greater the distance you are from the window....... It is not as clear cut as less than 10 degrees doesn't work....... 10 degrees works real well in a whole lot of situations......... Rod
