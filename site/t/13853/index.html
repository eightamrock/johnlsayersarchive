The ceiling will be approximately 2.4m and above the studio will be a bedroom.
Do you mean that you'll be STARTING with a 2.4 m ceiling before you even build the studio, or that you are designing it such that the studio will end up with the final inner-leaf ceiling height being 2.4m? There's a big difference. Maybe 20 - 30cm or so (give or take a few), depending on how you plan to build your ceiling and floor. If you are talking about the former, then do you mean that the bottom of the subfloor above you will be at a height of 2.4m, with the joists underneath that, or do you mean that the bottom of the joists is at a height of 2.4m? That's another big difference... Another 10 to 20 cm, or so...
For the ceiling, my architect (for the house extension) seems to think it won’t be a problem to part-support a double layer plasterboard ceiling of this size from the joists above if needed.
Another reason to change your architect! He "thinks" it might work? And he came to that solid conclusion without consulting a structural engineer? And he's the guy who is DESIGNING all this? :shock: In any event, if you did that then you will NOT achieve good isolation, since your ceiling will be directly coupled to the floor above. You will clearly hear all impact noise from above, such as people walking on the floor, vacuum cleaners, brooms, kids bouncing balls, etc. And they will hear what is going on down in your room. In order to get good isolation, you need to decouple your ceiling from the floor above. That implies that your ceiling will be supported on an entirely separate set of joists, that are attached ONLY to the inner-leaf walls of your room below, and are NOT attached to the floor above, or to the outer leaf walls.
Extension build – is there anything I should be stipulating to the builders regarding the construction of the extension or insulation methods to be used?
Yep: First, find a builder who has built successful studios before. Then tell him it will be a standard two-leaf totally decoupled double hermetic sealed construction, following all the usual precautions for such a build. Meaning things like very careful attention to triple seals under stud walls, total air-tight sealing on both inner leaf and outer leaf, only surface mount electrics (no through-wall mounts of any kind), star-grounded electrical system, correctly dimensioned HVAC system with silencers and internally damped ducts, double or triple sealed solid core acoustic doors, etc. If you get a builder who has never done this before, you will have to spend several weeks training him on how to do it, then you will have to supervise him very carefully every step of the way, since any mistake he makes can totally wreck your isolation. such as forgetting to caulk along the top of the inner-leaf wall, where it joins the inner leaf ceiling, leaving a tiny 1mm gap there. That is guaranteed to trash your isolation. Or forgetting to plug the main electrical feed conduit where it comes into the room. Or forgetting to seal the HVAC ducts properly. Or accidentally letting a single nail bridge the gap between inner and outer leaf. Or any number of other things. If you combine an ignorant architect with an ignorant builder, you are pretty much guaranteed of failure...
Studio door – installing double doors will cause problems if one of them has to open into the hallway. I’m also wary of the stress placed on the timber frame if I use a single thick door. Are there any lightweight materials I could use to beef-up a door, or alternative approaches?
That's a contradiction in terms! You cannot "beef up" a door with "light weight" materials! By definition, "beefing up" a door means adding mass to it. LOTS of mass. If you put light-weight materials on a door then you have wasted your money, since it will not do anything at all assist isolation. There are no magic materials in acoustics. All materials follow the exact same laws of physics. You cannot bypass the laws of physics in your build: they are the same everywhere in the entire universe. In order to get good isolation on a single door, you will have to build Rod's "Superdoor", or something similar. You can find the construction details on how to do that on pages 93 to 95. And that beast is HEAVY! Carefully follow the instructions on how to mount it, especially regarding the heavy-duty hinges, and the triple seal. That really is the only alternative you have if you can't do a normal double door.
Window – as mentioned, I plan on using the Laptoppop window block for the front, but would like to have some natural light from the rear (south) so aim to include a control room style window. Is there some way to access the gap between the external/internal windows for cleaning or does it have to be a sealed unit?
It has to be sealed if you want good isolation. Both sides have to be sealed airtight. Making a window operable greatly reduces the isolation.

xSpace16 January 2010#10
If isolation is a key importance then everything you can do to remove flanking is important. Flanking is the building allowing sound to travel thru it with no restriction. It [flanking noise] can come from anywhere and go to anywhere, all it needs is a path. This is why people that do this for a living and others that have studied flanking will make every effort to break any connection to remove the potential for flanking to interfere with introducing noise and reducing isolation capabilities of the environment. Your build already has built in flanking hazards with the upstairs bedroom. If you allow a hard connection to your house, and all the noise capabilities and noise energy the house can produce, then you will be installing a reduction in your isolation simply by allowing this. It may not be something to get all "spooked" up about :) But you said isolation was important and you have several things going against you in this area that suggest, maybe, it is important, but not so important that the bedroom cannot stay:) Again, certain codes in your country may not allow a complete decoupling of the one structure from the other. Having a bedroom overhead suggests to me that this is an addition to your residence. Otherwise it may be viewed as an apartment building. Code and insurance will look at either in different ways, depending on what is or is not allowed in your country.
