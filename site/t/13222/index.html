Hi Richard. Welcome!
These are back of the envelope first guesses for shell isolation/TL. Shoot me down in flames: 1. Assume the drummer (or bass gtr) achieves 130 dB peak SPL at 30Hz, 50 Hz and 1KHz and is close to the inner wall of the imaginary live room
Wow! That is SOME drummer you have! And SOME drum kit! I've measured plenty of drummers at 110 dB(c), many at 115, a few at 118, and a very small handful at 120. How you get ten times more energy out of a drum kit than that (and ONLY in the 30 Hz band!), is beyond me (not even if you hired Lou's gorilla and a few of his friends....) I think all your skins would be shredded long before you got there. But let's assume you have kevlar skins, and super-tough brass, for argument's sake: So, if your drum kit is putting out 130 dB SPL in the 30 Hz band alone (A weighting or C weighting, or flat, or whatever else you like: there's little difference at those levels), then the total sound energy output is going to be somewhat greater than that. Let's say 135 dB for argument's sake: That is already beyond the threshold of pain. So not only is your drummer doing an amazing job of making the most noise ever recorded from a drum kit, he is suffering painfully in order to do it, and will be stone deaf in roughly 23 seconds (or rather "seriously and irreversibly hearing impaired".) At 132 dB you can actually feel your own eardrum flexing. At 137 db, all frequencies are physically painful to the human ear. 130 dB is roughly the level you get out of a huge stack of speakers at a rock concert, measured one meter away. Also roughly the same level produced by a 747 at full take-off thrust, measured 20 m away. I'm really not sure how you get that kind of energy out of a drum kit! That's just my way of saying that I think you are over-estimating the output of your drummer, by an order of magnitude! :) I totally doubt that you will ever get more than 125 dB out of your drum kit, and that would just be the instantaneous level as you hit all the surfaces you can reach at once, such as smashing the kick, snare, crash and ride, as hard as you possibly can, all at the exact same instant. (Maybe you could gain an extra half dB or so by also smashing your face into one tom, and your elbow into another tom.... :) ) Anyway, your average levels are more likely to be 115 dB, perhaps peaking around 120 if you have Lou's gorilla on your payroll. So you'd probably want to redefine your top level to something more like 120 dB SPL (C) measured one meter from the drum kit with the crazy gorilla beating it to hell.
2. Threshold of human hearing is ~50 db SPL @ 30 Hz, ~45 dB SPL @ 50 Hz and 0 dB SPL @ 1KHz (using ISO 226:2003 equal loudness curve)
No, actually the threshold of human hearing is 0 dB SPL, which corresponds to a pressure variation of 0.00002244 Pascals. Normal atmospheric pressure is about 100,000 Pascals, so 0 dB SPL is a very, very small pressure variation, but it is still audible. It makes your ear drum move about one third the diameter of a hydrogen atom, believe it or not, yet we can actually still hear that, under the right conditions: It's pretty hard to find "the right conditions", though! However, if you sit quietly in an anechoic chamber for about 20 minutes or so, you can get there. You actually start to hear the sound of your own blood pumping through your own veins, if your ears are in decent shape. Then you can hear sound at 0 dB SPL, if you are lucky. But anyway, the point is that your threshold seems to be off base. 0 dB is the absolute threshold of human hearing, but it would be absurd to use that as your goal. However, 50 dB is also off base. 50-60 dB SPL (C) is about the average background level you'd find in an office, home or maybe a quiet restaurant during the day. 60-70 dB SPL (C) is roughly the level of a normal conversation. 40 dB SPL (C) is a whisper at about one meter. 30 is about as quiet as you can expect to find in nature: the middle of the desert on a calm night, for example. So you'd probably want to revise your goal down to about 45 dB SPL (C), which is roughly the level of a quiet bedroom at night in a quiet neighborhood. And if your goal is to NEVER disturb your neighbor, then you need to aim for a level of 42 dB, measured inside your neighbors house, or maybe 48 dB just outside his window.
So total silence immediately outside the studio can (theoretically) be achieved with 80 dB TL @ 30Hz, 85 dB TL @50Hz and 130 dB TL @1KHz
I'm not sure how you got to those figures, but I don't think it would be easy to build a wall that gives you 130 dB TL at 50 Hz! Maybe several inches of solid lead sheeting would do that. Not sure.... I don't see that as being acheivable by any other means! But fortunately, you don't need to. Your numbers are just off: Way off! Anyway, based on the above, you probably need total isolation of around 70 - 75 dB, as measured on "C" weighting on an average SPL meter, for pink noise. And that is not taking into account any particular frequency band: just the overall measurement of full spectrum pink noise. The isolation level for any specific band would be somewhat LESS than that, obviously. If you want to break it down by 1/3 octave frequency bands then you'd need to do a spectrum analysis of your worst-case scenario (mad gorilla on the drums, crazed orangutan on the bass, rabid baboon on the electric guitar, amps turned up to 11), to come up with the requirements for each frequency band. I'm not sure if you really want or need that, as pink noise is a reasonable substitute, but it would be the way to go if you think you do. So your goal should probably be around 70 to 75 dB TL. Of course that is NOT the same as STC-75. 70 - 75 dB total isolation is doable, but not easy.
The TL characteristics of this approach in the range 63Hz - 125 Hz seem particularly poor due to system resonances and gap size.
Yep. And that's right where your kick drum lives, too! No matter which way you cut it, a two-leaf wall is WORSE than a single-leaf wall at low frequencies. But of course, 2-leaf is still the best possible isolation you can get on a budget. Single leaf is lousy at higher frequencies, compared to 2-leaf, and 3-leaf or 4-leaf is really REALLY bad for low frequencies. Fully decoupled two-leaf MSM is the way to to go. Lots of mass on both leaves, large air gap totally filled with insulation, and everything sealed airtight. That is the only way to do it. Chant that mantra every hour on the hour, five times in a row, until it becomes part of your DNA. Fortunately, your kick is not putting out 120 dB all by itself. So you certainly don't need to block 130 dB for the 50 Hz band alone! (which is what you originally imagined). And you also don't need to block 75 dB just for the 63-125 Hz range. You only need to block much LESS than 75 dB in that band. If you really need to find out how much, then measure your kick all by itself, out in the open air, at one meter, and you'll know how much you need to block in the range 30 to 130 Hz. (or whatever the spectrum of your kick turns out to be....)
- 200mm concrete blocks, plastered on both sides
That's a pretty good start, right there! Fill the blocks with dry sand for improved mass. This will get you a long way towards where you want to go.
An inner/outer leaf gap of >100mm with appropriate density mineral wool or other spring
For your case, I think I'd go to more like 200 mm. Greater air gap is better isolation, and also gives you a lower MSM resonant frequency, which is GOOD!
- A different material/thickness of inner wall: mineral wool-packed stud wall with multiple gypsum layers
Or maybe a second concrete block wall? Or a brick wall? That would do the trick, provided that you can keep the cavity between the walls clean and free from flanking paths (mechanical bridging), such as from dripping mortar, or careless bricklayers leaving rubble behind. You'd need an awful lot of gypsum board layers to match the mass of 200 mm concrete block, so bricks or blocks might be easier, if you really do need such high levels of isolation.
- No contact between inner and outer walls
Goes without saying! Any contact at all will kill your isolation. This is arguably the single most important point of all, for any MSM wall. Any connection means you wasted oodles of money. Second most important point: Airtight. Even the slightest crack means you wasted more oodles of money. So it is feasible to do what you want, and the rule is the same as you'll find all over this board: two-leaves ONLY (never, 1, 3, or 4) lots of mass on both leaves, large air gap between, fully damped with mineral wool / fiberglass, fully decoupled, and hermetically sealed. But that's just walls we are talking about. What do you have in mind for your roof and ceiling? How are you going to maintain that kind of mass in your roof, for example? Maybe construct the building with a flat concrete roof? That would work. Hang a half dozen layers of 5/8 drywall on some really tough beams for the inner leaf ceiling, and you are probably there. Then you have three more issues to consider: Doors, windows, and HVAC: These are all holes in your leaves. A hole in your wall is a Bad Thing. So you'll need to build all these with the same 75 dB goal in mind, if that's the level you really need. Doing doors, windows and HVAC to 75 dB TL is not an easy task. That will take some planning, and some careful construction. And of course, none of the above takes into account the top-secret hush-hush magical treatment that you are (not) talking about. Maybe that will absorb another few hundred dB, all by itself? :) - Stuart -
