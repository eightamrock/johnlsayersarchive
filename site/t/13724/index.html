Yep. Definitely true.
I haven't been able to pinpoint why. I assume it has something to do with early reflections.
Basically, yes, among several other reasons. Like Lilith said: It helps to create a reflection free zone (RFZ) around your mix position. That shape turns out to have the best geometry for accomplishing that task, but you need to get the angles, ratios, key dimensions, and geometry right, or it won't work well at all. That shape also has other benefits that I won't get into here, since I might end up writing a novel-length response... (not that anyone would ever accuse me of doing such a thing!!! :) ). So you need to flip your CR's around, such that the narrow end is at the front, facing the LR's, and you need to get the angles, dimensions, ratios, geometry, etc. right too. And like Lilith said, your CR's also needs to be symmetrical,. If they are not symmetrical then you will never get an accurate stereo image in them, and your mixes will not translate well.
Some more information on this topic and any other suggestions would be extremely helpful.
Two big suggestions: Buy Rod's book (search the forum for "Rod's book" for details), and buy the Master Handbook of Acoustics by F. Alton Everest. Once you have read and fully understood both of those, you'll be well on your way to being able to design and build your studio correctly, and to educate your architectural firm on the remaining zillion and twenty one issues that are so extremely important to studios but make no difference whatsoever to a house, shop, office or apartment.

OK, that makes more sense now! But don't allow them to fool around with the layout, dimensions, geometry or anything else acoustically related. As long as all they do is draw up plans, file them, and get your permits.
I've been working in greatly non-symmetrical non-acoustically designed control rooms for a decade and have been more than happy with the results.
That's strange, because without symmetry in a control room you CANNOT get an accurate soundstage and stereo image. Without clean, balanced acoustics with close to flat response, and a good RFZ around the mix position, you have no idea how the music REALLY sounds. Without that, your mixes simply will not translate well. I could never be happy in a room like that. I could get my mixes sounding great in a room like that, for sure, but they would sound lousy anywhere else... You can't mix successfully in a room that does not tell the truth.
however I'm working within these space constraints.
That's a rather large space that you have there! The constraints are only imposed by your design. A lot of people on this forum would kill to have that much space available for theirs studios!
Looking at the design I haven't been able to figure out any easy way to flip the control rooms around. Hence why they're oriented the way they are.
Well, until you do figure that out, your chances of producing a usable studio that works, are pretty low. You can't beat the laws of physics by just hoping that they'll go away and maybe not apply to your space. They still will, just like for any other space. There are very, very good reasons for the shapes, ratios, geometry, dimensions and layout of studios, and they are all related to the laws of physics. You can't fight that. If you want a studio that works, then you have to design in accordance with the laws that the universe has imposed on you. If not, then you'll have to continue being happy with a mediocre, non-symmetrical, unbalanced, non-acoustical, non-flat response room where mixing is a nightmare and the results don't translate.
