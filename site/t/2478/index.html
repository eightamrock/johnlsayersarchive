knightfly14 October 2004#2
Eric, drums are a bear to control; in a small room, even moreso. What's your available headroom in there; can you afford to lose 10-12 inches and still live with it? Because about the only sure way to kill drums enough to not bother neighbors is a completely floated room, and the floor of it needs to be a floated concrete slab of at least 3-4 inches over at least 4" air space, which is filled with rockwool for damping. Then, your inner walls are built on that slab and a ceiling is either supported on top of those walls, or suspended BETWEEN those walls, and caulked tightly. Doors would need to be heavy, solid, and sealed. Otherwise, you will leak sound into the surrounding room which will then vibrate the structure, which will transmit to other parts of the building and re-radiate into other rooms. This is especially true of bass drum and low tom's. This degree of isolation is doable, but only if you're serious and don't take shortcuts... Steve

knightfly14 October 2004#4
The concrete floor will transmit a lot of sound into structure (and thence into other rooms/floors) if it is allowed to be in direct contact with (or hard flooring for that matter) the sound field; that's why I mentioned the floated concrete slab. Your walls should all be one form or another of a mass-air-mass structure; so if there is wallboard on the OUTside of a stud frame, then that frame should be filled with insulation, and either resilient mounts with two layers of wallboard or (better) separate frames resting on the floated slab, with two layers of wallboard on those frames. The idea in all directions is that you have mass, then air/insulation, then mass; nothing more, nothing less. The more air/insulation space, the better; the more mass, the better. Different masses for inner and outer leaf helps avoid a common resonance in both, which would pass one particular frequency easier than others. So, if there is already wallboard on the inside of your studs and the outside is fixed, you can remove the wallboard (if you do it carefully along the stud lines this can be re-used to beef up the OUTER layer some; just cut the old board into strips that will fit between studs, and put a light bead of construction adhesive along the edge next to each stud and press in place. This will increase the mass of the OUTER leaf without having to remove that wallboard also. Then, if there's room you can put up a separate inner frame with insulation filling the entire gap, and you won't need resilient mounts for the inner two layers of wallboard because of the separate frames. These frames can be either wood or steel; steel is available in narrower widths than 2x4's, so might be a better choice. Since this wall would NOT be load bearing, all you'd need is some rubber-mounted sway bracing at the tops of the wall; then, your ceiling could go on resilient channel (If necessary, you can use the extra wallboard against the subfloor above for more mass in that leaf) - you would end up with the mass of the upper floor + wallboard glued at edges ONLY, then insulation fill, then resilient channel or RSIC clips and hat channel, then two layers of 5/8 wallboard suspended INSIDE your inner walls and caulked. I think that cover it; if not, you know where to find me :) Steve

knightfly17 October 2004#6
Yeah, what you need to end up with for acceptable drum isolation is a double framed wall; how you get there depends on what you already have, and (as you mentioned) what you're willing to do. One thing to be careful of, though, is possible sources of flanking around your double wall construction; the floor, ceiling, and all walls need to be decoupled inner from outer leaf EVERYWHERE, or you get flanking through any hard connections via framing, etc. So, if you change your OUTER leaf as you mentioned, you may run into problems getting your INNER leaf separated from structure. This will limit your isolation seriously, to the point of NOT isolating the drums effectively. I know very few situations are "ideal", but I need to reiterate what the ideal condition is so you'll know if you can get there or not, and what obstacles are in the way - A true room-in-room setup has a heavy, floated floor that is not in hard contact with ANYTHING around it; inner wall frames/wallboard are then built on top of this, making sure (with rubber isolated sway brackets) that no part of these inner walls comes in hard contact with the outer walls, which also need to have only ONE side paneled. Then, the inner ceiling mass for this room can either be hard fastened to joists resting on these inner walls, or it can be suspended resiliently from the outer ceiling framework using Resilient Channel, special RSIC-1 clips and hat channel, or something like Kineticsnoise.com's special isolating suspension mounts. The final goal is to have two masses separated by insulated air gaps COMPLETELY surrounding the space to be isolated; the sound should have to pass through two separated masses in order to get to where you don't want it, no matter which direction. The heavier these masses, the better isolation; the wider the insulated air gap, the better isolation; and the mass leaves need to be damped by having insulation pressing lightly against the inner surface for best results from any given combination of materials. I've seen tests of REALLY good, heavy double concrete walls where you literally could not hear a pistol shot through the wall; then, one side was core-drilled and a single pencil wedged lengthwise between the two leaves of concrete; the hole was then filled with mortar and let set - after that, just loud conversation was easily heard through the wall. The point of that last was to illustrate just how important it is for ALL paths for flanking to be eliminated. Your outside concrete wall, if sound isn't isolated from it, will transmit sounds that hit it into the rest of the structure; then, any part of that structure (framing, or anything NOT decoupled) will radiate that sound back into other rooms, even other floors. Here's a link on flanking, just so you can check it out for yourself - http://irc.nrc-cnrc.gc.ca/irc/fulltext/rr103/rr103.pdf If you have any way of either taking a few pix or doing a drawing of what is already there and what you propose to change, it would help discussion quite a bit... Steve

knightfly21 October 2004#8
Sorry about the delay, life's been even more "interesting" than usual lately - First, you definitely want RC under the double layer; this will be true on any ceiling or wall except the hard concrete, where you'd want more air space before using wallboard (like narrow steel studs with insulation) The Rotozip plan is a good one (if you have a good shop vac :=), just use a bead of construction adhesive around the perimeters of the drywall "inserts", and if you're nervous about them falling (I probably would be) rip some 1x1 cleat material and fasten cleats under the second layer of wallboard, screwed into the sides of the joists. That way, there's no way the stuff can separate from the subfloor and create a third leaf after it's all closed up. Metal studs are fine, and they should NOT have firm contact with ceiling joists - you can buy or build sway brackets for the tops so they don't contact the outer wall OR the joists directly - here's one suggestion I gave Aaron for his that you may be able to apply - http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... 0176#10176 RC is only needed if both sides of the SAME frame have wallboard (or mass) like a ceiling, or a single framed wall - when there is a separate frame, no RC is necessary. Your floor will depend on how much iso you need; drums are difficult, so would need a fully floated high-mass floor to be sure. On the concrete walls, one test would be to put a LOUD boombox upstairs and listen to the wall with either a stethoscope or a drinking glass between the wall and your ear; if you can hear ANYTHING, you have flanking thru that wall and it will need to be decoupled, RC under wallboard is minimum; separate frame is better, and ALWAYS insulation. BTW, if you can the 3 PCF fiberglass (703) or rockwool will help a few dB over standard house stuff. Late for work, hope this helped... Steve

knightfly23 November 2004#10
Yes, the layers of gypsum need to be firmly against the subfloor with no air gaps - one way to do this is to nip off the nails and grind smooth - another is to sharpen the tips of the nails so they will push into the wallboard easier - hopefully the nails will only be a problem for the first layer you add between the joists. If you use construction adhesive, it needs to be fairly runny - most are not. Too stiff a bead and it won’t let you squish the inserts firmly enough against the subfloor. Probably a better way is to use drywall joint compound; if the subfloor is older, plank type construction it will probably have gaps. If so, these should be filled with joint compound and smoothed with a taping knife before adding the wallboard inserts. Then, you would use fairly thin compound in a 1 to 2 inch stripe along the edge of each joists, leaving the REST of the insert NOT glued - you can glue two layers up at the same time, then fasten them firmly in place by using long 1x2 cleats held firmly against the edges of the wallboard while nailing or screwing the cleats in place. This operation will probably take at least one helper, in order to keep the inserts from pulling away from the subfloor while you're putting the cleats in place. Once you’ve finished that part, you need to caulk where the cleats meet the joist and where they meet the wallboard inserts - this needs to be a hermetic seal. Doing it that way with the cleats, even if the joint compound should fail in a few years the cleats will keep the extra wallboard from falling down into the ceiling insulation, causing a triple-leaf effect and hurting your low frequency isolation… Steve

knightfly28 November 2004#12
Running screws up into the subfloor through the gypsum layers will worsen the isolation a few dB, as does any metal penetration through multiple layers in a wall or ceiling - this is why the cleats instead. You could probably get away with not using the cleats if you were sure that whatever adhesive you used along the edges (only) of the gypsum inserts would hold indefinitely; I personally don't feel that's a good idea, because if the adhesive lets go after you've already finished the ceiling, what will happen is that the gypsum inserts could end up dropping down in the cavity, causing squashed insulation and a 3-leaf barrier, all of which could cost you more than 10 dB of isolation... Steve

As to flanking, any inner wall frames should NOT contact the upper joists but have a small gap (1/4" to 1/2") - any position control for these frames will need to be done with decouple sway braces. If you don't want to spend a lot on these, check Aaron's floating floor thread (REFERENCE section link) - I posted some drawings of possibilities, and Aaron built one and took pix, it's here http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... &start=210 Sketch is on this page - http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewt ... &start=165 I also have one wall which is plaster/lathe on the outside of the space and tongue and groove 3/4" sheathing on the inside. Should I pull this sheathing off so I only have one air space before building a 2x2 steel stud wall in front of it and then covering with wall board? Yes, otherwise you'll lose LF performance of the wall. For your flanking questions - if an inner wall frame has hard contact with ceiling joists, then you need RC or RSIC clips/hat channel inside before the wallboard layers; if your wall has resilient sway bracing instead, and NO hard contact with struture, then no resilient wallboard mounts are necessary. In either case, I would put up your WALL layers first; then, hang your wallboard layers on RC, suspended INSIDE the inner wallboard with a 1/4" gap around the perimeter - use the methods outlined in the COMPLETE SECTION sticky, a link within the REFERENCE section at the top of the page - do your first layer on the ceiling, caulk, add the second layer, caulk, finger form the caulk on the inner layer so you can put up inside corner beading, then finish normally. Doing the ceilings this way, you will get minimum flanking between rooms because the ceiling layers are not continuous; the inner ceiling layers, the inner wall layers, and your caulk, make up a continuous mass layer which keeps flanking noise from impinging on the rest of structure (assuming you either have sway-mounted inner frames OR RC mounted wallboard.) HTH... Steve

They are prone to condensation and ghosting. Metal studs should never be used with cavity insulation because it makes them even colder. If metal studs are used they should be limited to interior walls or to the interior of rigid insulation assemblies. Metal studs should never be used below grade unless they are separated from slabs with thermal breaks (“sill gasket”) and separated from foundation perimeter with rigid insulation. Below-Grade Walls The main problem with below grade walls comes during the summer when warm moist air comes in contact with basement cold surfaces (see Figure 27). Basement walls should be insulated with non-water sensitive insulation that prevents interior air from contacting cold basement surfaces. The best insulations to use are foam based and vapor semi-permeable. Vapor semi-permeable materials allow the basement wall assemblies to dry to the interior. No interior vapor barriers should be installed in basements – ever – because they trap moisture inside the assemblies. One of the worst assemblies for basement walls from the perspective of mold and moisture problems is a foundation wall that is internally framed and insulated with fiberglass cavity insulation and covered with a plastic vapor barrier. Metal studs only further aggravate the problem. Air gets in behind the framing and condenses on the cold surfaces. Alternatively, moisture from the foundation enters the internal framing. The moisture is trapped within the foundation assembly by the vapor barrier and deterioration occurs. All bottom plates of below grade frame walls should be thermally isolated from basement floor slabs with thermal breaks. Commonly available “sill gasket” (3/8 - inch thick foam – 31/2-inches wide) is an excellent thermal break under bottom plates."
