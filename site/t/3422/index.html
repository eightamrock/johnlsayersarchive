knightfly7 July 2005#7
Lynn, one of the downsides to non-parallel surfaced rooms is trying to estimate what they will sound like BEFORE they're built; I'm not aware of any software solution to this, other than possibly CARA (which I own but have yet to find time to learn to any depth) - I know this program lets you do non-parallel walls, and it's deep enough to model almost anything if you have the time - it's deep enough that even on a fast P4 some simulations can take more than a day to complete. More here - http://www.cara.de/ENU/index.html Although there are a few quirks this is a LOT of value for the ridiculously low price of $70 Eur - again, if you don't have a LOT of time, don't bother... One way of "ballparking" non-parallel rooms is to AVERAGE all dimensions; this works pretty well, and in any case the final analysis must be done with tests using both hard/soft ware and EARS, and adjusted with the various acoustic treatments mentioned here and at other acoustic oriented sites. The vast majority of "golden" room ratios were arrived at long before it was so easy to run a set of numbers through a spreadsheet, point being that these should not be considered as LAW - they will, however, make it easier to get in the ballpark with a design. For example, you can look at the space you have available, compare the possible plan dimensions to some of the "golden" ratios, and pick the one that comes closest to your space; then, changing one dimension (or two) of the three can be decided on by what third dimension gives one of the preferred ratios - you can then use that dimension (width, for example) to set the AVERAGE width of your room, choosing angles for the walls that will minimise or eliminate early reflections at the mix location - Example - if your CR space can be a maximum of 16 feet wide, but you need a wall splay of 18 degrees each side to avoid reflections from either speaker off the side walls, you would move the points of the side walls IN by (say) 2 feet (this depends on length of the wall section obviously) - this would then make the AVERAGE width between walls 2 feet less (2 feet each side, half of that averages 1 foot, X 2 sides = 2 feet lost in total AVERAGE width) - so this new value would be the one used in modal calculations ) Keep in mind that this does NOT mean you will have exactly the same modal response in a splayed-wall, sloped ceiling room whose AVERAGE dimensions are the same as a fully rectangular room with 3 pairs of parallel surfaces; only that the GENERAL response of the rooms would be similar, but with GREAT differences depending on where in the room measurements were taken. Personally, I would not rush into a design without spending a LOT more time learning the ramifications of different choices; where you appear to be at this time is building something you HOPE will work, and possibly too far along to make changes that actually WILL work - I'm not judging here, so much as warning you about possible pitfalls; concrete tends to be relatively permanent, so it's usually good to know where/what/how you want it BEFORE you call the delivery truck - One thing that's prompting my concerns is your plan; I looked here and on your link, including the PDF - and found no mention of a roof line. This (unless I'm just not seeing it) tells me you haven't considered (not much available here or elsewhere on this :oops: ) that a really quiet studio requires planning far ahead for a complete "containment" shell before ever getting into room shapes, acoustics, flooring choices, etc - I'm just waking up after my last 12-hour graveyard shift and need to be somewhere else shortly, so I'll have to cut this short for now; meantime, check out this link http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.php?p=5570 and, for an excellent spreadsheet which does all three types of modes as well as Bonello distribution, etc - http://forum.studiotips.com/viewtopic.php?t=497 This second link will probably require you to join the studiotips site and be logged in, but it's well worth it. If you can post an elevation or something showing your roof line/slopes, etc, it will help figure out what can be done with inner ceilings; overall, though, you also need to consider maintaining a mass-air-mass shell around the entire space for best isolation from things like airplanes, trucks, trains, or any other loud rumbly interference. Gotta run, hope this helps. I usually don't have time to check into the Design forum all that often, so if you want more input you can PM me as you did - not trying to ignore you, just 'way more things going on than there are hours in the day... In your case, making sure your ENTIRE outer shell is heavy and sealed should be your immediate priority - inside that, you'll need a second mass separated from the first by insulated air space - this also brings up the subject of moisture/vapor/air barriers, where they go, if they're necessary or a detriment, what's required by your local code, etc - for a TON more on that, check out some of the Building Sciences links in the REFERENCE section of the Construction forum - once at the Building Sciences site, check out all their "white papers", and spend at least an hour looking over all their info. Joe Lstiburek is one of the few Engineers who can talk "human" talk and still knows his stuff. Sloped ceilings; absolutely. you will want a MINIMUM of 6 degrees, more if possible. In a CR, if you have the headroom you want a LOT more slope so you can eliminate early reflections at the mix position. People who don't have a few extra feet of headroom are forced to put 2-4" of rockwool or equivalent spaced 2-4" away from the ceiling, at least in a 4x6 foot "cloud" centered over the mix position, in order to control flutter and early reflections. In a tracking room it's even MORE necessary NOT to have parallel surfaces, especially if you want a more LIVE environment. Any surfaces that are parallel will mean more area where absorption is necessary, which will leave no choice but have a more DEAD room. Lack of choices SUCKS for a studio - ideally, you should be able to place portable treatments to get the degree of liveness/reverb you want for the particular situation, but if it MUST be dead in order not to be "boingy", then you're STUCK. You didn't mention which books you bought; one excellent (but not cheap) one is Phillip Newell's Recording Studio Design. It's about $70 at Amazon, and there are some things you'd never need, but it's a course in acoustics (and a whole lot more) all by itself.
