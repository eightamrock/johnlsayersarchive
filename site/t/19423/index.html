This was the pink 1/2 styrofoam board insulation
:shock: OK, so you were right when you said you had done so many things wrong! :) Not trying to "rub it in", just adding this comment for those who find your thread in the future: Styrofoam has no useful acoustic properties at all. It is great as thermal insulation, yes, but does nothing at all acoustically. It is closed-cell foam, and therefore air cannot penetrate into it. If air cannot get in, then neither can sound. The type of insulation that works for acoustic absorption (fiberglass, mineral wool, acoustic foam, etc.) is more correctly called "open cell fibrous absorption", because at the microscopic level it is made up of numerous tiny flexible fibers separated from each other only by air. If you blow into one side of such material, the air moves through and out the other side. Since "sound" is basically just vibrating air molecules, the sound waves can move through the entire thickness of the material, and whenever a vibrating molecule happens to encounter a fiber, it causes that fiber to move and flex. That does "work" on the fiber (in the physics sense of "work") using up energy. The fiber absorbs some of the sound energy and turns it into low-grade heat energy, which is dissipated within the material itself, and also within the air, to a certain extent. Since there are millions of fibers, there are millions of collisions between vibrating air molecules and the fibers, so fibrous absorption is very efficient at removing some of the energy in the sound as it passes through the panel. However, with closed-cell materials, such as styrofoam, there is no way for any of the above to happen. There is no way for the sound waves to move through the material causing fibers to vibrate, so there is no way for it to absorb sound energy. Styrofoam and other closed-cell insulation has no acoustic uses.

Will the average guitar nerd listening to my demos even care?
Very, very likely, if it allows him to hear the guitar as it really sounds, rather than the way your room mangled and distorted it into sounding.... The issue is this: You can get any mix sounding great in your room the way you proposed to build it: that's not the issue. The demo can sound fantastic when played ... in THAT room, since you will be compensating for the room's defects in the mix, either consciously or subconsciously. But when you take the mix and play it some pace else, such as in your car, living room, MP3 player, a club, or the system at the home of your "guitar nerd" client, it will sound lousy: the "compensation" that you built into the mix to make it sound good in YOUR room, is no longer needed for that other playing location, so it will just sound "wrong" when played there. In other words, as I said before, your mixes will not "translate". That's why the concept behind control room design is to make it sound neutral. The room should not add to or take away from the direct sound coming form the speakers. It should just "disappear". It's a myth that control rooms should "sound good": they should NOT sound good! They should have no sound of their own at all. So when you mix in a neutral room, your mixes will have nothing but the straight natural sound of "no coloration" in them, and therefore will sound the same no matter where you play them. They will sound great in any other playing location, because they are neutral. That's the concept, at least.

Soundman202025 May 2014#9
Stuart, quick question: Would it be totally unacceptable to achieve symmetry with the use of gobos?
Unfortunately, you can't change the modes of the room with Gobos. Modes are all about the hard, massive, rigid boundaries of the room. Gobos might break up the modal response a little, to a certain extent, but they won't change the modes. Gobos would work reasonably on high frequencies... but then again, those are not very directional anyway... - Stuart -
