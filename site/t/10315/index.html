rod gervais11 March 2008#2
Aaron, I have been fighting this battle for lot of years now - and there really isn't any way (with standard wood frame construction) to get out of this dilema. So I decided follow these paramaters: For new construction design a decent 2 leaf isolator into my roof -and then use a seperate "within the room" construction for my rooms - with mass only on the bottom of the inner frame. For rooms within existing buildings - I alloow the roof to breathe however it was designed to in the first place - build my room within a room inside that envelope - and apply plywood with drywall over the top of the inner room's ceiling frame - so my 3rd leaf is there instead of at the exterior frame. Although I get better isolation in the first case - this is probably more due to the the fact that the outer shell as a whole is designed with isolation in mind - and not just the roof. But - I also get excellent isolation in the 2nd case - The bottom line is this - don't sweat things you can't change - just make the best of them - and in either situation - pay very close attention to details - it's adhearance to getting the smallest details right that make or break your project from an iso point of view....... not the 3rd leaf in a ceiling that needs to breathe in order to exist for the next 30 years........ Rod

Something to think about: If you have rigid insulation directly attached to the underside of your roof decking you DO NOT need to ventilate your roof. If you have fiberglass or some other form of insulation then you MUST vent. The only potential drawback with not insulating a roof (when done properly) is that the life of the shingles can be shortened. This is basically moot if you use high quality shingles. On the other hand a vented roof with poor ventilation can ruin your decking. I had a roof with proper soffit vents and a proper ridgeline vent and it still failed because the cheap cobra ridge vent did not vent quick enough for the small space in between the sheetrock and the decking (cathedral ceiling). I installed a better ridge vent (lomanco) and the issue was solved. I was getting moisture on the back side of my decking and moisture was growing mold in the spring and fall when the temperature difference between outside and inside was not as drastic as it is in the winter and fall. Another roof in my house ( a post and beam house I built) has 2x cedar decking as the exposed ceiling inside, with rigid polyiso insulation sandwiched between the decking with NO air gap. Moisture can not get in there, thus the decking has stayed dry and will last. Habitat post and beam out of Mass. has been using this roof formula for years and they have seen shingles and decking last 30 plus years. Venting is key if you have an air space under your decking, not needed if you don't. I don't know if that changes anything for you. Also, if you ridge vent, use a rigid plastic vent like lomanco. Cobra vent eventually fills with dust and starts to fail, plus only vents enough for attic spaces where there is a lot of air (ironic that small air spaces need faster venting). It is widely used because it is cheap, easy, and the problems with it don't start until well after the contractor is out of the picture.
