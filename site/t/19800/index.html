Hi Matt, and welcome. Please read the forum rules for posting (click here). You seem to be missing a couple of things! :)
The space is basically 40 x 30 with 9' ceilings.
That's a really nice sized space! You can potentially build a great studio in there.
My current plan is to build a basic decoupled room design with wood studs 24" on center, double wall design with 1" airspace in between walls. Studs will be staggered
There seems to be an issue there: Why do you want staggered studs when you are also doing separate frames for your leaves? Staggered stud walls are for situations where there cannot be a separate from for the inner leaf, for whatever reason, but you already said that you will be doing "double wall design with 1" airspace in between walls". so you do not need staggered stud! The inner leaf wall for each room is just a single 2x4 stud frame with drywall on only ONE side of it. Since each room is built like that, as a single-leaf stand-alone structure, there are automatically two leaves between the interiors of any two rooms, and also two leaves between any room and the outside world. If you were to build a staggered stud wall for each room, with drywall on both sides, then you would have FOUR leaf walls, which would give you terrible isolation for low frequencies. You would also have three-leaf walls to the outside world, which aren't quite as bad as four-leaf but still much worse than the correct two-leaf design. If you were to build 4-leaf staggered-stud walls, you' not only get lousy isolation for low frequencies, you'd also spend a lot more money than you need to! But your SketchUp model doesn't show any of that: it would be good if you were to update it with the actual stud framing layout that you plan to use, and the drywall, so we can see exactly what you have in mind, and help you fix it so it will work.
Studs will be staggered until I get close to a window.
Windows are framed exactly the same as the rest of the wall: single stud framing. The framing does not change around windows or doors. If it did, then isolation would be compromised. One of the key principles of getting good acoustic isolation is that surface density of the entire wall must be kept consistent throughout. There should be no places in the wall where the surface density is significantly lower than the rest of the wall, and that includes windows, doors, HVAC, electrical, plumbing, and all other parts. So if you calculate that you need two layers of 5/8" (15mm) drywall on each leaf to get the resonant frequency you need, then your surface density is about 24 kg/m2, so you need to use the same density in the framing around your windows, the glass itself, your doors, your electrical penetration, and your HVAC silencer boxes.
Double 5/8 drywall with insulation. Not exactly sure what I'm doing with the ceiling yet,
The same as the walls! Sound doesn't care about the difference between "left", "right" and "up". If your walls are isolated to one level, but your ceiling isolates less, then you wasted a lot of money on the walls! Sound will just take the easy path out, through the ceiling, totally bypassing the walls. Sound is like water in may aspects: it detours around barriers, and finds the simplest path out.
but I'm thinking about doing a spring and resit clip design attached to existing joists.
Then you could forget about the new stud walls completely, and do the same for those as for the ceiling. Just put the RSIC clips on the existing wall studs, put up the hat channel, and hang your new drywall on that. As I said above, there's no point in making the walls so much better than the ceiling. That's just wasting time and money. If you don't need much isolation such that the amount you need can be obtained from just clips and drywall, then that's what you should do for the complete room. On the other hand, if the amount of isolation you need requires double-framed walls, then that's what you need on the ceiling too.
I would prefer to not lose too much ceiling height. If you have some better ideas I'm definitely open to anything.
There are a couple of techniques that can help to minimize loss of headroom. One is to interleave the new ceiling joists between the existing joists above you. That isn't always possible, since there might be cross bracing, HVAC ducts, plumbing, or other things up there that prevent it. If that's the case, then another really good option is to build the ceiling "inside out", meaning the drywall goes on top of your new ceiling joists, not under it: the joists are exposed to the room where they can be used as part of the acoustic treatment: no height lost at all.
Floor is 4" concrete and I plan on leaving that and building floor on top.
If you have a perfectly good concrete slab on grade as your floor, why would you want to build anything at all on top of it? That's about the best possible floor you can get for a studio! Building something over it can only make it worse, in every way. If the concrete is in bad shape, then maybe lay laminate flooring or ceramic tiles on top of it, but don't do any building! Your floor should be as hard, solid, rigid, thick and massive as you can possibly get it, and it's hard to beat 4" of solid concrete!
I have a sketchup design and I'm waiting to get back other drawings from my architect.
As I mentioned above, your SketchUp model does not show the details of the isolation system you have planned, and that's critical. Also, it does not show which of the walls are existing, which are new, which are load-bearing, which cannot be moved, how they are built, etc.
Any advise and feedback is greatly appreciated. For now I want to make sure my overall design isn't weird in any way.
What is the ratio for your control room? It seems rather long and narrow. Did you check that with a room mode calculator? Also, you are only showing one of the doors for each pair, but you really should show both, to clarify the area that each door sweeps as it opens. Your vocal booth, for example, is impractical in that sense, since the inner door has no place to open into! It would hit the singer and mic as it swings open. Ditto for the kitchenette. Speaking of the kitchenette, it's never a good idea to have anything "wet" that opens directly into the live room, control room or any other acoustic space. Each time the door opens/closes, there's a potential sudden change in humidity in the room, and that can affect both instruments and mics: some are sensitive to changes in humidity, and the tone changes. I always try to keep bathrooms, kitchens, bars, green rooms, and anything else that has water associated with it, outside of the acoustic shell of the studio, where it can't do any harm. Have you considered other layouts?
I'm also slightly concerned about the window in the control room and what to do with that. I plan on keeping the window, so it's just a matter of making the best of it.

How will you decouple that wall form the rest of the existing structure, so that you can complete the inner leaves correctly? Is it a load-bearing wall? What type and thickness of glass is used in that window?
