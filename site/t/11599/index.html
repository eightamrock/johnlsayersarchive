xSpace30 January 2009#6
The benefit is a more even response across the frequency spectrum from the room. It isn't magic, but it is better than just laying out walls and framing it up. That said, ratios, based on what I have read, seem like they work better in bigger rooms. Small isolation booths and small rooms in general are going to have sound frequency issues no matter what you do. But it is always recommended to apply some type of rectangular room sizing to anything you construct. This will at least eliminate some of the problems associated with square rooms, cubed rooms and dimensions that are divisable by each other. So that is the benefit of determining good room dimensions from the very start of any studio build or isolation booth. edit: Any room, in general, will benefit. If you build a home and apply ratios to your rooms, even that will be a positive to you.

Here is exactly what Rod's book says about the ratios, this can be found on page 28. "In order to design rooms in which these types of problems can be minimized, a series of known good ratios have been established over the years specifically for small room designs." It also says that the larger the room, (greater than 5,000 cu.ft.) the less important these ratios are because "there is generally enough travel distance so that the sound has a chance to decay to the point that constructive/destructive actions in the room are beginning to minimize." My understanding of these ratios is that they should be followed exactly because any deviation from them is going to affect the room modes. So my question from before was, "How do you apply these ratios to a corner control room?" In other words where do you measure from to get your L and W of the room?
