Soundman202020 November 2012#3
Hi "robsn", and welcome! :)
The landlords condition is that we build a drum raiser filled with sand ,
Why would you want to fill it with sand? That doesn't make a lot of sense. The purpose of a drum riser is to decouple the drum kit from the floor, normally by resting the deck on a thick layer of insulation. If you replace the insulation with sand, then you have not decoupled anything! You have added mass, sure, but that won't help if you don't also decouple.
a 2.5cm thick wall around it
I don't understand this either: Why would you want to build such a thin single-leaf wall around just the drum kit, when you say that you are also going to isolate the entire room? Are you saying that you actually want to have a drum booth AND a live room AND a control room?
We want to seperate the room so we have a control room and a rehearsal/recording room with a vocal booth.
OK, but you also spoke about have a room for the drums, built on a drum riser: Where is that going to be? I don't see it on the diagrams.
This is my first idea:
The walls are coupled. To isolate a room, you need to have a totally decoupled inner-leaf wall that does not touch an part of the existing walls or ceiling. Your control room shows a wall around only three sides, so you need to add the wall for the other side. The live room only shows two sides done so you need to do the other two as well. Also, the iso booth only shows two walls done for isolation: the other two also need to be done.
I came up with another idea
The control room and live room layout are better, but the other two rooms are not practical. Also, all of the walls are still coupled. If you want good isolation, then you need to correctly decouple the walls in each room. - Stuart -

Soundman20203 December 2012#5
I worked on the design and decided to make the control room and the booth decoupled from the outer walls.
Great, bu is there any reason why you didn't do the same for the live room / rehearsal room?
Because we have two drumsets we thaught to make two seperate risers with wheels. It would be nice to be able moving them around.
All that you will achieve with a drum riser is to decouple the impact noise from the floor. Is that your goal? A riser will not help at all in dealing with the airborne sound form the drums. All it does is stop the physical impact of the pedals and hits from getting into the floor.
1. Question: Are wheels + insulation a good way to decouple the raiser?
Yes, you could do that. Put the wheels on one end, such that they are slight above the floor when the riser is sitting flat, then put handles no the other end so that you can lift that end (like a wheelbarrow) enough so that the wheels touch the ground and the riser is off the floor, then you can wheel it around as needed.
2. Question: Does sand make sense when the raiser is decoupled?
For what purpose? Adding mass is always useful, but you are not trying to isolate a room here: just to decouple the impact from the floor. Two or three layers of thick wood is plenty for accomplishing that. Besides, if you want to put wheels on it, you don't want to make the things so heavy that you can't move it!
Door: For the entrance door and the door attached to the wall we built i thought to use massive wooden doors and add another layer of mass to it. Am I right?
That door doesn't really matter, since you have chosen not to decouple the rehearsal room from the building. Just a single solid-core door will probably be fine. As long as it has the same surface density as the leaf that you put it in, and good seals, that's all you need there. It would be different if you decouple the room properly, but with the plan you have chose there won't be much isolation anyway, so the door does not need to be much better than the rest of the room.
and to those touching the outer walls we added a layer of 3mm rubber foam and screwed them to the walls.
In that case, the rubber didn't actually do much at all, except to improve the seal around the edge of the wall. The simple fact of screwing through the rubber into the outer-leaf means that you have short-circuited the decoupling provided by the rubber, and the new wall is fully flanking to the rest of the building.
I think it will isolate the air-sound more than it will decouple the walls.
It's the same thing. Airborne sound makes the walls vibrate, and since the are mechanically coupled to the existing structure, the vibration will flank to the rest of the building.
Till now we added one layer of 12.5mm gypsum drywall
That is too thin for maximum isolation. You should only ever use 16mm drywall for isolation. 12mm is too thin, too flexible, and too low in mass.
and left a 3mm space to the outer walls
Do you mean at the end of the wall, where the new drywall joins up with the existing outer wall of the building? That gap is not any use, since the framing itself is coupled and flanking.
Question 1: Waste of money?
Not a "waste", no. Just money that is not very well used! :) - Stuart -
