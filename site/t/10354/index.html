gullfo22 March 2008#3
8) as Andre points out, the homasote probably doesn't do much but the green glue will add quite a bit to the equation since it is not a glue as its name implies, but rather a viscous constraint layer damping mechanism - it never really hardens and allows the two layers of drywall to move independently while being dampened. don't put the green glue between the homasote and the drywall, but between 2 drywall layers.

Actualsizeaudio wrote:
If I understand Eric, the laminated glass can be made with glass that is first tempered before it is glued together. Would there be any advantage to this for a door window? From reading the glass section of the book it seems clear to me that lami glass is all you need for safety reasons. To temper glass one need to heathen it. This PVB foil couldn't withstand this temperature, hence you understood right: http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toughened_glass Last year I did a studio where standard laminated glass was used for the normal windows, but laminated tempered glass was used for 100% glassdoors without wooden (or whatever frame). Laminated glass, keeps all peaces together, when glass breaks. This extremely thin foil is unbelievably strong. In fact laminated glass was designed for the safety world. The acoustics proved a useful side effect. Tempered glass is better impact resistant (small stones or whatever flying against the screen of your car window). Tempered glass when it breaks (if it happens as supposed to) breaks in a million pieces without these very sharp edges typical for standard glass acting as knives when it breaks. Tempered glass is also used for doors or whatever where the hinges and other accessories are directly mounted on the glass. It's a matter of money: Combining both properties is expensive, and these panes have to be made on size in the factory because you can't cut them afterwards (grinding could possibly be an option, anyhow it's expensive). Standard laminated glass (2 layers of glass) can still be cut with standard means (is difficult, you have to cut it in both sides, hence it asks some practice). Such panes are produced in large sizes. For normal situations (unless you have an enormous amount of people passing through) standard laminated glass will do. If you have such a 100 % glass door, you better use it tempered to mount these accessories on/through that glass. Acoustically there is no significant difference. Hence the choice is mechanical or increased impact resistance. I'm just speaking of my own experience. I've no idea what standards worldwide have to say about all this. :) If you have unlimited budgets you can go for the best. But whether that's sensible is a very open question mark. Note that in laminated glass there are differences. The last +/- 15 years (can't remember exactly) there are special versions of laminated glass where the foil is specific designed for acoustic purposes. But the effect is mainly to be found in the coincidence area. Hence it has mainly sense when you go to thick panes where this coincidence falls within a dominant/defining frequency range. Stadip silence from Saint-Gobain is such a type (rouglhy price ca 135% of normal laminated, but depends)

Folks, I do prefer tempered glass to float - and also to laminated glass. My only reason for this is due to the fact that you can scratch or pit float and standard laminated glass - while this is not the case with tempered. With tempered - either it is unmarred - or it is broken - it's just that simple. I think I shall revisit this issue and make it a one of chapters I expand when I prepare to publish the 2nd edition of the book. Sincerely, Rod
