guitardad729 April 2007#2
I am going to use a combination of mortar and self leveling concrete to level out part of my floor which drops over 1 1/2" in 9.5' Recommended to me by Sandersd in my thread here
Have you ever heard of a mortar bed such as is used under tile? Masons use it to level a floor in preparation for laying tile. It can be several inches thick. My point is it a common practice and is considered by many tile men the best substrate for laying tile. If it works for tile, it'll work for other flooring also. You don't need self-leveling concrete unless you are laying a floor that requires a precisely leveled floor. Just make sure any aggregate in concrete is smaller than the thinnest areas of concrete.
I'm not going to say this is the best way, only 90% sure this is what I am going to do as I've not yet found another option. 1st I'll put down the mortar as flat as can be, then build my inner walls on that. Then drywall and most of the build happens. Then the self leveling concrete just before the wood floor. Self leveling concrete is something like $40 per 60 pound bag and would be expensive by itself for an entire floor... BUT it can be feathered out to an edge unlike regular concrete which needs to be 3 1/2 thick or something like that. To my understanding it's mostly used for leveling, pre installing hardwood floor and other floors which need a very level surface. You can also partially or completely float a wooden deck/float your floor. A search through Google brought me to a DIY leveling a floor technique (please try and find it :D ) which was partial floating. Many PT 2x4's were used, each ripped down to a precise angle, space 16" or 24" on floor and then 3/4" ply on top of that. This technique had nothing to do with studio building. IF you were to do this you would want to fill the voids with insulation or even sand is recommended BUT make sure you read the Floating Floor sticky this forum before even thinking about floating a floor. :D IN my case a mistake by my architect was made, for near the same money I spent on a new footer I could have had a new slab poured and not have to deal with my floor uneven, which part of used to be a patio. Redoing your garage floor would be the best, would cost the most BUT would give you the options of isolated slabs and footers under your new heavier then average studio walls. My wild A$$ guess is it might cost you up to $2,000 depending on how much you can do yourself. I'll be watching this thread to see if any one else has a brilliant idea to level out a conrete floor for a studio. cjbdrm, please when/if you make your decision how to level this, let us on the forum know. Good luck and congrats on the (I presume) home purchase. Marc
