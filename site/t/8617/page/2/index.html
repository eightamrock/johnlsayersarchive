sharward1 July 2007#32
Yes. Ditch Witch is the famous ditch-digging gas-powered tool. It can be operated by one person and it makes digging utility/plumbing trenches a breeze. I've never operated one before, but I've seen them in use many times. They're a bit like large chainsaws that cut into the ground, carrying chunks of earth up on their teeth. You'd be a fool not to use one for that task! :-) Be sure to call the national "Before You Dig" phone number a week or so before you dig... You'll then get the assurance that there are truly no utilities on your dig route, or if by chance there are, techs will mark the locations with stakes and/or marking paint. If you fail to do this, then you alone are liable for any damages, assuming the collision doesn't kill you, in which case your estate will be! Substantial variances on your concrete point to it not having rebar. As a rule, if if a concrete crack is substantial enough that you can stick a coin (dime) into it and have it stand up without holding it, it's bad and should be replaced. Advice was given about consulting a structural engineer. Actually, you should (also?) get an evaluation from a geological engineer!! Our first home was settling at one corner of the garage quite substantially, with cracks in the wall and the floor telling the tale, all visible during our escrow period when buying the place. At the advice of our home inspector, we ordered a geological survey to assess the situation and recommend a remediation strategy. It ends up a nearby birch tree's roots had obviously jostled some sections of walkway, causing a damming effect of the rainwater that came from the rain gutter downspouts at that corner. The recommendations were to have the tree and its stump removed, tight-pipe the downspouts to the street, and reset the concrete to a level state. We had a contractor do that work (on the day we were to close escrow in fact! :shock:). In your case, shoddy construction may a factor, but my money's on nature having gotten the upper hand. :roll: --Keith :mrgreen:
goldstar3 July 2007#33
j Check the gutters and drainage around your building to make sure you have positive drainage away from the building. Water puddling around the perimeter could have caused some small amount of settling which could crack the slab (though not necessarily ruin it). Can you find city or county records for the garage when it was built? From your photos, it doesn't look to be old at all, and local code should tell you about rebar requirements. You shouldn't be able to see it (rebar) from the edge of the slab anyway; this would let moisture in to rust the rebar. I don't agree with Sharwards comment about the cracks in the concrete indicating the need for replacement with dime-sized cracks in it; lots of perfectly good concrete has cracks in it. Frank
