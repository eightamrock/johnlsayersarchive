We are under contract to buy a home that has a 12x20, slab-on-grade, single car garage that I will be converting into a studio. My plan is to ponder the floor plan (one room, or control room/live room/vocal booth?) while I get the building shell ready. Getting the shell ready will include the following: 1. Get electricity to the building. As of now there is no electricity installed in the building. The breaker box in the house is at the opposite end of the house from where the studio is. jwl wrote:
I have not yet checked the municipal regulations. I doubt that there is much there, this is in a pretty rural area. But I will definitely be checking that out, and doing the whole permit song-and-dance.
Yeah, you'll want to check on this right away, just to be sure. Some communities (mine as an example) prohibit full garage conversions. Mine requires a minimum of 10' wide by 20' deep by 7' tall area free of obstructions and an operable garage door at least 7' tall and 8' wide. If you had a restriction like that, your project would be over before it started. There may also be setback restrictions, depending on how close the garage is to the property lines. The town may prohibit habitable space around the edges of the property, while allowing storage/garage there. Habitable vs. non-habitable is a big distinction in terms of city/county planning restrictions. Find out now what restrictions, if any, would apply to your situation before you get too far along. A half-hour visit to the permit counter can settle this. :-) Would it be completely out of the question to build a new standalone building out back, spacewise and/or moneywise? In retrospect, I really wish we had bought prooperty with a larger lot and a smaller garage -- I'd rather have started building from scratch, big time.

OK, time for an update. The wellwater report came through, and there were high levels of radon in the wellwater. This is quite common in this part of Maine; apparently radon is a byproduct of the natural radioactive decay of trace amounts of uranium in the soil. It's quite common in wells which are drilled into granite, which is the case with our artesian well. So it's no surprise. Sadly, as of now there is no law in the state of Maine mandating that would make ignoring radon problems illegal. Currently, Maine "recommends" corrective action when your levels are 4,000 pCi/L (picocuries per liter); ours was at 20,990. That's moderate, our inspectors have all seen many cases in the hundreds of thousands, and even a few over a million. Radon in the water is not so much harmful when you drink it, the problems come when it goes airborne (say, when the water coming out of the faucet in a shower or while doing dishes), and we breathe it. At the end of the day, radon is a lung cancer risk. But I'm pleased to say that we've reached an agreement with the seller to install a radon mitigation unit, which works by aerating the water, releasing the gas outside. So the biggest obstacle to us closing on this property has been overcome. This is very good news. We should arrive at a closing date in the next few weeks, and it looks like we'll be in by the end of May. Also, this radon mitigation unit will take up more space on my 100amp breaker, this to me reinforces the notion that I should run a separate power line for the studio. I'm having dinner with a friend of mine who is an electrician in Maine on Monday; I hope to pick his brain some.

sharward29 April 2007#13
Ah yes, my beef-up phase! Boy, am I glad that's behind me! You'll probably come to the conclusion that I committed overkill. You'll probably want to do two layers of 5/8". I did a layer of 17/32" OSB, three layers of ½" drywall, and one layer of 5/8" drywall, with Green Glue behind the last layer. Your worst enemy may be the rubmle of trucks. Next time a truck rolls by, get on "all fours" and feel the slab to see if you feel the rumble. If you do, you've got a potentionally pesky and expensive problem on your hands (literally). Depending on the outcome of that, you may actually be a candidate for a well-engineered floating slab. --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward30 April 2007#15
In my case I used a layer of OSB to start with because then I'd have something to attach drywall to. If you have plywood sheathing over the entire building, you don't have to use OSB. OSB is more expensive and less dense than drywall. So, if you use drywall, it's cheaper and better for isolation. OSB does tolerate some occasional moisture, as long as it's able to dry out, whereas ordinary drywall is basically ruined by moisture. --Keith :mrgreen:
