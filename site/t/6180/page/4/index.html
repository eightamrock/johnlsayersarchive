Nice concrete work. :-) And nice framing work too! I'm assuming you built those walls "in place" and not on the ground and then tipped them into place (since the headers were done first). If so, that's how I intend to do my outer leaf walls I think (about 20 linear feet), coming up soon. However, that outdoor step -- it's not to code. :-( Doors leading to outside on residences need to not result in a "step down." You need (I think) 36" of level surface. There are also requirements for the steps in terms of rise and run, and if you have multiple steps they have to be close to the same height (not sure of the exact language on that). This "landing at the door" issue is what caused me to toss my floating floor plans. I would have lost too much square footage to my sound lock. It was a very frustrating time for me in my planning stages. Now I realize your home is older and was probably built before this code was adopoted. But it looks like the french door replaced a slider much more recently. That french door install needed to be done according to what was current code at that time. Technically the landing needed to be done at the same time as the door. So, for this reason, I don't think you can play the "grandfathered code" card. If anyone (a houseguest for example) were to trip and fall in that area, the person could have a good personal injury case due to the code violation... Not to mention the unpleasantness of someone -- a friend or family member -- possibly splitting their head open there. And lest you think that's hardly likely, it isn't, especially at night when there's limited visibility. --Keith :mrgreen:

len-morgan1 July 2006#89
I'm a little confused. You have put up three layers on the ceiling before even one layer on the walls? That's not the best way to do it. The "normal Good Housekeeping Seal of Approval" way is celing,walls,ceiling,walls, etc so that if you took a cross section you'd see a zig-zag pattern. There is a drawing Knightfly did that shows this. (Search for drywall and sequence as a start) The idea is that you want to make it as difficult as possible for the sound to get in/out. If you do all the ceiling THEN all the walls, sound has a straight shot at the joint to get out with only calk to stop it which leads to my next question: Why aren't you using backer rod? It can't be because of the cost. It's dirt cheap (if you buy it from the right place) and you can have it in only a day or so. By filling "large gaps" with only calk, you are asking for trouble down the road that can be difficult if not impossible to fix later. The backer rod is used so that the calk only touches two surfaces. If you touch 3 (notice how "3" keeps coming up as bad in acoustics!), sides, you are going to have one of them pull away due to drying out, seasonal temperature changes, natural motion in the building structure, etc. and as I already said, if you've got a straight shot because of the way you've layered your drywall, you just blew a major part of your isolation! :-( I understand the urge to do SOMETHING. To show progress. Fight the urge and make sure you've taken everything into consideration BEFORE you lay hammer to nail. I think my biggest advantage in getting as much right as I did on my studio build so far was having a separate building in another part of town (although only 1/2 a mile away or so), so I didn't have my wife asking me every day where all the money was going and was I almost done yet? :-) I could take my time and if I had any doubt, I'd ask here if I was doing (or going to do) things right. As to your bottom plates, I think you have 2 choices here (maybe 2 1/2 depending on how you count. 1) Use pressure treated lumber for the bottom plates 2) Use regular stud quality lumber and "sill seal" between the bottom plates and the concrete 3) Use pressure treated lumber AND sill seal. Sill seal is foam on a roll (like the stuff a TV comes wrapped up in) that you lay down on the concrete and build on top of. It's available at Home Depot, Lowes, and I'm sure any decent building supply place. If you don't do this, you stand a chance of moisture coming up through your cement when it gets cold and rotting the bottom plates. This can be unpleasant down the road a year or two when you end up wearing your 3 layer ceiling as a hat as it all comes down around you. It's funny in the cartoons but not so much in real life. The expense is minimal and the peace of mind is worth it. len
