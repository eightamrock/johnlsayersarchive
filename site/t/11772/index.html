Soundman20207 March 2009#5
Hi Trey: Welcome!
Are the specific angles of the walls in the control room important? I modeled the shape after some of the floor plans i found on this site, just not sure about the exact ratios/angles of the walls.
Like Brien said, 6 degrees for your CR walls, and 12 degrees for the ceiling, all angled away from the speakers. That assumes that you are using the normal layout for speakers (60 degree intersection angle). If you go with a different angle, then you should splay your walls more. If you do a 90 degree angle for the speakers, then you'd probably be better with 12 degrees or more of splay on your walls. Are you going to soffit mount your speakers? It's hard to see on your diagram. About ratios: search the forum for "room ratio", and start reading! But do make a large pot of coffee, first! There is a stack of info on that, more than you can get through in just a couple of hours. Basically, the idea is to find a set of ratios that roughly fits the space you have, then adjust your walls nad ceiling to fit that ratio. The ratio determines the "spread" of the room modes around all three types of axis (longitudinal, tangential, oblique). The idea is to get to a ratio where the modes are spread out as evenly as possible across the low end of the spectrum, not all bunched up around a couple of frequencies with gaps in between. That would make the room very resonant in that part of the spectrum, and "dead" in other parts. You'd have hugs dips and valleys across your room response (in both the frequency and time domains), and you want to avoid that, at all costs. There are various sets of ratios that various people have calculated over the decades, as being "ideal", and giving the most even spread of modes: Choose a good one that is close to your room's existing ratio, and nudge your walls and ceiling around until you hit that ratio. Things get more complex if you room is non-rectangular (ie, if it has splayed walls, as in your case), but the best plan then is to figure out an imaginary "best fit" rectangular block that kind of averages out the splaying of the walls / ceiling, and use that as your "ratio". It isn't very accurate, but close enough for the long wavelengths at the bottom end of the spectrum. But search the forum for these concepts, and follow any links you find to off-site pages. It's an interesting voyage! That's for your CR of course. Your LR you can make irregular, if you want, with non-parallel walls, vaulted ceiling, whatever makes sense. Just try to avoid obvious traps, such as curved walls, tight angles, parallel hard surfaces, etc. You'll be treating it acoustically anyway, so you can minimize problems, but don't make things harder for yourself than they need to be! In general, keep the floor reflective (concrete, wood, etc), and the ceiling absorptive. You can always use throw rugs if you need to deaden the floor later, but it's hard to rip up fitted carpet if it isn't live enough! Like Brien said, if you have vertical space, then use it! You are privileged few among us more humble home studio builders, who can squeeze out maybe 2.5 meters of height with a lot of luck. You have the luxury of plenty of vertical space, so make use of it. (A side note on CR room ratios and vertical space: Normally you see the first "1" in the ratio as referring to the ceiling height, but you can turn that around and use the "1" for the width too. Don't use it for the length, though, since that would imply that your speakers are firing across the short dimension of the room, which is not a good idea. Your speakers should always fire down the long dimension of the room, not across it.) One thing that might be a concern: You said you are on the third floor? How tough is that floor under you? What is it made of? Did you get a structural engineer to inspect it yet? You are going to be piling on several tons of additional weight, so you need to make certain that it can handle the load! - Stuart -

xSpace8 March 2009#7
The concern with elevated areas is two-fold, at least to start. These are concerns to the builder and should be given due notice. The type and kind of isolation you develop requires heavy build materials, heavy in the tons. If you had an area 1000 sq. ft rated at 40psf liveload, it could carry (theoretically) 40,000 pounds spread evenly across this 1,000 sq. ft. But this number is misleading.* The 40 pounds per square foot is a number placed on the overall footage of the room. But the construction often required is not evenly spread across the entire floor. It is placed directly on top of stud walls that are directly on top of a considerably smaller footprint of the existing floor space. I think it is called point loading. So now you have a perimeter area equal to 130 linear feet, or 43.33 sq. ft. attempting to carry the same load that had been applied to a 1,000 sq. foot area! The structure will not take it. You would most likely not have it completed before the structure reaches breaking point and collapses. The other issue is not as disastrous physically but financially. The amount of effort and materials it can take to reduce the intrusion of your music into the surrounding areas can be prohibitive. These are the reasons that so many, whether they know it in fact or from having heard it said so many times, will tell you that building an elevated recording environment is almost always a poor idea. Granted there are exceptions, and they are few. The biggest exception is that isolation is not a requirement. This lowers the material combination's considerably and allows a reduced isolation concerned space to be developed while still attempting to maintain a good acoustic environment. Brien edit: * This is an over-simplification on my part. Even a 40psf live load does not expect a constant load to be present at all times. This is the reason a 150 pound person can walk across a 40psf live load rated floor and not have it collapse. The floor can take it :) What it cannot take is a static load at the boundaries of the engineered limits that never changes.

OK, I had a few minutes free, so I cleaned it up for you, and re-positioned the speakers, based on the assumptions that this really IS your inner leaf wall, and the you really ARE building the front and back walls inside-out, and the side walls normally. The intersection of the speaker lines should be a few inches behind your head, not in your head. the lines should hit your ears, not your nose. Soundman202011 March 2009#21
Should I build my control room inside out?
I don't think you need to, in your case. It's a good solution where space is really tight, as it gains you a few more inches, but if you have plenty of space, as you seem to, then the extra construction difficulty is probably not worth it. It is harder to build, since you wont have good access to some parts of the wall where you need to seal, raising the walls will be a major pain as they will be very heavy (you have to put the drywall on first, before you raise them, as you won't be able to get into the tiny gap to put it on afterwards. etc. So construction is more complex. The other advantage that it gives you (apart from extra space) is a savings in building materials, since you can use the studs that are now facing your room to attach acoustic treatment, and make good use of the space between them, instead of building frames to hang your treatment on the wall afterwards. I'm not sure how much you could save like this, but it would probably be worthwhile, if your budget is really tight. But overall, if you have a reasonable amount of room and you are not on a shoestring budget, then you probably don't need to do it. - Stuart -
