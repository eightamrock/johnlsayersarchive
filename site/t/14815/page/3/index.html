Soundman202019 October 2010#52
It is completely stuffed with insulation and will get a second layer of drywall. We also packed in some of the white foam that you see in there, really dense, heavy stuff to add some more mass,
That was probably not a good idea. Using insulation this is too dense will reduce your low frequency isolation. Anything over about 50 kg/m3 is too dense. Even worse is if you "pack it in" tightly, as that could very well create flanking paths through the wall, especially with very dense insulation. It should only be laid in place, never "packed in". You can fill the cavity, certainly (if code allows it), but you should never overfill. When you say "white foam", what exactly do you mean, anyway? What kind of "foam" is that? Most foams do not have any useful acoustic properties, unless they are open-cell foam. Most common foams are closed-cell. Those are useless, acoustically. So what did you actually use in there? If that is some type of packing foam, most likely it is doing more harm than good. If you don't know what it is and what its acoustic properties are, then take it out and replace it with something that is know to work well, such as 703 wrapped in fabric. - Stuart -

Soundman202028 October 2010#65
So you dont recommend just setting them without angle..
You CAN set them without any angle if you want, and doing so maximizes isolation, but then you have to take care with your lighting to avoid glare coming back at you from the glass surface. That's the "optical" issue Glenn is talking about. With no angle at all you do run the risk of having highlight reflected back, making it harder to see through. So basically, if you can figure out a good lighting plan to avoid that, then sure, put the glass in flat! But if you think it might be an issue, then angle it slightly, in whichever direction is most likely to avoid glare. As long as you only angle it a bit, you won't compromise isolation too much. And if you are concerned about isolation, then you can always get thicker glass! With MSM, it is usually possible to compensate for reduced air gap by increasing mass. - Stuart -
