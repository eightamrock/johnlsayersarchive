The room "modes" are basically resonant frequencies at which the room itself vibrates, simply due to the dimensions of the room. If you play a note on an instrument, and the sound wave for that note fits exactly between two parallel walls, then that will cause a "standing wave" in the room. Basically, the sound sort of "bounces back and forth" between the walls perfectly at that exact frequency, but not at other frequencies. So that specific frequency is a "mode" for that specific room dimension. For example, if you have two walls facing each other exactly 4.2 meters apart (about 13' 9"), then any time you play a note that has a frequency of 82 Hz (a low "E" on your keyboard or bass guitar), that "mode" of the room will vibrate in sympathy with the note. The "mode" can be said to actually store some of the sound energy from that note, then it will release the energy back into the room even after you stop playing the note: in other words, the room itself will continue to vibrate at that frequency for a certain amount of time. That is a "mode". (Its actually a bit more complex than that, but it gives you a rough idea). Since the modes depend on the dimensions of the room, and since they also occur at twice the frequency, three times the frequency, 4 time, 5 times, 6 times, etc... for all of the harmonics of that note, all they way up the scale...., so obviously there are going to be a LOT of modes in a room. And those are just the simple "axial" modes that occur between two parallel walls. There are other kinds of modes that bounce around between any four walls of the room, and yet other modes that bounce around between all six walls (the ceiling and floor are also considered to be "walls" here). So you are going to have lots of modes in your room. OK, so obviously modes are not nice: they can make you hear some sounds too loud in your mix, since the room is adding its own resonance to some notes and not others. They also add a sort of "reverb" to some notes and not to others. Not good. It would be nice if there were some way to get rid of the modes in the room, but that simply is not possible: Modes are a direct consequence of having walls around your room. The only way to have no modes, is to have no walls! So you cannot eliminate the modes. But what you CAN do is to make sure that the modes are spread around evenly, across the spectrum. In other words, you want to make sure there are no large "gaps" where there are no modes at all, and you also want to make sure that you don't have several modes all piled up at the exact same frequency, or very close frequencies. You want to spread the modes around evenly. You do that by carefully choosing a "room ratio", which is basically just what it sounds like: It is the relationship between the dimensions of the room (length, width, height). You do NOT want any two of those to be the same, nor to be multiples of each other. So if your room is 2.5 m high, then you must make sure that it is not also 2.5m wide and 5 m long! If it were, then you'd have dozens of modes all piled up 34 Hz, 68 Hz, 136 Hz, etc., with huge empty gaps in between. That would be really bad. So the idea is to choose dimensions for your room that gives you an even spread of modes. What is important here is not so much the dimensions themselves, as the relationship or ratio between those dimensions. So you need a good ratio. Fortunately, several scientists have already figured out a bunch of good and bad ratios. You don't need to do figure that out all over again: they already did it, and wrote books about it. So you just need to choose one of those known good ratios that happens to be close to the dimensions of the space that you have available for your room, and then you adjust the location of the walls that you are going to build, in order to get as close as you can to a good ratio. The other thing you can do is to then treat the room with various kinds of acoustic "things" (such as absorbers, resonators and diffusers) in order to control the modes a bit, so that they don't have such a bad effect on the room. You can't actually kill the modes, but you can sort of "beat them into submission" a bit with good treatment. So your room needs to start out with a good ratio to have a good modal spread, and then it needs to have suitable treatment, to squash the modes a bit. That's all there is to it! That's what modes and ratios are all about, basically. - Stuart -

i know that we are supposed to finish the rooms with sheetrock on the frames..
Correct. The sheetrock can go EITHER on the inside of the the studs (room side) OR on the outside of the studs (wall cavity side). But NOT both.
how come some studio finish them with just the frame and fabric.. no sheetrock what so ever.
There is ALWAYS sheetrock (or some other form of mass). If not, then you don't have any isolation. It's that simple. In order to isolate, thre has to be mass. So what you are seeing in those "other studios" is either inside-out walls (like Brien said) or you are looking at the frames that have the acoustic TREATMENT on them, not the frames that have the ISOLATION on them. It can be two different things. Or you can combine it into one. Provided that you design it carefully, plan it carefully, and think it through BEFORE you start building frames... You can so the same for your room: you can either build inside-out and use the same frame for both isolation and some of the treatment. Or you can build separate frames for isolation and treatment. They are two different things, and you haven't shown us your design for the treatment yet (only for the isolation), so its hard to say what you can and cannot do, without first seeing your design. Maybe you should just post your entire SketchUp model here, so folks can have a look at it and see if you are getting things right. It sounds to me like there might be some issues with what you are planning. Post your model and we'll look at it. Also post some photos of the space, and what you have done so far. - Stuart -

Soundman202014 October 2010#46
Yup: Going from the "iso-booth" side through to the "control room" side, it would be like this: - 2 layers of 5/8" drywall - studs (new) - air - studs (from the existing wall) - air - studs (new) - 2 layers of 5/8" drywall Or if you are going to build inside-out, then it would be: - studs (new) - 2 layers of 5/8" drywall - air - studs (from the existing wall) - air - 2 layers of 5/8" drywall - studs (new) And of course, you'll have some insulation in there somewhere, too. But the basic idea is that you want ONLY two leaves in there, and a large air space between them. Each leaf is made from one for more layers of drywall, but you do not want more than two leaves. - Stuart -

Soundman202015 October 2010#50
Now I have 2 question.. What would be the situation if we can not remove the drywall on the control room side from that pre-existing wall?
That would be bad: That would leave you with little choice but to build a 3-leaf wall. You CAN compensate for the three-leaf problem by adding more mass to the leaves (more layers of drywall), or by increasing the depth of the air cavities between the leaves. It is possible, but not good, and not easy to calculate the MSM resonance for a 3-leaf system. Avoid if possible.
and if we are allowed to take it down.. Do we need to take down all the drywall from the complete wall (around the door framing too) or just in that section where the iso booth and control room are next to each other (iso booth is not the same length as the control room)
Probably just the parts that are in common, facing each other, and maybe a little extra. You could actually just punch very large holes in the drywall, if you don't feel like taking it all off. And "large" means "LARGE"!: As in a couple of square feet of hole in each panel, at least. As long as the air on either side of the inner "leaf" is well coupled through lots of large holes, that should do the trick. If this is a landlord issue, then why not just offer to commit in writing that you will put up new drywall on those studs when you eventually leave? If you are going to tear down all of your build when you leave, then nailing up a few sheets of drywall and painting it isn't going to be much extra work! - Stuart - - Stuart -
