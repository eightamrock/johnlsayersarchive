sharward17 August 2007#4
There may be some specially engineered products available at Mason Industries or Kinetics Noise Control to accomplish that, but you'd really need to ask yourself what kind of "flanking" you'd be trying to avoid. It probably takes more energy to vibrate your one foot concrete slab than the theoretical flanking path of your wall could ever dream to generate from airborn noise. Your priority should be to isolate the walls between rooms from each other. --Keith :mrgreen:

That was about 2 years ago, but as I remember it, the U-boats are designed with a "typical" load in mind. That is to say that unless one is going to know, within tolerance, exactly how much load is going to be on the room and that the load will be equally distributed, doing a lot of math to find out just exactly what the required spacing of U-boats is necessary for a particular application is a bit futile. To rephrase: Unless one has a room with static (never changing) load, then doing all of the calculations required to fine tune the spacing is pretty useless. Example: I build a room to be empty so that I can take advantage of the acoustics of the room (a live room in a studio - imagine that) then record (as in my case) a string quartet. The next day I bring in two full sized grand pianos. The next day I want to record a solo vocalist that weighs 100+ lbs. The loads are less than static. In order to compensate for every conceivable circumstance, you would have to be able to adjust the floor. With any elastic type device, be it springs, pucks, U-boats, etc., there is a reasonable tolerance. I described to them what my normal use for the room would be and they suggested the space needed between the U-boats. In my case it was very typical 16" spacing. Since their "model" was based on floors using 16" centers, with a load similar to mine, that is how I built my floors. Where the floor joists meet the walls, there are additional U-boats along the walls (I forget the exact spacing).
