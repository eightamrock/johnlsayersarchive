knightfly15 December 2005#2
First, you won't weaken your walls by cutting the floor; the wall's strength comes from the wall thickness, grade of concrete, amount of rebar inside, and the sizing of the footing the wall was poured on. These are all mandated by code and type of soil the building is on. However, you WOULD weaken the floor itself especially if it wasn't poured on a well-compacted base; there could be voids under the slab that aren't causing a problem right now because the slab is resting on the inner lip of your wall footing, and is all one piece. For what you want to do, I'd not even consider floating a floor; the lost headroom, major headaches, etc, just aren't worth it for a room that will see no live drums or bass. If you build out your separate inner walls as shown , use some type of sway braces to anchor the tops of new wall frames, and suspend your ceiling INSIDE those inner walls on either RC or RSIC clips, insulate properly and caulk everything the RIGHT way, you'll reduce the sound transmission to the upper floors enough for what you are doing. For one thing, getting a balanced mix is much easier when keeping the SPL's down to around 85 dB anyway; this is just a solid listening level, no blood pouring from the ears or anything :? HTH... Steve

Stan, if your floor is BONE dry you might get away with a butyl sheet (don't need any OTHER barrier) - butyl has roughly twice the damping ability of rubber or neoprene, so between that and any Constrained Layer Damping that takes place, it should lessen flanking from room to room - best would be if you ISOLATE each room's floor, just as if you were fully floating them, and put each room's single frame and single leaf on its own floor. A layer of particle board above the butyl will improve m-a-m on the whole thing, plus give a better surface for the laminated flooring. If you can tape a large piece of saran wrap to your concrete overnight, and you see ANY sign of moisture condensed on it the next morning when you lift it up, your floor is NOT dry enough. IF that's the case, then about the only way you might get away with that floor is to have a dehumidifier crunning 24/7, only turning it off during use of the rooms. Warm air drives any room moisture toward the cooler surface (the earth) so will try to drive moisture thru the butyl, which it can NOT do; as a result, the moisture would stay in the flooring and rot it. Keeping the overall RH below 40-42% would be mandatory, which all wooden musical instruments prefer anyway. cyeazel, your case is almost entirely different; you're talking about a concrete floor that's BETWEEN two habitable spaces, so moisture wouldn't be a problem (assuming both spaces are conditioned, that is) - so for your setup, I'd go for it (although IIRC, you've been talking about floated floors ANYWAY, right??!? (And you SHOULD be, if you still want your office people to not "fire warning shots to your head" as you climb the stairs... :? (1st drawing at top of post) It's not too bad for inner frames to touch intersecting inner frames, same with outer-to-outer - worst case it costs you 1 or 2 dB in total isolation, and not at all frequency bands - don't remember which end of the spectrum is worse, but either way it's negligible. What you DON'T want is for inner frame to touch OUTER frame at any point - this can cost you up to 20 dB of isolation at midrange, and up to about 6 dB @ 50 hZ. Another thing that's NOT shown in that drawing is backer rod; this is needed at all caulk joints to prevent early failure. See this http://www.johnlsayers.com/phpBB2/viewtopic.php?p=19989 for a pretty complete rundown... Steve

Stan, the answer is (1) - by putting your walls on top of particle board which is on top of the butyl, you're building sort of a "mini" floated room setup - I think you'd see a small improvement in room-to-room isolation that way, as compared to having both frames of your betwee-room walls resting on the same concrete (but with a thermal break material under each frame, of course) - What this will do is make it so any sound vibrations that "rattle" one frame must go thru particle board, butyl, concrete, butyl, particle board before they can directly rattle the OTHER frame. (This won't change the coupling you get thru the air/insulation gap, but it should reduce flanking AROUND that gap... Steve
somalade2 June 2006#15
Just wanted to make a quick comment on the following:
knightfly wrote:
First, you won't weaken your walls by cutting the floor; the wall's strength comes from the wall thickness, grade of concrete, amount of rebar inside, and the sizing of the footing the wall was poured on.
In many cases it is probably safe to say that the wall won't be affected by cutting the slab, but this does depend on how the wall was designed. In the case of a retaining wall, there will be a large horizontal thrust at the bottom of the wall due to the soil pressure acting on the face of the wall. This load needs to be resisted somehow. In most cases the footing for the wall will be buried sufficiently in the ground so that it is 'keyed' in and can't move. But in some cases, the design will rely on either friction on the base of the slab, or in the case of a basement, the slab can be used to hold apart the base of opposing walls. In both of these cases, cutting the slab to isolate it can affect the stability. The result isn't likely to be catastrophic - but you could find that the base of the retaining wall moves in by the thickness of the sawcut, which will negate the benefit of isolating the slab in the first place. Becasue of this, before cutting existing slabs in cases like these, you should first seek the advice of a structural engineer.
