sharward10 February 2007#2
Congrats on a nice intro to your project, RedWallStudio. :) Drawings or photos will be nice at some point, but for now, I think I can offer you this. Neither of your two options for addressing your common wall with your neighbor is ideal. While it seems you've got a good basic understanding of decoupling, you seem to be missing the overall mass-air-mass equation of a two leaf system. What you've described to me are either three or four systems. :( Ideal: lots of mass, followed by lots of air, followed by lots of mass. That's two leaf. Each mass is a leaf. Multiple layers of drywall without air between them constitute a single leaf. That's the bull's eye of the target. Sometimes you can't achieve that due to circumstances beyond your control, but you should strive for that whenever possible. The concrete block wall is one leaf. (This is true even if it's hollow... In the past there had been discussion of a hollow concrete block wall being considered two leaves, but that has since been dismissed.) The drywall on the neighbor's side is another leaf, even though there are most likely fusion points between those leaves. The drywall on your side is another leaf. That's three so far. One of your proposals calls for adding resilient channel (RC) to your side of the drywall and then adding more drywall to it. That would be four leaves with a very small amount of air between the two leaves closest to you. That's very bad. Don't underestimate the power of the "air" in the mass-air-mass equation. The other proposal of yours calls for erecting a new wall frame parallel to the existing one and adding drywall to the face of that frame. That's better, but you'd still have four leaves. :evil: You'd be better off removing all the drywall on your side of the garage and building a new frame several inches away from the existing concrete block wall. The larger the gap, the better. Leaving an 8 1/2" gap between inside edge of 3 1/2" framing will yield you 12" of gap from inner surface of drywall to concrete block wall. More is better, but the bang for your buck starts to drop off past 12". If you do that, you'd still have that third leaf on your neighbor's side to deal with. :roll: You're probably not going to have any influence on that. Perhaps ideal would be to have the neighbor's drywall and frame removed and the concrete block wall rendered with stucco to increase its mass and eliminate the third leaf... But I'm guessing that's not going to fly! :lol: You'll probably have to just accept that you have a third leaf and attempt to compensate for it by extra air and extra mass on your side. Consider a third layer of drywall on your new wall frame, or perhaps some Green Glue between the layers, or both. As for maintaining a functional garage door, all I can say, is YIKES. :roll: I honestly can't see any chance for that. :( I have yet to see a design for a functional sectional garage door in a studio where isolation is a high priority. Being in a townhouse, I'm thinking you may be under some restrictions that would govern the extent to which you can convert your garage to living space. I recommend you review the "Permits, Codes, Licenses -- and WHY YOU SHOULD CARE!" thread, and add to that verification that you don't have any [url=http://www.realtor.com/BASICS/condos/ccr.asp[covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs)[/url] that would prohibit you from doing what you want to do. I hope that helps you get moving in the right direction... --Keith :mrgreen:

DO NOT BELIEVE A WORD ON THEIR SITE. I don't have the energy to debunk the crap on their site today. :-( You get substantial benefit by increasing the distance between your leaves. Keep studying here to see more documentation and examples of that. Grab yourself a cup of coffee and get cozy with these documents:
  • IR761: Gypsum Board Walls: Transmission Loss Data
  • IR832: Sound Insulation of Load Bearing Shear Resistant Wood and Steel Stud Walls
Both of these reports are published by Canada's National Research Council, Institute for Research in Construction (NRC-IRC). Compare the graphs that demonstrate transmission loss. The higher the lines, the higher the soundproofing. Low frequencies are very difficult to soundproof, and those are the frequencies with the greatest improvement by increasing the depth of the gap between the leaves. Have you purchased Rod's book yet? ;-) Finally, you mentioned "handful of db" as though a small number of decibels doesn't make a difference. Depending on the frequency, a 6 db reduction is equivalent to a 50% perceived reduction in sound, and a 3 db reduction is distinctly recognizable... So don't be so quick to dismiss the power of "a handful of decibels." --Keith :mrgreen:

sharward11 February 2007#7
One thing you need to come to understand right away is that a wall system performs as a system, so while a concrete block wall may not be very good at stopping sound on its own, it can be quite good when it's a part of an overall system -- ideally two leaf, but in your case, probably three. I think by having a good heavy and thick multi-layered gypsum framed wall a foot or so away from the concrete block wall you share with your neighbor will perform very well. It's that garage door that's got me worried... So much so that I wonder if this thing may be finished before you even get started. :( Understand that if you have outstanding isolation on your ceiling and walls, but terrible isolation at the garage door, you will have terrible isolation, period. We sometimes call that the "weakest link" effect. You can plug four holes in your leaky bucket, but if you still have one hole left, you still have a leaky bucket. What's worse is that, contrary to logic, that bucket will leak about as much as it did before you plugged the other four holes. :( I consulted an acoustic engineer here in Sacramento when I was still in my design stage about a year and a half ago. He relayed a story of a drummer who built a soundproof room and did a really good job on his walls but he neglected his ceiling, and as a result he was sued by a neighbor. The engineer (who was not involved in the design of course) was an expert witness in his defense. He lost the case. The engineer told me he usually wins cases but he lost this one because of that darned ceiling not being addressed. Weakest link. It's real. Rod's book will be a great read for you. I'm sure you'll be posting your prasises on the "Rod's New Book" thread soon enough. ;-) --Keith :mrgreen:
gullfo11 February 2007#8
another thought comes to mind as well. have you put a drummer and bass player in your garage and done some measurements? maybe have a couploe of hard hitting band members pop over and do some testing. talk to your neighbor and set up a time when you can get access to his place as well as yours (and the outdoors), get a decent sound level meter, and makes lots of notes while your friends play all out for 15-30 minutes or so. the reason for the drum and bass test is to allow you to find the problem areas rapidly. generally speaking the low end and low-mid will penetrate the walls and floors really easy whereas the guitar and upper range stuff will tend to be easier to isolate. you want to find all the areas where low end is getting through. for example, if your townhouse is over the garage, you might get some loss of isolation through your place and then into the neighbors place. it might not all be just the garage where you find sound coming through... listen to the garage floor in your neighbors place as well - have someone tap with a hammer and see if your garage spaces share the same slab (you can actually do this sooner than the drum and bass test). If they share the same slab it might be a problem. then again, after the drums and bass test, you might find that in the neighbors garage its Xdb loud, but in their home its hardly noticable. this may help in determining the level of effort you need to go through to keep your neighbor happy. once you have the sound levels mapped out and some drawings and photos, people should be able to assist more effectively.

sharward12 February 2007#10
I hate to burst ypur bubble, but it's not possible to do the kind of soundproofing you need in a manner that is not "permanent" and that would allow you to circumvent the municipal planning/permitting process. Go visit your local building department and find out what kind of garage improvements you're legally allowed to make sans permit, and I'm willing to bet it's limited to floor coatings and cabinetry. Also, typically noise abatement ordinances measure noise levels at property line, and inside the building if it is a multi-family dwelling. I'm almost certain that noise levels exceeding legal limits as measured in a neighbor's garage would be in violation. Look up the language of the noise ordinances in effect where you live, including your CC&Rs. You'll most likely find that your burden is greater, not less, than you thought. I hate being such a downer on this project -- I just want to be sure you don't spend lots of money, only to land yourself into a situation where you have neighborhood strife and/or legal problems because of faulty assumptions. --Keith :mrgreen:

A couple of comments: 1. First, I would highly recommend that you get to know your neighbours and give them an idea of what you'd like to do in your garage (i.e. approximate times of rehearsals, # of times per week, etc). If you get them involved at the design stage, then they're less likely to want to call the authorities on you. Also, this will give you an idea of what neightbour is most likely to be the one to call the city. At any rate, establishing good ties with the neighbours is probably the most cost effective thing you can do. 2. Your drummer will need both supply and return air....remember it's an air-tight structure and you can't rely on cracks and gaps to allow air to find its way back to your furnace/air con unit. Yes, you can put baffles in the HVAC..I built some and described em in another thread. I'm not sure how well they work, but the shouldn't hurt, and they don't impede airflow. 3. If you're going as far as having an isolated drum room and using headphones, why not just buy a set of V-drums, and not bother with the isolated drum room. It would probably cost about the same (if not cheaper) by the time you add the cost of mics/preamps to get the drums into the monitors of the other musicians, plus will offer way better communication that thru a window. 4. Is it possible to build a wall behind your garage door...i.e. retain the cosmetic front of the garage door, but behind it is a proper wall? Or, are you trying to keep this space useable as a garage?
