Hi "aramism". Please read the forum rules for posting (click here). You seem to be missing a couple of things! :) But to answer your question: It depends on what the purpose of those panels is, and what the basic design philosophy of the room is. However, before getting into that, you have a much more serious problem: Your room is square! :shock: Since both the length and width are 12.5 feet, all modes in the 0,x,0 direction will line up perfectly with all modes in the x,0,0 direction, meaning that you will have huge modal response issues at all frequencies where the wavelength coincide with the room dimensions. For 12.5 feet, that means major modal issues at 45 Hz, 90 Hz. 135 Hz, 180 Hz, etc., all the way up the scale, and those are just the axial modes. The same thing happens with the tangential modes at 68 Hz, 136 Hz, 204 Hz, etc. And since the axials line up almost perfectly with the tangentials at 135 Hz, that is going to be a major problem. There's a type of graph called a "Bonello Plot" that shows how good or bad a room's modal distribution is. Here is the one for a room with your dimensions:
Image not preserved: square-bonello.jpg
As you can see, a good room shows a smooth curve. A bad room shows a dip in the curve. A really bad room has two or more dips in the curve. Yours has two. So before going any further with treatment, I'd strongly, strongly suggest that you back up a few steps and change the basic room dimensions to make it usable for mixing. As it is, there's no way you'll be able to get usable low-end acoustic response in that room. You need to change one of the dimensions to make that space usable as a mixing room. This cannot be fixed by any amount of treatment inside the room: it is an artifact of the room dimensions themselves. Since your contractor seems to know a bit about acoustics, it is surprising that he didn't spot this: it is a major flaw in the room design. But anyway, after you fix that, there is the issue of how to treat the room. A room with those dimensions needs a total of 428 sabins of absorption to bring it within ITU and EBU specs for a control room. That implies 428 square feet of perfect absorption. As near as I can tell from what you say, your treatment is as follows: Corners: 2x4 = 8 ft2, x 4 corners = 32 ft2 Clouds: 2x8 = 16 ft2, x 2 clouds = 32 ft2 Skylight: 4x4 = 16ft2, x 1 skylight = 16 ft2 Side walls: 8x2 = 16ft2, x 10 panels = 160 ft2 Total: 240 ft2 So even with all you are doing, you still only have half of the absorption that you need for that room. Nowhere near enough, especially considering the major modal issue you will have (those corner traps are much too small to do much for that). So your builder is just plain wrong on this: you need much MORE absorption in that room, not less! The equations do not lie. Not only that, but the Sabine equations assume "perfect" absorption, but there is no such thing in real life: OC-700 series absorption is good stuff, but far from perfect. Take a look at the published specs:
Image not preserved: 703-specs.jpg
As you can see, even the veritable Holy Grail of 4" OC-703 is far short of being a perfect absorber across the entire spectrum. It is really good, yes, but not perfect. so you actually need MORE surface area of absorption than the equations say. You also need to balance it across the spectrum. Since the panels do not absorb the same for all frequencies, and since ITU specs don't call for that anyway, you need to place the right amount of absorption for each frequency band. Lows need a huge amount of absorption, mids a lot, and highs much less. So you need to balance that, by properly installing the correct type of absorption at the correct locations in the room, and suitably covering bass traps with limp membranes to reflect highs while absorbing lows. Now, since your room is small, and since it has major bass resonance issues, let's consider that while looking at what your contractor is proposing: He says you should use 1" 703 or 1" of 705. Look at the specs for that. 1" 703 has an absorption coefficient of .17 at 125 Hz. 1" of 705 has an absorption coefficient of just .02 at 125 Hz.! In other words, they would do practically nothing for you major bass issues. He also suggests using a 1" air gap, but that changes hardly anything at all. It is basically the "A" mounting method shown in the chart above. To get any real benefit from an air gap, it needs to be big enough to have an effect on the wavelength in question. The "E-405" mounting method show in that table does make a big difference, since that is a 16" air gap (405 mm). A 1" air gap will have some effect, but very little. In any event, as you can see from the chart, 705 is not much use for bass trapping, with or without an air gap, and bass is where your issues are, undoubtedly. 1" of 703 flat against the wall still beats 1" of 705 mounted the same way, across the entire spectrum! 705 is fine for highs, but not for lows. It is simply too dense to be any use for low frequency absorption. Even 701 beats 705!
5) the entire rear wall has spaced out RPG qrd diffusors
Sorry, but those are totally uncalled for, and make no sense is such a small room. Numeric sequence diffusers can only be used in rooms that are large enough for the lobing artifacts to smooth out, and yours is nowhere near large enough. Of course, diffuser manufacturers don't bother to tell you about that, but if you check their very own technical data, you'll find out that you need to have AT LEAST 10 feet between a diffuser and the ears of anyone who is doing critical listening. And the distance could be greater than ten feet, depending on what frequencies the diffuser is tuned to. So forget those diffusers: they are doing more harm than good, and they are taking up the space where you need major absorption. You could easily get another hundred square feet of absorption on that rear wall, right where it is needed, and it can be thick, deep absorption, spaced away from the wall.

No, because that is irrelevant to the issue. Thickness by itself has no effect on sabine equations. It only matters indirectly. What matters, as I said, is that you need a total of 428 square feet (surface AREA, not thickness) of perfect absorption to get that room under control. Note what that says, carefully: "SQUARE FEET OF PERFECT ABSORPTION". "Perfect absorption" means that all frequencies are absorbed totally, completely, and evenly, 100%. There is no such thing in real life. The only "perfect absorber" is an open window facing infinite empty air on the other side. That0s why acoustic manufacturers publish charts, tables and graphs that show how close to "perfect" their products are. Thicker absorption is generally a bit closer to "perfect" than thinner absorption, since it absorbs a lager amount at low frequencies. Thin absorption does not do that, so it is less perfect. But none of that changes the total amount that you need in your room, since that is expressed as square feet of absorber that is directly facing the room. If you put another layer behind the layer that is facing the room, you have NOT increased the total surface area: all you have done is made it a bit more perfect, so it absorbs better at lower frequencies. In other words, you improved the quality, but did not change the quantity. No matter how thick it is, you still need 428 square feet of surface area facing into the room. And since real-world absorption is no perfect anyway, you actually need much more than 428 square feet.

The plywood is not there so you can add more insulation! It is there to try to help break up your terrible modal problems just a little bit. In effect, a thick, heavy sheet of plywood interrupts the path of the sound waves around the room, and re-directs them slightly, just enough to make a small difference. Your cloud basically works in the vertical direction, but your modal issue are in the horizontal direction, which is why you need to tilt that cloud down at the front quite a bit, so there is at least some angle on it, into the horizontal. :shock: Ooops! Sorry! I should have warned you about that! There are techniques for doing horizontal superchunks: I should have pointed those out for you. BUt it sounds like you got there anyway. One thing I would do on all of your corner traps is to put a piece of plastic across the front, before you put the cloth cover on. Use ordinary 6 mil plastic, the kind that contractors use inside walls to make vapor barriers, and you can find on big rolls in Home Depot. So staple a layer of that to the framing across the front face of the trap, then put the cloth cover on. There are two reasons for that: one is acoustic, the other is practical. Acoustically, it reflects some of the high frequencies back into the room, so you don't lose too much of that (keeps the frequency response of the room more even), and second, it keeps the fine fibers inside. Over time, the insulation will shed many tiny little fibers that can get into your equipment and damage things like faders and pots, and also block air filters and gum up cooling fans. So keeping those fibers inside the traps is a good idea!
