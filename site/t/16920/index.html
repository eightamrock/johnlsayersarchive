Soundman202031 August 2011#4
Hi Mark. Welcome! Simple answer: yes, you do have a triple leaf system, and like Brien said, you have the worst case for that system, since your thinnest layer is in the middle. Hard to figure exactly, since you have different surface densities on each leaf, but that assembly probably has a resonant frequency somewhere in the range of 120 Hz., meaning that it is basically transparent to sound up to about 170 Hz, and doesn't isolate well until around 240 Hz. If you would have removed the center pane, the story would be rather different: Resonance at 50 Hz, isolation starting at 70, good isolation above 100. So if you can figure a way to do so, you should take out the center pane.
I am trying to confirm if that is still true when the leafs are of varying thicknesses (particulary when the middle leaf is only 1/8 inch thick).
Yes, the rule is still true. Having different thicknesses doesn't change anything, except that it makes it harder to calculate the overall result! For panes of the same thickness and same spacing, it's not that hard, but with different thicknesses and spacing... well...
I am planning to turn my attention to the walls
For proper isolation, the entire room should be done to the same level: all six sides (four walls, floor, ceiling). Low frequency sound is not directional, and simply wraps around objects that are smaller than its wavelength, such as walls, houses, etc. So just putting one wall in its way is not useful: it will still get you by going around that wall as though it wasn't even there, and getting in from another angle. In order to isolate effectively, you have to do the entire room to the same level. The question is: What level of isolation do you need, and how much do you already have? What are you trying to accomplish? - Stuart -
Soundman202031 August 2011#5
I guess my question is should I leave it as is or would I be better off removing the internal 3rd leaf and beefing up the middle leaf with a thicker pane?
If you are going to remove the middle leaf, then don't replace it with anything! Leave it off! You could always keep the glass stored away somewhere, and put it back if you ever leave and have to restore the place to its original state.
That would only result in approximatley a 2 inch air gap.
Exactly. That thin gap is your enemy. The general recommendation is to never have an air gap thinner than about 4 inches, and that is for walls where you also have insulation in the cavity. For windows, it's best to keep it over 6", if you need good low frequency isolation. For most cases, with gaps above 4" there's a fairly linear relationship between mass and air gap: If you have to halve the air gap you can double the mass to compensate, or vice versa- But below 4" that linearity decays, and you have to add ridiculous amounts of mass (glass) to compensate for reduced air gaps.
I'm just trying to create the best possibile solution without removing the original window if at all possible.
Your other option is to increase your air gaps on both sides, and make both outer leaves as massive as you can afford. But it all depends on the rest of the structure: How are your walls built? Are they also decoupled MSM systems? - Stuart -
