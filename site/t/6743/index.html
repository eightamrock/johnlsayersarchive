The final wall is over 14 inchs thick; 8" concrete and 3" of polystyrene on each side. Not only does this make for a very "quiet" wall, it also provides R30 thermal performance. Here is my idea for suspending the ceiling. (see attached cross-section) Plan to use 1.5" nylon belt (seat belt) at 2' on center looped around a 2x4 stud and attached to the joist via a nailing plate, to suspend a gridwork ceiling. Then screw at 2' on center 5/8 sheetrock to the suspended gridwork. This will become the first layer of my suspended ceiling. Next will add a .5" fiber board sheeting. (adhesive caulked in place) screws ad hoc where needed to support weight. Final layer of adhesive caulk, then attach 5/8 sheetrock (alternate direction) and screw 2' on center to suspended 2x4. Make sense? Your thoughts? __ SUSPENDED CEILING CROSS SECTION ___ - 1st leaf is the upper floor/OSB subfloor and gypsum/fiber mud. - R-30 insulation plus air space fill the middle section. Approx 16". - Ceiling is suspended via the use of nylon seat belt straps. Sound transmission is min. if any. Approx load bearing 3500lbs. - 2nd leaf begins with 2x4 stud running perpendicular to floor joists. - 5/8 sheetrock --> adhesive caulk --> fiber board --> adh. caulk --> 5/8 sheetrock. - final tape, texture and paint on studio interior ceiling.

knightfly18 November 2006#13
Checked out polysteel a few years ago, understand they've gone away from their original design and now use a more uniform concrete thickness - still, in either form this is essentially a single leaf wall - only thing is, the foam parts are useless for sound proofing as they have very little mass - the only GOOD news is, they don't form a multi-leaf wall because they're bonded to the concrete on either side. For even better isolation though, this should still only be considered as one leaf in a 2-leaf wall - if your "WAF" is as important as you say, I'd forget the suspended ceiling approach (2 reasons - not as good isolation from above, and we don't know what the load rating of your basement ceiling trusses is, nor what's "left over" to use for supporting a ceiling. Odds are, unless you specified before construction that your floor trusses should be able to support an extra 10 PSF or more, they CAN'T. Builders tend NOT to give anything they're not asked for/paid for, so at most they would probably have inferred that you might want to put a lightweight (5 PSF or less) suspended ceiling downstairs... 3 layers of 5/8 rock weighs approx. 7.5 PSF not including any framing, so a complete grid system with 3 layers would run nearly 10 PSF added to whatever weight your existing upper floor applies to your trusses. Ceilings - separate is better for iso between rooms within the studio - it won't matter much for iso to the upper house, but might also be better in that respect - it won't be worse. In a nutshell, for best isolation in every direction you should think of your studio as a bunch of separate little "houses", each with it's own heavy, single-leaf/multi-layer walls and "roof", only tied together by flexible materials(sway brackets) - Also, if you want these rooms to SOUND good you need to NOT think like a carpenter in at least one way - carpenters tend to think in multiples of 4 feet when building, because sheet goods come in these sizes and it's cheaper to build in 4,8,12,16 foot increments with 8 foot ceilings. IF you build sound rooms that way, they'll make your life miserable trying to get them to sound good. For example, a 6x6 vocal booth with an 8' ceiling will sound like a box no matter what you do afterward; where a 5 x 7 vocal booth has 1 square foot less floor area, and with an 8 foot ceiling there are no two dimensions that are going to resonate at the same frequencies. Much better. (Yes, it's more of a PITA to build; never said it was gonna be easy :cry: ) Just some starting points; HTH... Steve
