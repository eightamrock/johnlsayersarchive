Soundman202020 June 2009#11
Well, the room is not small, but it isn't huge either, so it will need bass trapping. It also doesn't seem to be based on any known ratio: The inner leaf hard surface dimensions on your diagram give a ratio of 1 : 1.08 : 1.9, which comes up as being close to "A worst case scenario calculated by RPG", according to Bob Gold's calculator, and it fails 2 of the 3 tests proposed by Walker, Hoeg and Christensen (EBU paper). Between 24 Hz. and 94 Hz. the modes of the room itself are the dominant acoustic characteristics, and there are a total of 21 modes below 100 Hz, (and about one hundred modes below 150 Hz.), so yes, I'd say that bass trapping is important in such a room. The Bonello diagram for those dimensions is none too encouraging either. Or did you use another ratio as the basis for the room? I just calculated the above based on what I see in your diagram. You say that the room "seems" to have even bass response, but how did you determine that? I mean, is that just from subjective listening with ears, or did you actually measure it objectively with something like ETF, and generate waterfall plots and accurate response curves, plotted at 1 Hz intervals in the low end? That will tell you if you need more bass trapping or not. If you already have those plots, could you post them here? I'd be interested in seeing them. I'm also confused about that large black triangle thing in the rear corner: I'm assuming that the black triangle IS a bass trap, right? But when I look at the dimensions of the room, it seems like that actually ISN'T a bass trap, and is just an angled wall, since you mark the black surface as the limit of the room (inner leaf hard finish surface). But that makes no sense, since if that is an angled wall, then it means that your room has practically no bass trapping at all, so I doubt that the room response would be very even. Could you clarify what that black triangle shape is? Angled wall, or large superchunk bass trap? - Stuart -

Soundman202020 June 2009#13
The rear right is a hard surface,
That's what I feared. So you don't actually have any real bass trapping in there at all, just a couple of inches of absorption on the walls, and that small thicker section on the left rear.
When I plug those numbers into Bob Gold's calculator I get a ratio of 1 : 1.44 : 2.53 ?? 22'10" x 13' x 9'
Well, according to the numbers on your diagram, the height of the room is 12 feet, not 9 feet (taken to the inner leaf finish surface). Even if you take into account that "angled blue shape" on the ceiling (what is that, anyway? Is that the "acoustic tiles" you mentioned?) the height of the room only gets down to 9 feet just a few feet from the front, so the average height is well over 9 feet, probably more like 10. I also noticed you said:
The floor is industrial carpet.
Why would you want to carpet the floor, if the ceiling is already absorbent? That will make the room too dead, most likely. The general rule is that opposing surfaces should have opposite treatment. So if you have an absorbent ceiling, you should have a reflective floor. Also, industrial carpet is probably doing unpredictable things to the acoustics. Do you have the acoustic specs for that specific carpet, to tell you what frequencies it absorbs best, and which ones it absorbs least, to take that into account for the rest of the treatment? In any event, it will only be affecting the highs, not the lows, so it is contributing to making the room "duller", leaving the bass boom in.
I've only done subjective testing so far, I hope to get more results as things progress.
That sounds like you are doing things backwards! You need to test the room BEFORE you can decide how to treat it! If you just put up treatment and then test the room, you won't know if you made things better or worse with the treatment! In fact, you should really have test it when it was just an empty shell, before putting any treatment in at all. As it is now, you really don't know what you ahve treated and what you have not treated, or if you made things better or worse!
