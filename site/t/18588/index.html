Okay, so you have: Intake 75cfm Exhaust 205cfm Difference -130cfm So your room is at negative pressure. The general advice on this is to have positive pressure in the room. This means that upon opening the door air comes out, meaning any dirt or debrit from outside is pushed away. If you have negative pressure, this is drawn into the room instead. Assuming you have built your room well then it should be 99.99% airtight, so having such a large input/output ratio is un-neccesary, and working the fans too hard. If you can, try your intake at full speed (150cfm), and your exhaust so that is slightly lower. The closer they are, the less of a pressure change you will feel when you close the door.

Soundman202015 October 2013#4
The cfm numbers I posted are at 0" and 0.1" static pressure for the 409cfm and 150cfm fans, respectively. Static pressures are higher than that, due to used of 4" duct and length of duct runs, bends, etc.
OK, but static pressure applies to the entire system, from intake register to exhaust register, not for individual segments, so both fans "see" the same static pressure. Therefore, the fans should be identical, not different, and should be run at the same speed, not different, regardless of how the duct is sized.
Would modifying the setup by enlarging the holes in the wall to 6" and using 6" duct help?
That would reduce air velocity and static pressure, which in turn would reduce the noise generated by air movement inside the ducts, but that won't fix the imbalance in fan volumes.
I figure the circular area of the duct would be approximately doubled, so wouldn't that allow me to run it at full speed with the same noise that I get now at half speed with 4" duct?
Duct size does not affect FAN noise. It only affects air-motion noise, which is the noise created by the air itself moving through the ducts, not the noise created by the fans. Those are two different aspects of HVAC noise. What you should do is first figure out how much air you need to be moving, in terms of "room changes per hour", based on room volume / occupancy, convert that to CFM, then figure out what size ducts you need to be able to move that volume of air at air velocity less than 300 fpm. Then you figure what the static pressure will be in those ducts, and based on that you look for fans that can provide the necessary volume of air (CFM) when faced with that static pressure load. - Stuart -

Soundman202017 November 2013#8
The solution is simple: Cut the flexduct at the entry point and exit point, take it out of the box, put in a couple of baffles, and glue duct liner all around the interior. This is the general idea of how silencer boxes should be built: viewtopic.php?f=2&t=1929&start=74 viewtopic.php?t=8425&start=2 viewtopic.php?f=1&t=11542&start=5 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=9761&start=0 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=11485&start=98 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=11508&start=157 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=13821 viewtopic.php?f=1&t=15378&start=44 viewtopic.php?f=2&t=18202&start=16 The flex duct is not doing anything at all positive for you, and as you have already discovered, it is doing bad things. If you make this into a proper silencer box, then the sudden dramatic change in cross section at the entry and exit creates a large acoustic impedance mismatch, meaning that sound cancels itself out to a certain extent as it tries to enter and exit the box. The air flow also slows down greatly as it enters, then speeds up as it exits, and move through the box at a much lower speed, creating far less turbulence and noise. The baffles force the air to change direction drastically a few times, wile blocking the sound, and the duct liner helps to keep things quite as well as damping any natural resonance inside the box. So you don't have to replace your "duct liner box": you only need to modify it so that it is a proper silencer box. - Stuart -

Soundman202018 November 2013#10
I'm not sure I have enough cross-sectional area in each half of the duct box to make it into a dual silencer box.
Just taking out the flex duct will more than double the cross sectional area. :) Do the math: The cross sectional area of your 6" duct is about 28 square inches (in2). You say your box 8-3/4" deep, and 16" wide. So you have 8.75 x 16 = 140 square inches of cross section in there, vs. 28 for the flex duct. :shock: You will lose 1" all around from the duct liner (it is 1" thick), leaving you with a an interior cross section that measures about 6-3/4" by 14" = 94.5 square inches. That's well over THREE TIMES the cross section of your flex duct, meaning that the air velocity will drop to less than ONE THIRD of the current speed. And since noise is exponentially related to speed, you get a LOT more than 66% reduction in noise created by the air flow itself, due the lower speed.... If you just did that alone, without any baffles, you'd get a pretty good reduction in noise. But with 35" of space vertically, you have space for two baffles, leaving about 9-1/2" x 6-3/4" of cross-sectional area 64 square inches in between baffles. That's still well over twice the area of the flex duct, and those baffles will help greatly to improve your isolation even more.
Also, what do I use to attach the baffles to the box?
Make the baffles from plywood, OSB, MDF or something like that. Screw and glue them in place, then put in the duct liner.
And where do I find duct liner, can I order it online?
And HVAC supply house will carry it. Google it for your area. You might find this useful: http://www.hvacagent.com/Suppliers.aspx?ssState=34 - Stuart -
