For the level of "sound proofing" you are seeking $1,000 is not a realistic budget, even a "starting budget". Try atleast $3,000 to $5,000. 1st $30 you should spend would be on Rod's book available in the stickies.
to me, it seems like the ceiling is the weak link and should be treated first. would it be better to add a layer of drywall and greenglue(or resilient channel) to the ceiling be a good solution to eliminating upward sound, or would it be better to frame out a drop to the ceiling and add drywall? would you recommend fitting all the walls with either method?
Well... I think you've got to make a real analysis of what you can build, what you're willing to build and how much money & time you are willing to spend to achieve your goal. Add a layer of drywall & green glue with or without RC will add to iso BUT the last pic you posted looks scary. Are those 2x4 ceiling joists on there side? If so or if not adding weight to a ceiling safely means calculating the dead-load of the ceiling before adding material. IF those ceiling joists are on side OR are over 7' 3" then I would consider the PITA of ripping them down regardless of constructing a "sound proof room". Do a search for an online span calculator. Also we measure "sound proof walls" with STC (for voice only) and then for music with TL @ a low frequency like 50Hz, again please do a search on this web site for definitions of those terms (if you've not already) Drop ceiling... Kinda... build a new inner ceiling that rests on inner walls, inner and outer walls and ceiling do not touch. Maybe: Rip out ceiling, determine and design how high of a new ceiling you can build going for as much and maybe more height then you already have. Maybe you can move a few pipes or build around them better. Maybe or maybe not rip out existing walls. If it's all concrete your good. Hopefully there is insulation in the stud bays of inner walls already. Before you put new ceiling in, beef up floor above via drywall in the joist bays attached to floor. We've nicked named this proccess as "beef up".
all of the doors in the room are hollow core. are doors like this totally useless with my goal in mind? can drywall or another material be added to add mass to the door, or would it be more beneficial to just buy new doors?
Just buy new or recycled solid core doors.
should i worry about adding insulation to the floors at this point? would it do the same to put the guitar amps off the ground? i've heard of building a floor with wood over hockey pucks, what about putting amps on hockey pucks?
NO insulation to floor. NO floating floor (over hockey pucks or anything else) Please do a search on this topic or look in Rods book. Guitar amp on a floated assembly really won't make a big diff on sound transmission through wall. Bass amp and drums floated do help.
am mainly looking for help in method. getting new doors and adding a layer of green glue and drywall seems like the way to go, but would it be sufficient in blocking out sound?
IF you're exisiting ceiling is OK to add weight to then MAYBE. I would not trust it though becasue if you were to build this way and it was not good enough you would run out of available dead load from existing joists, then the only thing to do would be to rip out what you've added, add beef up to joists bays under floor above, then rebuild inner ceiling. PLUS it sure does look like the existing inner ceiling is solidly attached to other structure in the basement. Making a sound isolated room requires a "room within a room" design where the inner and outer leaves/frames/wall are decoupled as much as possible. AND each room needs to be airtight... who knows how air tight your outer walls and ceiling/floor is until you rip it out. Airtight means you need to think about air exchange or not being able to breath with 3 or more people in the room. Also would be a really good and safe thing to do is research your local building codes for can and can't do and think stronly about getting a permit. Although viewed by some as a complete PITA, this will force you to plan out everything in advance avoiding possible design oversight/buidling mistakes and will require someone else to objectively look at your plan and work for safety of you, your family and neighbors. Marc

guitardad7228 December 2006#6
also, other than actual construction on the walls/ceiling, do things like bass traps have a big effect on cutting sound transmission?
NO. Anything that will make the room sound better via acoustic contraptions like bass traps will decrease the amount of iso you get from the walls/ let more more sound pass through. Some acoustic contraptions help sound pass through the wall a lot with a triple leaf effect like a slot wall and some acoustic contraptions effect iso loss very little like a cloud or DIY panel absorber. For bass traps iso loss depends on which type of bass trap you choose to build. When designing for acoustics, beefing up the MASS or AIR in iso wall construction around the acoustic contraptions (depending on contraption type and amount of iso loss you can live with) is the best course of action. Marc

Sharward, it could only be considered storage, not workshop because in order to "work" in a room it needs to be habitable (at least that's what Florida building code is). Another thing to note is that houses are sold all the time with non-permitted additions/work. Many workarounds for the sale are available and sometimes there are none. When I recently renewed my insurance there was a box to check IF owner had done an addition without permit/no inspection, meaning they would still insure the house with further inspection and PITA stuff to owner. Also had a friend who enclosed a patio without permit, sold the house, did not rip down the work and they got through the sale with modification to county record of lot... I do not know all the details, just know it was done. I am not an expert in home sales or legal stuff to that effect but it seems to me if the listing agent was smart he could have easily worded the sale contract to cover his and sellers butt for the non permitted addition. IF your building code is similar to mine and once you find out min height to make a room habitable for your local building code you might be able to rebuild the room with at least a good part of the room above min height. Not all of the room has to be above min height. In a finished attic space where the ceiling angles down at the sides the only part of the room that counts as habitable space is the area higher then min height. Thing is the room would still be habitable IF you have at least 70 square feet above the min height regardless of actual floor space. Sad thing is after looking at the picture you posted, it sure does look like the room could have been finished above 7'6". For whatever reason it was not done... I'll suspect it was part of the plan by who ever renovated the house to 3 condos to finish the basement BUT they did not pay attention to details as they renovated. Maybe the "new service" could have been installed better and out of the way of height of room. Maybe you could still move them the gain height, BUT messing with your neighbors water, electric or other service would at least be interesting if not impossible.
