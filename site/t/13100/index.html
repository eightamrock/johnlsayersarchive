Soundman202029 May 2009#4
Hi Ian. That place you linked to shows an excellent construction method for creating a three-leaf wall!!! That's a really, really bad idea... Three-leaf construction will, of course, make your isolation WORSE than it is already at the low end of the spectrum, and only improve the high end. That does not sound intuitive, since you think you are adding more mass to the wall, but in fact you are also re-tuning the resonant frequency of the entire wall to a higher frequency, and skewing the transmission loss curve, so at the lower end of the spectrum the wall will now transmit sound BETTER than it did before. It will only isolate better at high frequencies. You would be better off just adding the extra layer of drywall directly on top of the existing drywall, with Green Glue in between, and probably for about the same overall price. But even better would be to take off the existing drywall, put resilient channel across the existing studs, full the gap with mineral wool, then put the drywall back on again, then put another layer of drywall on top of that, once again with Green Glue sandwiched in between. However, that only treats one wall: you probably also have issues with your ceiling, floor and other walls. They will probably need similar treatment. Just treating one wall won't help much if sound is flanking around that wall through the ceiling or floor, for example, which it most likely is. You will probably also have issues with windows that you'll need to think about. - Stuart -

I didn't say "just add mass" I said "add mass plus Green Glue"! Big difference :) Green glue is not an adhesive: it is a special acoustic polymer that has been proven to have excellent sound isolation properties. Unlike the secretive manufacturers of that product that you linked to, the manufacturers of Green Glue have had their product actually tested by independent acoustical laboratories, and have published all of the results of those tests. It works, and works well. Next: It's not just the issue of the additional mass, but rather the issue of the air gap that you create between the new mass and the existing mass. This is a simple principle of physics: By adding a layer of mass over an air gap, you create a resonant system, which is implicitly coupled to (and part of) the existing resonant system behind it (the existing wall). Your new addition changes the resonant frequency of the entire system, driving it up, which is always a bad thing for low frequency sound isolation. It's good for high frequencies (speech), bad for low frequencies (music). That material that you linked to looks an awful lot like MLV (mass loaded vinyl) or something similar, but the manufacturer is very careful to NOT specify what it is made of, except to say that it is "acrylic". Perhaps because of the bad rap that MLV gets on places like this forum. He also does not publish an tests done on his product by independent labs. I wonder why? MLV is just mass, as is drywall. Mass is mass. Sound waves don't care if you make 100 kg layer of mass from bricks, MLV, wood, concrete, drywall or lead plate. It is all the same for the sound waves. They are not picky, and won't bother checking for the manufacturer's logo on their way through. They will react in exactly the same way to any kind of mass. Higher density helps from the point of view of space, since you can get more mass into less volume, but mass is what stops sound, not what the mass is made of. Sound waves don't care if you paid US$ 50 for the mass, or US$ 500, or US$ 5,000, or even US$ 50,000 for some exotic extremely dense material. They just see "mass", and that is what stops them. The issue is that, while MLV undoubtedly is mass, it is really EXPENSIVE mass! So if you have money to waste, then by all means, go ahead and buy it! But do realize that you could get the exact same mount of mass from another sheet of drywall, and also that by mounting ANY mass over an air space you have created a resonant system. On the other hand, if money is important to you, then drywall is really CHEAP mass, and is easy to install and work with. :) But to answer your original question: Mass law states that each time you double the mass of a sound barrier, you improve its transmission loss by 6 db across the entire spectrum. So just adding mass to the existing wall will improve the isolation by 6dB, regardless of what the mass is: If it is drywall with Green Glue underneath it, then you will get much better than that. But if you put an air gap underneath it, then physics says that you have raised the resonant frequency of the system, tilted the transmission loss response curve, and thus lowered low frequency isolation. You can compensate for that, but in order to do so you need... more mass! You can't trick the laws of physics.

Soundman202030 May 2009#8
and 6 db seems like almost nothing to me though so I'm wondering if its even worth it.
Bingo! :) That's the point I was trying to make. Mass law alone does not do much for you. You need a LOT of mass before you start getting substantial benefits. Which is why Green Glue is such a useful product. In fact, to get that 6 db increase, you need to double the mass. So since your wall already has one layer of drywall on each side (one layer on your side, one on the neighbor's side, you'd need to add TWO layers of drywall to get that 6 dB gain, which is definitely not worth the effort, or adding four extra layers will get you 12 dB. In other words, if all you use is drywall that it is not worth the effort, just like you said. However, Green Glue changes that. Adding just ONE layer of drywall with Green Glue in between, will get you 12 dB already (instead of about 4 dB). Two layers of drywall with GG will get you the 15 dB that you mentioned as being reasonable, based on your "knob twiddling test". To give you a better idea of the problem you wold be creating by following the instructions on that web site you linked to originally, here is some information on the reason why triple-leaf construction is such a bad idea, and some real-world graphs showing actual test results. The data comes from the National Research Council of Canada. http://www.greengluecompany.com/underst ... leLeaf.php Interesting reading! Take a look at the graph at the bottom of the page. The black line shows the high isolation you get from a good two-leaf wall. The green line shows just how much that changes if you make it into a three-leaf wall by adding more mass over a thin air gap. Their final comment summarizes it nicely: "Triple leaf ... constructions should be avoided like the plague. You will always get a lower level of sound isolation, and this loss may be most severe where you need performance the most – low frequencies." If you look at the diagrams of how those walls were built that gave the results shown in the graph, you will notice that the worst-case graph (green line) is for a wall built exactly like they tell you to on that web site you linked to. Like I said originally, following that advise would lead you to disaster! - Stuart -
