Soundman202013 October 2010#2
Hey Dub, Welcome! The good news is, you came to the right place! And the better news is that you came at the right time, in that you haven't built anything yet. Too often people arrive here after having already started, then learn that what they have done is no use and needs to be taken out again. You have lots of questions, and lots of other stuff worth discussing.
The projected size of the room-to-be is around 16-18 feet in length, about 9 feet high... about 9 feet wide,
Oops! Watch out for dimensions that are the same as each other, or multiples of each other. A room that is 18" long, 9" wide and 9" high is going to be REALLY bad, acoustically. Your room modes will all be piled up on top of each other, and you'll have serious resonant issues. Search the forum for "room ratios" and "room modes", then start reading everything you find, to get an idea of what this is all about. The long and short of it is that you want to avoid certain ratios of dimensions, as they will do bad things to your room. In your case you say you will have an sloped ceiling, so that would help to a certain extent, but it would also make the calculations an awful lot harder. Bottom line? Try to come up with better dimensions. By the way, the dimensions I'm referring to here are your final inner-leaf dimensions, meaning the hard surfaces of the isolation walls that you will see when you stand inside the completed room, before you start putting in the acoustic treatment.
and we're planning on having a raised floor...like by 3 inches.
Probably a bad idea! Why do you want to raise the floor? Any time you raise a floor, you create a resonant cavity underneath it, which will sing along with some notes and suck up others... not fun. Raising your floor also reduces your ceiling height: Height is your friend! Raising your floor 3 inches means that your ceiling is no longer at 9': it is now down to 8'9".... Since this is a garage, I'm guessing it has a concrete slab on grade floor: That's perfect! Don't mess with it. More on that later....
The drum kit will be on a separated (also built-from-scratch) drum riser, or at least its own carpet.
Drum riser is good (if built correctly). A patch of carpet is good. either will work fine. No need to raise the floor.
There's one pillar in the space that we'll have to build around, and the front end of the room is like an arrowhead-shape with a flat tip.
That will be a problem, but there are ways to work around it.
We had intentions of going with Quietrock within the frame, using Genie Clips, Silentseal,
Don't waste your money! Those products aren't BAD per sé, but they wont get you where you say you want to go cheaply, ad there are much less expensive ways of getting what you need. You say you want isolation (there is no such things as "soundproofing", by the way... :) ). In order to get isolation you need two things only: mass and decoupling. The more isolation you want, the more mass you need, so it makes sense to buy the cheapest mass you can get. Quietrock is not it. The more isolation you want, the better decoupling you need: Genie Clips wont get you far enough. We'll get back to that point...
The first question laying heavy on my head is in regards to the side of the room nearest to the already-framed wall of the inside of the garage. My dad had tossed the idea up to not frame that side at all, just cover it afterward with the drywall/Quietrock, or whatever it will be. Is this a good approach, or NO?
You mean the black colored wall with the stud framing visible? DON'T TOUCH IT!!!! It's great just like it is. Great as a base to start from, that is. Like it is right now is hopeless for isolation, but it is an excellent place to start. If you put drywall on those studs, you ruin any chance of getting good isolation.
What method of soundproofing (or combination of methods) is best to look at in my situation? For the walls, ceiling, floor...everything.
That's it, exactly! You got it perfectly right, without even knowing. What I mean is, that in order to isolate your room successfully, you HAVE to treat the "walls, ceiling, floor, everything", and you have to do it all to the same level. You cannot do one wall one way, the ceiling another way, and another wall a different way: They all have to be done to the same level. So do doors and windows, your electrical system, ad your HVAC system. Like I said before: there is no such thing as "soundproofing". You cannot stop sound. Any sufficiently loud sound will get through any conceivable barrier. Pop a hand grenade inside practically any "soundproof" studio, and I guarantee that you'll still hear it outside.... :) So you can't stop sound, but you can control it (sorry for stealing your punchline, Brien, but it's damn good!) The cheapest way to isolate a room is to design it as something called a "MSM system". MSM means "Mass-Spring-Mass". It's a simple principle of physics. I won't go into all the scary details yet (you'll learn about that later, as you design your room) but the basic concept is that you build another room inside the existing room, but not touching it. You put up new walls a few inches away from your existing walls, and you hang a new ceiling across the top of those new walls. No part of the "new" structure can touch any part of the "old" structure. That is called "decoupling". By doing that, you simply break all solid paths that sound could possibly travel through between the outer "wall" and the inner "wall", leaving only air between them. Air is a spring. The two walls are mass. Hence, mass-spring-mass. MSM. That's the basic idea. It's a little more complex than that, but not too much. The basic concept is simple: build one massive room inside another massive room, decouple everything, seal everything airtight, and you have major good isolation. Technically, the two "walls" are called "leaves", so you'll see lots of references here to your "inner leaf" and "outer leaf". The "outer leaf" is your existing wall (which needs to be beefed up a bit), and the "inner leaf" is the new wall that you have to build. Simple!
What kind of floor surfaces (once the room is done) are smart options for my situation, and which would be best to make my drums sound as "big" as possible? (I guess by that I mean more reflective sound.)
Simple answer: Plain old concrete is just GREAT for that! Don't touch it. Or if you don't like the looks of concrete, then just lay laminate floor on top of it. Keep your floor hard and reflective, and make your ceiling soft and absorbent (in places at least). Good drum sound.
What about electricity (outlets) and lighting? Like any of the questions below, this involves holes and such...so I'm really at a loss of how amending the holes works so not to defeat the efforts in soundproofing.
The best idea for power is to bring it in at one single point, then distribute it and surface mount everything inside the room. If you don't like that idea then you can still poke holes in your precious MSM wall if you want, but you will have to seal up those holes carefully, and replace the lost mass around them. It can be done, but it is a potential weal point. Best avoided if possible.
What kind of door do you recommend?
A really, really thick, really, really heavy, very solid one! Or better still, two of those, back to back. One in each leaf. And carefully sealed airtight. At least two independent seals on each, preferably three.
What do you recommend for the best insulation? Dense insulation, I assume...
Nope! Not too dense. Use mineral wool or plain old pink fluffy fiberglass. If you go with density that is too high, your low end isolation will suffer (things like kicks, toms...) If you go with density that is too low, your high end will suffer (things like cymbals....). That doesn't sound intuitive, and seems to be backwards, but practically nothing about acoustics is intuitive, so don0t sweat it. What you want is around density of 30-40 kg/m3 for fiberglass, or a bit more if you use mineral wool: around 40-50 kg/m3. Don't go much higher, or much lower.
Ventilation! It's a must-have...and I'm sure it begs for meticulous work so not to have sound exiting so much. That's always been a concern hanging over my head.
Yup! People have this bad habit called "breathing". They need to do it a few times per minute, so you do need to get fresh are into your room, and stale air out. This is especially important here, considering that each leaf of your room is a very massive and totally hermetically sealed barrier, while in between them is a huge amount of thermal insulation. You need HVAC. In order to make that work without losing isolation there are many things you have to do, such as line your ducts on the inside, make them very long, put multiple 90° bends in them, and install silencer boxes: You also need to dimension your air conditioning equipment correctly so that it moves the right amount of air at the right speed, and dehumidifies it to the right degree....
The framing will be done by my uncle who is a professional framer/carpenter. He is actually coming this weekend, possibly with the materials to frame, if *we* are ready for him.
This might seem harsh, but no, you aren't anywhere near ready for him to frame anything for you. You don't have a design yet: Not even a basic plan. And you were about to do a lot of things wrong, until you came here. You were about to shoot yourself in the foot! (no offense!) I'd suggest you should first take the time to learn the concepts, then design your room, and only then invite your uncle over to start framing it. Your uncle will also need to learn some new techniques, since what you need to do is a bit different in some aspects from what he normally does. To put some perspective on what you are trying to accomplish: it is basically the very hardest type of isolation! You want to isolate acoustic drums so you can play them any time, day or night, without the neighbors either calling the cops, or firebombing your garage. Drums are the hardest instrument of all to isolate. An average kit played averagely can easily put out 110 dB, and more like 115 db without breaking a sweat. You need to get that down to something like 40 dB (or whatever your local code of municipal bylaws specify) at your neighbor's fence. So you need something like 70 dB of isolation. that is a lot. An average house wall will give you maybe 30 dB of isolation. Going from 30 to 70 is NOT "about twice as much", which is want most people think. In fact, it is "about ten thousand times as much". I'm not joking: 70 dB of isolation really does mean that you have to stop 10,000 times as much energy as for 30 dB. Simple physics. Each time you go up 10 dB, you multiply the energy by a factor of ten.... And we didn't even mention your single biggest weak point in your entire build yet: your garage doors... Those are probably giving you more like 10 dB of isolation right now, maybe 15, with luck... So you need, realistically, about a million times more than you have right now, quite literally. It can be done, but it needs lots of mass, excellent decoupling, total hermetic sealing, and very careful planning. Plus it needs very careful planning. Oh, and did I mention that it needs careful planning? And don't forget to plan carefully, too. :) I'm not trying to scare you off: just laying out the ugly truth for you: You CAN get where you want to be, but it won't be as easy as you thought. The absolute cheapest and best way to do it is as I outlined above: room-in-a-room, two massive leaves separated by large air gap, using ordinary building materials, such as 5/8" drywall, 4" OC 703 insulation, and 2x4 or 2x6 studs. I'd suggest that you spend some time reading Sharward's build thread, here: viewtopic.php?f=10&t=2921&p=20903#p20903 That will give you an idea of what is involved in doing what you want to do. That's a really long thread, and will probably take you several days to read, but it will be well worth your while, I think. He is doing exactly what you want to do. - Stuart -

That's the basic idea. Another big thing that takes a while for most people to get their head around, is that room isolation and room treatment are two entirely different and in fact opposite things. The stuff you use to treat your room acoustically is really lousy for isolating it, and the stuff you use for isolating it is really lousy at treating it. In other words, stuff like fiberglass insulation and acoustic foam does nothing at all to stop sound. It sounds counter-intuitive that something that is absorbing sound is actually not doing anything at all to stop sound, but that's the way it is. There are a lot of things about acoustics that just aren't intuitive. So, your first order of business is to isolate your room. THEN you treat it afterwards. Isolating it will make it sound bad. Treating it will make it sound good again. Isolation makes your room sound bad, because it stops the sound from getting out. If the sound cannot get out, then it stays inside, bouncing arouuuuuuuuuundddddd..... Think of it this way: with both heads on your kick drum and no holes, that thing resonates loud and long. But fill it with towels and leave the front head off.... It sounds a little different! When you isolate your room, in effect you are making it into a kick drum with both heads on, and nothing inside. The sound stays inside, bouncing around. OK, that's not a brilliant analogy, but you get the idea. So the idea is to design the isolation such that it does the least "bad stuff" possible to your room. That's where the ratios come in. Just like you can make a drum sound bad or good by tuning it, and control resonance in various ways, so to you can "tune" your room, but not in the same way as you tune drums, of course! It works like this (simplified explanation): You probably already know that for each frequency there is an associated wave "length", meaning the distance between the peak in one cycle of the wave and the same peak in the next cycle. For the frequency where that distance happens to fit in between two walls of your room exactly, the room will resonate. So if you have two walls in your room that are 16 feet apart, then any time you play a note of 35 Hz, that wave will fit perfectly between the two walls, and you have a "mode". The same happens at 70 Hz, because two waves fit in. And 105 Hz, because 3 waves fit in. Etc, all the way on up the scale. For every harmonic of 35 Hz, the room itself will resonate in between those two walls. You will have "room modes" at every multiple of 35 Hz. Basic physics. So let's say your room that is 16 feet long, is also 8 feet high (for example). You probably see the problem already. If two wavelengths fit into 16 feet, then clearly one wavelength will fit into 8 feet. So now every time you hit 70 Hz, you are going to activate TWO room modes: the one where two waves fit into 16 feet down the length of your room, plus also the one where a single wave fits in vertically: So now the room resonates twice as loud for 70 Hz. OK, lets say your hypothetical room is also 8 feet wide... Now you have the room booming away fantastically THREE TIMES as loud, every time you hit 70 Hz. But if you hit a note at 80 Hz, the room does nothing: it does not respond, because an 80 Hz wave doesn't fit: it dies, fast. But if you could make the room 13 feet wide (instead of 8) then now you have a mode at 80 Hz too, and you lost one of your modes at 70 hz. Good! Obviously, modes are not nice: You want to choose dimensions for your room where the modes for the length do not line up with the modes for the width, and neither of those lines up with the modes for the height. You want to choose dimensions that "smooth out" the modes, so that they are spread evenly around the spectrum, with out any of them lining up, and also with no big gaps between modes. So far so good. But there are other ways that waves can bounce around a room. The above is just for waves that bounce back and forth between two parallel walls (axial modes), but they can also bounce around between any four walls (tangential modes) and even between all six walls (oblique modes). So it gets complicated... That's the basic idea (yeah, I know: half waves, not full waves, etc. Just trying to keep the explanation simple). Hence, "ratios". Scientists have already figured out what the good and bad ratios are, so you don't have to do it. Just look at the ratio that you already have (or rather, then one you would have for your inner leaf with your current design), find a "good" one that is fairly close to that, and "nudge" your dimensions a bit to get even closer. Simple. You don't need to go overboard, down to the last 64th of an inch: just get in the ball park. You might think that you can "cheat", and put your walls at angles so that the modes go away: they don't, unfortunately. They just move to different frequencies and different places in the room, and they get harder to calculate, but they are still there. There are other reasons why you might want to splay some walls, but getting rid of modes is not one of them. Some folks seem to think that they can simply put egg crates on the wall, or carpet on the ceiling, to somehow "confuse" the modes, or absorb them, or something, but that doesn't work either: modes are a fact of life. You cannot kill them. All you can do is stop them piling up and spread them around the spectrum evenly by choosing a good ratio, and you can take some of the "bight" out of them with abundant bass traps in your room. OK, that's the end of Acoustics 101 for today! :)

"lite" and "soundproofing" are two words that just do not go together! They are natural enemies: you can't even put them in the same room... :) Isolation needs mass, period. If it is "lite" then it has little mass, so it wont isolate. There are no magical materials that can defy the laws of physics (despite what you might read on some manufacturer's web sites! :shock: ) The only way to stop sound, is to put something in its way that it cannot move. If you want to isolate well, you can't do it "lite". And the reverse is also true: If you only do it "lite" then it won't isolate well.
