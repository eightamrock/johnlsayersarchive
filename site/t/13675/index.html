While I never saw this measured, I have serious question marks about this. Mostly or often in the packaging I know this is not rarely (rather strong) shrink foil meant to keep these low density packages compressed and strong enough for handling and transport. The analogy which is made here that lightweight foil indeed insulates badly for low frequencies while better for highs resulting in reflected highs at the surface and low frequenties absorbed by the wool in the package. Basically this is just an application of the mass-law. But this assumes that this foil can freely (in a certain degree) can vibrate. Low frequencies call for larger displacement and longer wavelengts. I have made extensive tests in an official univ lab with perforated steel sheets (with all kinds of perforations) on mineral wool with and without very thin foil between them. And here I got complete deviating results. When this foil is clamped between the wool and the perforated sheet, compared to the same tests without the foil, the effect of the foil is the reverse of one should expect, namely the low frequent absorption diminishes much more than the high frequent absorption. What we are talking about here is not just the mass, but the apparent mass. Hence the analogy of just foil, with foil stretched around something is a poetic license which can be true or not depending of how such package is strapped in this foil. I know that such things get their own life on the net and become axioms, but it doesn't mean that they are true or in which degree they are true or not. Often such things establish based on (over)simplified analogies.

Eric_Desart11 October 2009#9
brandondrury wrote:
I have to admit that I never considered for a second that the plastic may have an effect on low frequency absorption.
Don't worry. Even most acousticians have never considered this. What you find in most books is that's it's never confirmed. I never found my related indisputable and systematic measurement results anywhere described. The idea behind it is the following. Plastic foil is NOT acoustic transparent, it's a sound barrier. Sound brings a barrier into vibration, which in it"s turn brings the air on the other side into vibration. At a first glance one can call this bean counting: whether foil is acoustic transparent or it lets the sound through by an extremely lightweight barrier seems like unimportant. However physically it makes a lot of difference in these circumstances where the difference between transparent and being a barrier becomes defining or dominant. Plastic foil however has a very low mass. By definition and as per mass law he higher frequencies are better insulated than the low frequencies, therefore standard one has more reflection in the highs and less in the lows. In the lows it can even act as a membrane enhancing the low frequent absorption. BUT: This all assumes that that foil CAN vibrate. And in the lows you're confronted with larger displacements and wavelengths. If I give an analogy: round ventilation ducts with traditional thin steel have a significant higher low frequent insulation than square or rectangular ducts with the same section and steel thickness. Reason: these round ducts mechanically suppress the generation of these higher displacements and wavelengths. Hence, here other factors come into play than just mass. We speak here about apparent mass. And before this becomes a new axiom: In how far this is valid for YOUR wrapped blankets depends on the packing and is for you to check or experiment with.
