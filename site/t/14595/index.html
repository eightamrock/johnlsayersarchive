xSpace28 July 2010#10
Overall, yea, that looks accurate, but I would avoid "jamming" the panels into corners and rather leave some caulkable space there, in every corner including the ones made by the wall/ceiling and wall/floor areas. You may also find that it will minimize some of the frustration by hanging your ceiling first, then do the walls, backer rod and caulk, mud/tape/finish, and repeat this process with each layer until you have finished hanging. Paying strict attention to staggering all the joints, edge joints and butt joints. I have read where some are simply using acoustic caulk on the horizontal and butt joints on the layers beneath the final sheerock layer. So you could consider that as well. My thinking on this process, for it to be the most effective, would be that you would hang a piece of rock, caulk the butt joint, hang another piece of rock, etc. Once a full run was completed then caulk the exposed edge of the run and begin hanging your next piece that will embed into the caulk, and so on. Once complete with a wall or ceiling, then caulk the ALL the joints again to make certain they are plugged. You may not be interested in going to those lengths, but it is an option. Anything to stop the passage of air/sound is what you are after as far as one method or the other.

xSpace22 January 2011#23
"I decided to do it the way the contractor suggested to avoid the seam in the floor, and because I think the garage is big enough to stop any sound from going into or coming from the house. " Every aspect of this structure is directly connected, the slab, the walls and the roof, these are all flanking paths. What you are doing is trying to make a better isolated environment, what the contractor is doing is doing what he knows how to do, and you already know that has nothing to do with isolation. :) Your current configuration not only ties the slab to the house, it will tie the roof to the house, you may as well be building a bedroom since what you are trying to do, minimize noise interference, is being defeated by all of the coupling. Topping the existing slab, to raise elevations, if that works to achieve that goal is no issue, helps even. But the hard connection of another slab, intended to be part of an isolated environment defeats, I have to repeat, defeats your goal of developing an acoustic environment, and reduce flanking paths of which this is one, one of several in a typical residential structure. The joint under concern (the seam) is a contributor to your isolation, it is the "break" in the flanking path. But when you consider the roof is tied together anyway, what will you really gain if you break this first path? Pull out the rebar protruding from the existing slab, install a strip of 3/4 inch thermal sheathing the full height (from top of to the bottom of the footer and below if possible) of the existing slab to completely break any connection what-so-ever with the new slab and the old slab.
