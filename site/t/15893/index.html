gullfo27 April 2011#4
per Brien, you definitely want to get your structure squared away - no leaks, added mass, etc. then from a design perspective, you have plenty of space to create a number of rooms-within-a-room. on the isolation, assuming your roof is not longer the major source of sound leakage, you could put the control room and small isolation rooms, lounge, etc closest to the neighbor so you have a large buffer of low or mid sound levels and the live room and drum booths etc farther away. in the louder rooms using a damped membrane floor will eliminate direct transfer into the concrete. your biggest out of pocket cost besides building materials will be your HVAC so begin thinking about the options sooner rather than later. it may be standard metal duct and air handler and exterior condenser will be the core system and then using flex duct to drop off the metal duct into your baffle boxes will give you the isolation you'll need and keeps costs reasonable, plus it's something most AC experts can readily handle since you'll be doing the work on the baffle boxes and they can focus on the overall core system.

xSpace29 April 2011#6
"and is 99" from a concrete slab to the bottom of a 2 by 6 truss 48" o.c. ." The main issue for isolation, assuming you need isolation, is that your existing structure may not be able to support much,,,guaranteed the 48 inch span negates any addition of anything over head. One reason is that the span is too broad in that the material you install >will< be under the pull of gravity and this assumes, once again, that this structure can take it, which it cannot since with not information other than what we have, the structure itself will begin to falter with the added mass. Glenn's design is fine, fits right into your room, but it is an interpretation of how you can install your rooms...that's it. The ability to mass up this room is a long sought out process that we have not even begun to get involved in. All things considered it might be in your best interest to basically build a room in a room...inside of this weak structure. But who can tell, saving me, at the present :) The issue with building a room in a room and expecting the "spring" to exist in a flimsy thin skinned shed is that you omit the exterior of the build from the overall design and only look to the interior picture and the thinking is that this will not only get you a high degree of isolation but will be money well spent. It's gonna look good, but the isolation is gonna suck :)

gullfo29 April 2011#8
the floating wall example is what Brien is referring to in terms of an inner ceiling on the isolation walls versus using the trusses. then again, assuming the walls and additional trusses can support the weight, then using isolation clips and hat channel on the drywall layers for the ceiling in each room, plus the damped membrane floor means you could get around 94-96" interior. on the room sizing - just a rough guess on how to apportion the space. depending on your need for a larger or smaller CR or LR etc will determine the final dimensions. as far as mode calculation - taking the averages will have to suffice as it's very much more complex otherwise. then again, when the room volume is small enough, you're wasting time on trying to do rigorous modeling and instead build to purpose then measure then do final treatments. the basement looks very small and you're ceiling will still be the weak point.
