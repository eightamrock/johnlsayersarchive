rod gervais5 October 2005#16
Guys, Let me simplify something for you - Although the 3 leaf system is an ugly dog looking to raise it's head and bite you in the butt when you least expect it - and will cause you some loss in values for an assembly as a whole. There are times where there is just no other way out. However - in this case - the detail does not provide a 3 leaf system. I'm happy to see that Steve modified the gypsum directly against the glass - in cool or cold climate conditions this could cause some problems with condensation and mold............. HOWEVER - in his detail (when reading the verbiage - which is just as important as looking at the picture) you would only have a 2 leaf system.......... You have the glass leaf - and then the inner wall leaf. If you were to build a room within a room - then the inner leaf would simply be further inside the wall assembly. BUT - (big but - notice that?) - in reality - although you would never gain the full value of the added mass - you could still place insulation in the window cavity (after covering the inside face of the glass with either paint or a nice film to give it a finished look) add 3 layers of mass to the inside wall surface - (but only at the window location itself) then an air-space with your new wall assembly - and gain isolation value that would would not have had without the added mass at the window. Partly this is due to the fact that the TL values of the differing materials are vastly different - and partly due to the fact that the glass doesn't affect the wall as a whole - but only on the percentage of area that deals with the window itself. As I have stated many MANY times in the past - sometimes we have 3 (even 4) leaf systems because we just don't have a choice - and when that happens - we live with it - don't worry about it - and don't bother looking back.............. Sincerely, Rod
knightfly5 October 2005#17
Rod brings up a very valid point - sometimes you can't do the ideal thing. Where you plan a third leaf, you WILL weaken isolation near the window; there isn't much you can do about the physics of this, other than to compensate in other leaves. For your situation, there is no reasonable way around having a 3-leaf situation where the window is - but what you COULD do is to build your insert so that the added mass is CLOSER to the window - perhaps 25mm away. This would RAISE the resonance caused by the glass and the insert's mass, making it easier for your INNER leaf (which should then have 1 more layer of gyp than the REST of the walls) to better isolate - this because higher frequencies are easier to stop with mass. You might consider a cover AGAINST the glass, 1" of insulation (for condensation control), then the mass of your window plug (inset on frame - ask if you need a sketch of this) - then as much air gap as you can afford before building your "inside out wall", and (as mentioned earlier) one extra layer of gyp on that portion of the wall. Doing it this way would lessen the negative effect of multi-leaf resonances, but I have no way of calculating how MUCH... Steve

knightfly6 October 2005#19
I'd stay with 3 layers for the entire inner, "inside out" wall - if you were to use 3 layers of 15mm gypsum both for your insert (sized as in the pic, this is fairly critical) and 3 layers on your inner, floated wall frame, you should just start to hear drums outside when the inside SPL is around 90 dB - this is assuming you do tight construction, good caulking, no seams matching layer to layer, etc - the 25mm insulation between window glass and the insert would be MAXIMUM and (sorta) MINIMUM - less thickness and you may run into condensation problems, more thickness and you'll lower the m-a-m resonance of the cavity between the glass and the gypsum, which will worsen your isolation at lower frequencies. This was calculated with the 500mm air space you mentioned. Overall m-a-m would be around 24 hZ. If you've not built "inside out" walls, you'll probably need to know the order of construction and how to fasten insulation to the inner surface as well?? Steve

knightfly8 October 2005#23
If framing lumber isn't ungodly expensive in your area, there really isn't much reason to (and some reasons NOT to) build "inside out" walls - John's original intention for this was mainly for spaces where things are really tight; the "inside out" thing has two main purposes. One is to reduce the need for framing lumber while still providing a frame AND cavities for absorption material (you can staple cloth directly on the ONLY frame after absorption is laced) and to get the most mass in place with limited floor space. The problems with this approach are (1) placing the panels on the outside of the inner frame lessens the air gap, therefore lessening isolation especially at lower frequencies, and (2) several difficulties with build sequence. Where you have adequate space for walls, normal double framed construction is more practical. It just takes a bit more lumber if you're treating the inner walls... Steve
