jhbrandt27 October 2010#2
Cel, You will need at least STC65 - STC70 in isolation. You will not need RC if you are building an isolated wall. In fact, in order to get STC70 or better you will need the equivalent mass of about 3 layers of 15.9mm, type 'X' gypsum board on each leaf of your wall system. I would recommend a minimum of 2 layers and an appropriate air gap (100mm...?) to get you close. 1.) A single room studio would suit your situation perfectly. ;) 2.) Best dimensions would be: Length = 597cm X Width = 393cm X Height = 335cm - This will give you the most even modal response within your width limitation. Room volume is a nice 78.6 cubic meters and you have modal support down to 28.8 Hz. 3.) You need much higher STL (sound transmission loss). MLV is very expensive for what it does. Use another layer of type X. Cheers, John

Soundman202028 October 2010#4
If I'm not wrong, the brick wall will act as one leaf, so if I want a two-leaf construction, which I hear I will, then I'm thinking a stud wall with RC and two layers of plasterboard, one 10mm and one 13mm.
I'm agreeing with the other posts, and just clarifying the points already made: 1) Forget the RC. You don't need it. The purpose of RC is to decouple the drywall from one side of a single stud wall, which is not your case. If you build a separate stud frame, additional to your existing walls, then you have already decoupled your drywall, so RC is superfluous. Decoupling twice serves no useful purpose. 2) Mixing two different thicknesses of drywall does, in fact, provide a slight improvement in isolation, due to refraction and impedance mismatches between the layers. However, the effect is small compared to the benefits you get from simply adding more mass. Two layers of 16mm drywall will way outperform the 10+13mm approach. 3) 10mm and 13mm drywall is too thin for what you need to do. It does not isolate well down at the low end of the spectrum, since it is too flexible, has too high a resonant frequency, and too low a mass. What you saw in that pdf you linked to is perfectly fine for isolation in normal home and office situations: it will work for that. But a studio is a vastly different animal, and needs a vastly different approach. The sound levels are much higher, and the spectrum coverage is much broader, to start with, plus the isolation needs are very much different. Isolating conversation in the next room is just totally different from isolating a drum kit. But in order to help you with your design, you need to help us with specific details of what you have, such as photos and accurate SketchUp models. The more info you can provide, the better answers you will get. - Stuart -

jhbrandt29 October 2010#6
Celereon wrote:
If I only have 6m in length, then I lose out on floor space. If I plan for (internal dimensions) of about 400cm by 700cm by 300cm high, will this still allow me acceptable modal characteristics in this space?
Those dimensions, exactly, are non-ideal and not recommended. It will have triple incidences beginning at 172Hz. You would be better off with the slightly smaller space and much less treatment. I checked with these dims: Length = 597cm X Width = 393cm X Height = 295cm. This would give you a volume of 69 cubic meters and would work for a one room studio (drums too). Modal support down to 29Hz.
Celereon wrote:
4) In terms of the flooring, I've heard that hardwood floors offer the best acoustics. Would a timber laminate work just as well, if I use the appropriate sound-rated underlays?
Flooring: Hard wood floors are very common in recording studios because many instruments require a hard reflective surface (floor) to sound 'right' to humans. For example: Piano, acoustic guitar, Cello, Double bass, and the list goes on. Instruments sound more pleasing with a reflective floor because this is how we are used to hearing them. BTW, laminate, hardwood and concrete all sound similar. TV/Movie studios usually have a painted concrete floor. Acoustically, carpet is a poor choice due to it's absorption peak in the upper mid-range and it's uselessness at other frequencies. But when required, a thick Persian style rug really does the trick for cutting floor reflections - either in the control room or tracking room/booth. It doesn't matter what kind of underlay you use for a laminate floor (sound-wise) as long as it does not squeak or crunch. 'sound rated' underlays are good for IIC or impact noise. (like stomping feet, etc)
