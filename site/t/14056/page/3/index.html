For isolation, yes. The way I see it, anything solid introduces two possible risks: You could create a flanking path if it is too dense, and you could interfere with the MSM properties. So I'm thinking outside the box for a minute. Would it be possible to somehow build my control room ceiling *higher* than the trusses, to make the control room *sound* like a bigger space? In other words, can I get the same sonic benefits of a vaulted ceiling without actually having to build one? Looking at that sketchup diagram of my truss system, the proposed drywall location follows the slope of my existing garage ceiling and peaks at about 13'. That drywall would be used as my control room ceiling. Control room walls would then *somehow* be built to extend up into that space until they make contact with that drywall layer. Hottmeal22 March 2010#70
Quick update for you all. We've considered all the options and have decided to entirely replace the trusses with a "conventional rafter" system. This will leave us maximum headroom under which we can frame in the control and air exchange rooms. We'll be able to use standard studio build techniques (nothing experimental). As you may remember from my initial pics, I have a large triangle shaped vent in the peak of the ceiling, plus long vents running the length of the garage where the roof overhangs. Back on page 2 John indicated that I'll have to seal those off: "well you are going to have to seal that off - unfortunately a soundproof studio is an airtight studio." To that my contractor has asked the following question. I told him I'd check with the experts here to find out. "How do you propose to properly vent the roof system if the soffit vents and gable end vents will be sealed off? Understandably, this needs to be done for soundproofing, but what about building code and the ability of the roof to breath and properly vent itself? The shingle manufacturers will not warranty shingles if a roof is not properly vented, and the roof sheathing will have moisture/mold issues. Another concern will be ice buildup in the winter season. One of the concepts behind roof venting is to keep a cold space above the insulation to allow the moisture to escape and to allow the snow sitting on the roof to remain as snow and not be melted. If you close off these vents then how does the roof breathe? Obviously if you are closing off these vents than you must be using another technique to vent the roof. How is this done?"

Photo update. After considering the options, I decided to spend this year's budget raising the ceiling. It puts studio completion off by a year, but the end result will be soooo much more comfortable to work in. To anyone reading this: This part of the project is most definitely *not* DIY. I hired a commercial build crew for this, and let their construction expertise lead the way. This part involves entirely replacing the truss system with a "conventional rafter" system. My particular crew has decades of experience doing this. If you are faced with the same situation and want to try this approach, be sure to get all the proper sign-off from your structural engineer, your town, and your insurance company. Taking delivery of the 2x12's. These will form the supports for my roof.

The basic idea is to repalce the trusses by sistering these 2x12's alongside the original 2x4's. The new rafter system is nailed into place first and then secured in the middle with collar ties. When everything is in place, the old trusses are then simply cut away and discarded. The following picture shows the new system in place. You can see where the old 2x4's have been cut away. So now the project's at a standstill because my budget is spent. It will most likely resume next spring, or possibly this summer if I can sell some things to raise some cash. I ran some initial sound tests just to give everything a reality check. (Keep in mind that the control room is not even built yet, and there is only a single drywall layer mounted directly to the 2x12's of the roof.) Normal mix levels do not present a problem for anyone. You can barely hear the music when standing outside the garage. With the monitors cranked to extremely loud volume (louder than I would ever mix at) you can hear the lows inside my own house, but the sound does not reach my closest neighbor. In the end we'll have two drywall layers all around, with green glue in between. I have the framing materials for the control room walls, but that part of it won't begin until I can raise more money. Looking at the big picture, the *feel* of the room is really huge now. The pics don't quite do it justice. It is 24'x24', and the ceiling is about 12' at its highest point in the center. In terms of comfort, it's a night and day difference between this and an 8' ceiling.
