Soundman202017 September 2010#25
What he says about modal spacing is kind of OK, but there are some things in there that are "unusual"...
so we invented various fudges like golden ratios, masses of absorption, angled walls and graphic equalisers.
I'd hardly call ratios and absorption "fudges", although I certainly would agree that graphic equalizers are "fudges", and have no place at all in a control room. But choosing a good ratio is extremely important, and that is exactly what he claims to be doing by saying that he wants even modal spacing, so on the one had he is saying that using a good ratio for a room is a "fudge", while on the other he is actually doing exactly that! :shock: :?: In fact, the ratio that he has selected for that room is pretty close to Boner's ratio, but that is by no means one of the best ratios: it ranks at #14 on Eric Desart's list of good ratios, and fails one of the three tests outlined by Walker. That doesn't mean its a BAD ratio: It just isn't one of the BEST ratios. In fact, for that room, the modes will dominate the acoustics between 42hz and 139hz, so you WILL still need treatment in that range. It is a small room: it could not possibly be any other way! Angled walls seem to be personal taste. You can design great rooms with splayed / angled walls, and you can design great rooms without them. Personally, I like splayed walls. But that's just me...
Flutter echoes are a different matter, and happen when the sound source is between parallel reflective surfaces. This is why the area where the speakers are mounted is flared both laterally and vertically.“
That sounds decidedly odd, to me. I've never heard of angling soffits in order to reduce flutter echo! To me, soffit mounting is for a whole different purpose. One of the side effects is that it helps flutter echo involving the front wall a bit, but I've never heard anyone say that is the MAIN reason for soffit mounting, nor even for splaying the front walls. That sure sounds strange to me.... In my book, soffit mounting tries to create an infinite baffle, which brings a whole army of acoustic benefits regarding the speakers themselves and how they project sound into the room. And the biggest reason I can think of for splaying front walls is to create an RFZ around the listening position, not to control flutter echo. Even with those splayed soffits, you will still have flutter echos between all other parallel surfaces, so you'll still need to treat them.
Attached is the floor plan, the wall structure for most walls,
There are some things in there that got my attention too. First, you say : "a double skin for the east side as offices are on the other side of the wall". You cannot isolate a room by treating only ONE side of it, any more than you can build an aquarium by putting glass in only one side of the frame! Isolating a room is the same as building an aquarium: you MUST do all sides of the room the same, because if you don't then it won't hold water (or "hold sound" in the case of the studio). Sound is like water: it takes the easiest path out, and once it gets out, it spills everywhere. If you isolate only one wall of the room, then sound will ignore that wall and take the easier path out through the other five un-isolated walls. And once the sound is out, it will spread around that one "good" wall anyway, and still get to whatever is on the other side. So all six sides of the room must be done to the exact SAME level of isolation: Doing one better than the other five means that you just wasted a lot of money pointlessly doing that one. It is "all or nothing". Do all six sides properly, or don't bother.
The medium performance wall is made the following • 12mm Plasterboard • 12mm Softboard • 18mm Chipboard
I don't see the point of that at all! The 18mm chipboard is fine, but 12mm plasterboard is too thin, and I'm not even sure what 12 mm "softboard" is, nor what acoustical benefit it might be supposed to have. In general, 12mm plasterboard is too thin, too flexible, and has a too high resonant frequency to be much use in MSM walls. I'd go with two layers of 16mm plasterboard, instead of those two layers of 12mm stuff. Far better acoustic qualities.
The double skin is virtually this doubled
That sounds like you might be talking about a four-leaf wall!!! :shock: :!: Maybe you could describe the proposed construction for that in more detail, like you did for the simpler wall? There are more things on the plans: I also don't get why he seems to be using staggered studs for the walls, when you seem to have space to go with totally separate fully decoupled studs, with the resulting improvement in isolation. What is the reason for that? Is it that you don't really need much isolation, so it isn't a priority? I'm also more than a bit skeptical about what I see in the second of the plans that you posted: Is that REALLY the way he intends to support the ceiling? All that mass hanging off 3x2 joists, supported on 2x1 notches and sitting on 3x2 ledgers that are just screwed to the wall, THROUGH the layers of wall cladding that is already there, and spanning ten feet???? :shock: Are you SERIOUS? I don't know about the UK, but that sure as hell would NOT pass code here in Chile. Sorry, but please don't invite me to sit in your studio, if that's the way the ceiling is built... That's an awful lot of mass, hanging over your head from thin joists on unstable supports.... I'm no structural engineer, so maybe guys like Rod and Brien and Andre can comment on that, but to me it seems questionable, at the least. And that doesn't even address the issue that the entire design shown there is not even providing any isolation! It is all fully coupled, and therefore pretty useless as isolation. There are numerous flanking paths there, so that wall / ceiling will not isolate well, even if it does pass code. I'd also question the control room layout: You have the door in the rear corner, right where the bass trapping should go, and from the soffit angles it looks like your mix position is going to be exactly at 50% of the room length, which is the worst possible position.... But those are just me thoughts, from my limited understanding of acoustics and construction: Maybe the real experts here can correct me if I'm wrong on any of that, and add their own comments. - Stuart -
