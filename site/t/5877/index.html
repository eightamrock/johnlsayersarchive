Carpwftx23 March 2006#2
Hey man Im a fellow rocker who has dont this very type of recording many times. Full bands in my kitchen, bedroom bathroom what ever. I am as far away from an expert as you could passibly get on the subject of acoustics, but considering your time frame and my experince here goes. 1. If you dont have a good sounding room and don't have the time to get it that way just deaden the crap out of it and add space in the mix! You can add reverb and EQ to get it sounding better. 2. You said Metal/core. I think you meant Metalcore. If its a Metalcore band you are really in luck. That type of music deosnt require a lot of space. Its very dry sounding when done right. With the material you have and assuming your tracking all the instruments live at once without a headphone monitor mix? Your worst problem is going to be instrument isolation. So here is what I would do 1. Get some plywood for under the drums. Carpet makes them sound boxy. Hang pieces of your fluffy stuff above that area, the bounce off the celing makes over head mics sound like crap and its just hi freqency anyway, with all the cymbles. If you can get way without using overheads even better. 2. Sorta use absorbtion sparingly around the drum area. You want a little sheen there. Dont deaden the drum heads too much either! You will thank me later for that. 3. Isolate the kick! Stick a mic in the bass drum and make a tunnel with a blanket or insulation or something. If you can get this mic isolated your doing good. Put another one on the beater head to pick up the click if you can. 4. Just deaden the rest of the room with whatever you got. look at some of the absorber designs on the is forem and do you best to duplicate it. I wouldnt think you need to build anything. Just hang some junk up. 5. You can record a guitar amp in a box. You dont need a good acoustic space for metal guitar. The drier the better. Id make some shields out of your fiberglass and some partical board. Just use spray glue to stick fluffy on a half sheet of ply wood and sorta sheild it from the drum mics. The drummer should get just enough guitar to play the songs. Honestly drums are the key here. The best recordings Ive done like this, I just ran a signal from a Guitar Pod to the drummer and just had one guitarist play along with the drummer useing headphones. Then I just tracked the guitars seperatly after that. Your not gonna get low absorbtion out of the stuff you have, but I read that the fluffy stuff just kept in the original pakaged bails put in a corner makes a pretty good trap I hope this helps, by the way if you do have full monitoring through headphones availible. Just stick the amps in a few closets! Its the drums you got to be acousticly conserned about, and really thats more about the overheads if your close micing the rest of the kit. Just remember hard floors soft ceilings, and METAL=DRY. Mix it like this Bass drum kicking hard. Rest of the kit with a little reverb Guitars panned hard right and left. Bass right up the middle Vocals unusually low in the mix. This is how metalcore sounds so loud and chaotic. It gives the illusion of the band bieng so loud the singer is drownded out a little.

knightfly23 March 2006#4
Go buy enough rolls of the unfaced fiberglass to stand up to the ceiling in all your corners, they will help even out your room response fairly well. If the rolls of insulation don't want to stand up, you can put some sticky-tape picture hanging hooks on the wall corners, mounted sideways, and just use string between the hooks to hold the stacks of insulation from falling over. These should go about the middle of the roll just under the top one in most cases. The carpet is gonna make the whole room sound boxy (only absorbs highs and upper mids, leaving low mud) but the corner stacks will help pull out a lot of that mud; leaving them wrapped will keep from taking out too much highs. If you want less highs, take a sharp knife and make several slashes in each roll's plastic wrapping - You've already gotten enough good advice on the rest to get some pretty good results, it's gonna take a LOT more than a couple days to improve on it, and a LOT more experimentation too. Good luck... Steve

Did I just get a knod from the head honcho?????? Thats awesome I dont feel so stupid now! Couple more tips before you get started. Are all 4 mics going to be recorded on one track? Or will they get individual tracks and bounced later? Do you only have 4 mics in your posession? Deos your recorder have an RCA stereo line in? If so here is a reall good trick to add more inputs if you can borrow some mics. Go get a small unpowered Beringer mixer. They are dirt cheap. You could get one with 4 XLR ins and tape and line outs for under 100 bucks. Run 2 overheads to input 1 and 2 on the mixer. Get a good signal. Run 2 more mics for toms on input 3 and 4 get a good signal. Its imortant that you go ahead and pan and eq these inputs as if they are permanent, dont set the levles to hot, its a one shot deal. Send that whole mix out of the TAPE/OUT via RCA to the recorder onto 1 track. Mic your bass and snare on the other 2 tracks. You should have one left, Id put another mic on the bass drum. Record! You should have a strong signal on bass and snare that you can process independently and a fairly good mix of your toms and over heads all on one track just process that track the best you can. Important to note that what ever goes wrong on this stereo track cant be fixed indiviualy. If one mic craps out mid take you cant just fix that input. It effects the whole signal, toms and overheads. After your done take the Beringer back to the store the next day and get your money back!!!!! I use to record ENTIRE kits like this. All on 1 stereo track, it was a lot of work but punk rockers make dew with what we got. There is one very important thing to remember about doing this. Make sure the drummer gives good audible counts before each song and during breaks. The guitarist wont know where to start if they dont. If they are use to doing 4 counts Id make em do 8 just to be sure. The drummer is gonna hate how one guitar sounds in the headphones recording. Just tell them once he has the drums down the rest is butter. I even had one drummer who played his songs with out a guitar at all. Just a guy in the room with him playing air guitar and giving hand signals! If they know the song real good it shouldnt be a problem, but if they are a new band.........well have fun my friend

knightfly24 March 2006#10
The lower you can keep your overheads, the louder the toms will be in them; you should be able to get a good compromise height adjustment in 3-4 tries, so the cymbals and toms aren't killing each other. With a matched pair of mics (preferably small diaphragm condensers) you want to do an X-Y pair most of the time; this puts the diaphragms within 1/2" of each other, one just above the other, for least amount of phasing between them - the one on the left is your right channel, and vice versa since they'll be facing toward the opposite side - You can buy an xy adapter for a normal mic stand at any of the online music places, but after I saw how wimpy they were I used part of the hardware off the one I bought and made my own spreader bars out of thicker, stainless flat bar stock and added a pair of Shure iso-mounts - works great... Steve

knightfly25 March 2006#16
Carp, sorry I missed your question about my distaste for Behringer; I'm a musician/song writer/engineer/yada/yada, and I don't like people who pirate music, software, or ideas (it's different here, where I volunteer whatever I can that will help) - The way Behringer makes thing so cheap is they copy others' designs, then use as low cost parts as they can, and as cheap labor as they can get; I'm amazed the stuff works as well as it seems to, but if you get critical and try to match channels, whether you can hear it or not there are mismatches in circuitry caused by the cheap, wide tolerance components, that can cause phase differences at different frequencies, no two EQ sections having exactly the same frequency/phase response, etc - I've mainly just learned over the course of 60+ years that you get what you pay for, or LESS; so I personally tend to "buy right" instead of "buy twice" when I can possibly afford it. This is not to say that some decent recordings can't be made with less expensive gear; a lot of that is who's behind the knobs and how well they know their stuff - it's mostly my dislike of pirates I guess... Steve
