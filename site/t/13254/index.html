Jan Beck wrote:
Eric:
The springiness of neoprene (elastic) is no advantage over the dead weight (viscous) of roofing, for this kind of application. Elastic: stores energy and gives it back Viscous: absorbs energy
Where would you rate dry sand as either Elastic or Viscous?
Viscous. Sand (fine and dry, it must flow) works as a damper. Not necessarily as efficient as other synthetic materials but it damps. Shearing forces come into play (are needed) which aren't that high for sand. But it has it advantage of its high cheap mass of course. Sand is easy to use on strong horizontal surfaces, but wall filling (I mean normal cavities) is an unpractical idea since the sideways forces of sand filling in function of time become extreme (you blow up your wall = get shape of a drop = a lot of reinforcement or strength must be provided to withstand these forces). That's different of course if you use it to fill hollow concrete blocks. And sand is no magic. It's (in most cases) still better and more logic just to use traditional double leaf systems with absorption (whether lightweight or heavy walls). There are cases where I used sand with success because it was either cheap good mass (+ additional damping) or in special industrial cases (damping the steel (3 to 6mm) of HUGE silencers by welding an additional steel casing around them filled with sand). At FN http://www.fnherstal.com/ I once improved shooting ranges by spreading a thick layer of sand on the concrete roofs (was the easiest and cheapest manner in that situation). Brian Ravnaas once tested/measured the effect (vibration levels) of filling speaker stands with sand (and with leadshot and a mix of both I believe, not sure of the latter) with good success. The difference between viscous and elastic is easy to check. Drop a piece of elastic material on the ground (e.g. a dried ball of silicone) and it jumps up again as a ball (silicone deforms, stores the energy and gives it back). It restores to its original shape. Drop a lump of children's modeling clay or plumbers caulk on the ground, it deforms and falls dead. It does NOT restore to it's original shape (deforms, but absorbs/dissipates this energy). Stylized: Green Glue is mainly viscous, Silicone is mainly elastic. Traditional neoprene rubber is mainly or big part elastic, if it shouldn't one couldn't use it as a spring for decoupling. It does NOT decouple by absorbing the vibration but by acting as a spring (which relates to the isolation caused by the phase relationship between both leafs or whatever you're decoupling). Roughly: the faster something restores to its original shape the more elastic. The more elastic a decoupler the higher the insulation in the proper targeted frequency range but the worse the dip in the resonant frequency range (and the "worse" part is worse and much more risky than the gain in the "higher" part) Edit: I accidentally stumbled upon this: Sounds logical. But haven't bought the article (yet). http://cedb.asce.org/cgi/WWWdisplay.cgi?0601879
