xSpace5 February 2010#4
"I think that even with osb under the sheetrock, the 27' wall will be somewhat floppy without some sort of lateral support." It does require a ceiling that is framed to the new walls to be the most effective and to be a "room-in-a-room" type construction. "In normal construction, the top plate would be connected to the truss directly wouldn't it?" For load walls (typically the very ends of the truss) this would be correct. But for any other wall that may be inside the actual span of the truss (if it is NOT a load carrying wall) it would not make a direct hard connection but might usually be either cut short enough to allow for deflection of the truss or have some type of bracket to allow for this movement. "At the very least, the plates on the exterior walls are connected to the trusses using hurricane straps. So I dont think that using some kind of decoupling connection would be any different structurally." You are talking about two completely different pieces of hard ware that do two completely different things. The hurricane straps are used to >tie< the roof truss to the house in the event of damaging hurricane activity. That is why it is called a hurricane strap :) By design the straps make effort to keep the roof framing from flying off the structure, no matter from which side the wind is blowing. What you are wanting to do might be better installed if it is connected to the wall of your structure rather than the upper truss. But pictures prove a thousand opinions wrong everyday. In any event, I would get with the architect, designer or whoever is drawing your plans for verification of Dade County code if need be. Brien

cadesignr10 February 2010#11
I would tend to think this detail SHOULD have been on the plans when you submitted them to your local Building Inspection Department for approval. If they weren't, I would ask your BID about any codes which covers Truss connections at interior walls, as I'm sure there are some. I'm certainly no expert but I've read many articles on Truss uplift/wall connections, and it appears that this is a long term issue that has many concerns. Especially drywall joint cracking, if not outright wall lifting. Not to mention the structural issues of the trusses themselves. Personally, I would either consult with your GC or contact the truss manufacturer for thier reconmendations yourself. ESPECIALLY since the dead loading on the truss is more than normal(2 layers of drywall). From what I understand, Trusses can be engineered to connect at 2 or 3 points, and some manufacturers specify this by directly stamping on the truss. I would also tend to think your GC should be aware of this issue as it is probably code related in your area. In conclusion, I wouldn't decide on a solution based on forum advice. However, you may want to ask these guys.... http://www.inspectorsjournal.com/
