ndelonas21 October 2012#1
Hello friends, In finalizing my attic studio plans, I'm a bit concerned about sound leakage through the floor itself. I am unable to treat the floor from below. It's hard to tell how much sound transmits through the floor and how much is going out the sides and roof, since I haven't yet built the interior acoustically-treated walls. I own the apartment below the attic, so I'm not concerned about disturbing myself. However, I am slightly concerned about how much sound might reach the apartment below mine from the attic above. I played music through an amp at live-band volume up there and while it wasn't oppressive, you could hear the lower frequencies in the apartment below mine. It'd be nice to have just a bit more isolation, though, again, I'm not sure how much was coming out through the roof and down the exterior walls of the building. The floor is currently very old tongue-in-groove hard wood. It's far from air tight. I was wondering how much some kind of self-leveling gypsum concrete might help with cutting sound transmission. Any advice? House is framed in oak and solidly built. I'd get a structural engineer to verify it, but I'm sure the floor could handle a good deal of weight. If I do use gypsum concrete to seal and level the floor, should I do that before or after I construct the walls? Thanks in advance for your thoughts on this. -- Nick

Soundman20203 November 2012#6
I was planning to put the V-Drums on some kind of thick rubber matting such as the type used under weight-lifting apparatus in gyms.
That probably wont work to well. It will only work if the rubber has the right properties to "float" the weight of the drum kit plus drummer. If the drum kit is too light or too heavy, then you won't get the effect you are hoping for. The problem here is mainly the impact noise, created by stomping on the kick and hi-hat pedals. Search the forum for "drum riser", and you should be able to find the examples that RJ was talking about.
I was wondering how much some kind of self-leveling gypsum concrete might help with cutting sound transmission. Any advice?
The advise would be: DON'T DO THAT! At least, don't do it until you check with a structural engineer to make sure your floor joists can take that large extra load, and that your local building code allows you to do that.
I'd get a structural engineer to verify it,
Absolutely yes! But even then, this might not be a good idea. Isolation is an "all or nothing" issue. Just isolating the floor won't stop sound from flanking around that through the walls.
Salesman at Trademark Soundproofing believed two layers of plywood with two layers of greenglue would suffice for my needs.
But what exactly are your needs? How much isolation do you need, in terms of decibels? Without knowing that, neither the salesman nor anyone else can tell you what you need to do to isolate. - Stuart -

Soundman20205 November 2012#8
Bottom line, I need to mitigate background street noise of just a bit over 50 db. STC of 50 to 55 would suit my needs.
I'm not sure I understand: Are you saying that the ambient level outside your studio is around 50 dB, and you want to get that down to zero?
The walls I'm installing should give me 55+ STC.
What walls are you installing? Do you have a link to the manufacturer, or maybe you could describe how they are built? Also, I think I said this before, but isolation involves the entire room: It is pointless to install STC-55 walls if your floor is only STC-40 and your windows are STC-35. You would be wasting a lot of money like that. Besides, STC is pretty useless for rating studio walls. STC is fine for houses, offices and shops, but tells you nothing useful about a wall for a studio. STC does not consider any low frequencies at all, so it is no use for telling you about how well a wall isolates drums, bass guitars, keyboards and that kind of thing, where much of the energy is in the low end of the spectrum. You should be looking at TL, not STC.
The floor is the question mark, but it looks like the QG rubber should do the job.
I VERY much doubt that a layer of rubber is going to give you STC-55!!! :shock: :!: Sorry, but that just isn't believable. According to their own website: "Adds about 5 STC ... in wood or concrete assemblies. Ratings will vary depending on overall assembly." Even that is probably optimistic. I very much doubt that your current floor is giving you STC 50, so it is extremely unlikely that you will get to STC-55 for your floor with just that product. In fact, if you take a close look at IR-802, RR-169 and IR-766 you'll see that for rubber mat with laminate flooring most likely there will be no improvement at all below about 400 Hz, in the absolute best case, and there is a distinct possibility that you could even lose isolation in the region below 400 Hz. in the worst case. So you could end up making matters worse for low frequencies. That is hard data from independent laboratory testing. - Stuart -
