Soundman202020 January 2010#7
Just one quick comment here:
Phase 2 - Build timber frames for partitioning of rooms Phase 3 - Sound Isolation / Build the necessary floors / cabling Currently, I need advice on phase 2, just for the recording area (not the office / workshop, even though they dictate the layout). The positioning / layout of the timber frames with space for sound isolation and acoustic treatment.
You list building the partitions and isolating the rooms as though they were two separate things. They are not: The partitions between the rooms ARE the isolation! It's not that you build frames "with space for adding isolation". The frames are PART of the isolation, as is the rest of the wall. The entire wall is an isolation system that needs to be designed and built as such, not designed as a partition to which isolation can be added. That won't work. You don't add isolation to the partitions later, the partitions themselves are the entire and complete isolation that you will ever get between rooms. They have to be designed and built as such, as proper fully decoupled MSM walls, and they have to be built carefully. If are not designed and built to be the isolation, there isn't much you can do afterwards to fix that, other than taking them down and starting again. - Stuart -

Soundman202028 January 2010#14
After a lot of reading.... I've come to the conclusion that the best mix is at 38%, then a 60 degree triangle from the monitors, meeting behind the engineers head.
Well, actually, no you have NOT done that! In fact, you now have your head at the 62% position, instead of the 38% position, which places your head really close to the back wall, where you don't want it. The reason that you are now in this situation is because you have your speakers firing across the SHORT axis of the room, instead of down the LONG axis of the room. So yes, your CR needs to be narrower, and also deeper... which would have your speakers where the should be: firing down the long axis. OR you could flip your room 90°... (In any event, 38% is not written in stone: It's just a good starting point. You can move forward or backward a little, if need be, as long as you don't overdo it.)
I don't want to flip it 90 degree, I like the idea of vision into studio straight ahead.
Then you are stuck with the acoustic issues of poor speaker position / mix position! So maybe you need to re-think the layout, such that you can get a longer, narrow CR that faces the LR. Or if you can't make that fit, then a layout where the window is on one side of the CR, instead of up front. To my mind. having good acoustics in the CR is about a million times more important than having wonderful sight lines direct in front of me... Good acoustics kinda is the entire point of spending all that money to build a CR in the first place! :) Another issue: the way you have it now, your speakers are not centered in their soffits: they are closer to the middle panel. They should be centered as near as possible, both vertically and horizontally. So yo need to spread your speakers a bit further apart, and tighten the angle of the soffits to compensate. Finally: Your CR now looks to be almost exactly square! :shock: You might want to fix that... :) - Stuart -

Yes, that is where it is supposed to be: just a few inches behind your head. the exact distance will depend on the size of your head and how far your ears stick out, but it won't be more than a few inches back, unless you have a very strange shaped head! (or a very strange shape studio).
Does it matter if it's this far behind?
Yes! I'd suggest that you add a representation of your head to your diagram, so that you can see where your ears are. The acoustic axes intercept behind your head such that they also just graze through your ears. the objective is, after all, to have the speakers aiming at your ears! Not past your ears, nor at your eyes, but squarely facing your ears, dead-on, so that your ears are perfectly on-axis to the speakers. It seems to me that you cannot possibly do that in your room without changing the angle of the speakers, tightening up the intercept angle, getting away from 60 and closer to 90.
Another question: Do you calculate the 38% with the bass traps at the back of the room? Or without?
ALL room measurements are ALWAYS taken from the final finished surface of the inner leaf, without treatment. In other words, the walls that you see once the room itself is built, but BEFORE you put anything inside the room, such as furniture, equipment, and acoustic treatment. Once again, 38% is not an absolute immutable law! It is only a starting point, a rough rule of thumb to get you in the right ball park. The world will not come to an end if you happen to end up at 41% or 35%, or another position, in the same way as the world not coming to an end if you happen to need to tighten up your speaker angles... - Stuart -

Soundman202028 January 2010#20
Because if never done this before. + I've read somewhere that you should try and keep to the 60degree formula. (i know know that this i snot set in stone)
Yup! Not set in stone, for sure. Once again, it's optimal, a really good starting point, but if you have to change it to improve a more important aspect of your room, then you have to change it. You can go up to 90 degrees if you want (soffits at 45° each), with the slight downside that your sweet spot will be squashed a bit, and your soundstage will seem a bit wider. No big deal. You should be able to live with that. But having your ears wildly off-axis form the speakers would not be a good idea. I'd rather squash my sweet spot and broaden my soundstage, then suffer from poor response due to not having my ears somewhere on the speaker axes.
I thought that equidistant surfaces must be either side of the monitor. (?!)
Yes, which is why you want to move the SPEAKERS sideways, in order to accomplish that! If you move the speakers out a bit, then rotate them slightly, you'll find that the geometry will all fall into place neatly: you'll end up with wider soffits (a good thing, for better low end response), the speakers centered on the soffits (another good thing, for a tighter low end), the speakers aimed exactly at your ears (another good thing, for better accuracy across the entire spectrum), and you sitting at roughly the 38% position (another good thing, avoiding major peaks and nulls from room modes). It's a win-win-win-win situation! And all for the minor "cost" of a smaller sweet spot and wider soundstage? Sounds like a good deal to me! The only other thing you'd need to do is to increase the side wall angle a bit: 6° is for speakers with a 60° intercept. You'll need a little more on the side walls, depending on your final intercept angle.
