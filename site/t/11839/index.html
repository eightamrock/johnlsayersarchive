Soundman202025 March 2009#2
The bracket would then be screwed into the top frame base and the bottom of the I beam ceiling joist.
If you screw down both sides of the bracket, then you are short-circuiting the neoprene. It serves no purpose at all like that. Vibration will flank right past the neoprene, along the screw shank itself. So it would be basically the same as just screwing the bracket in place by itself, without any neoprene. Same effect. Take a look at how commercial braces are made: there is no physical contact between the two halves of the brace. They are only joined by the neoprene, nothing else. No screws, bolts, nails, etc. crosses the neoprene. The two flanges are attached to your walls, sure, but nothing sort-circuits the neoprene, like it does in your design. - Stuart -

Soundman202031 March 2009#15
Was thinking about using sleeve anchors to bolt the bottom sill of my frame to my slab floor instead of nails
Where I live, those things are code. Framing must be bolted here. No nails. Earthquakes are a problem, and a good shaker could pull nails loose, while bolts stand a much better chance of keeping things where they are supposed to be. Yeah, I think I'd go with bolts, even if the weren't required by code. It makes more sense to me.
What is the consensus about padding under the bottom sill plate on the frame?
It depends on what you are trying to accomplish! If all you want is a good seal, then three beads of caulk under the full length of the bottom plate (one under the middle, one close to each edge) will do the trick. Also an additional bead of caulk under each layer of drywall. That way you have multiple seals. John uses rubber strips in some of his designs. That will work too: Of course, both of these require that your floor is totally flat, with no cracks, dips or rises. Now, if you want to actually float your wall on pads, that's a whole different ballgame! Now you are in for some major mathematics, to figure out the correct type, thickness, placing and loading on your pads, such that the are compressed to the exact degree required to float correctly, not more and not less. You need to carefully and accurately calculate the entire mass of the wall that is to be floated, which includes the ceiling too, of course, since it rests on the walls. It also includes windows and doors, and since studio doors are really heavy at the jamb, you need to take that into consideration. If you do it wrong and end up with more mass in your wall and ceiling than you figured in your calculations, then you will over-compress the pads, they will "bottom out", and your wall will flank directly to the floor. On the other hand, if you didn't put enough mass in your wall and ceiling, then the pads wont be compressed enough to float, so once again your wall will flank directly to the floor. You'd also need to be very careful with the construction: you cannot allow anything to bridge across the pad from the frame to the floor, as that would "short circuit" the pad, and flank. So each bolt hole must be drilled to the exact correct size, and the collars and washers must be installed correctly, etc., If you forget to put a collar on one single bolt, and the bolt touches the wood directly, then your wall will flank and all of your effort and expense will have been for nothing. So floating your wall is an option, but not one that should be taken lightly. It is very hard to get it right, and getting it wrong means that you wasted a lot of money for nothing. In any event, if you are building double stud walls on a god concrete slab on grade, then floating the walls is probably not necessary at all: your windows and doors will be a far weaker link than lack of decoupling from frame to floor. I think I'd put the extra money into getting the best possible windows and doors. I think it would be much better spent there. IMHO! - Stuart -
