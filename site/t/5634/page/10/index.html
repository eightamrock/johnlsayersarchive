sharward31 August 2006#233
Hey Marc! Maybe... But since time is of the essence, I think you can probably do this without it. You have Rod's book, and he does cover this at some length (pretty impressively as I recall). You'll want to rent a walk-behind concrete saw and you'll have to buy a blade at time you rent it. Don't get the cheap-o abrasive blade, but don't spend big bucks for the diamond blade... Get the moderately priced ($100ish?) metal one. Cut about 1/4" to 1/2" on each pass, getting progressively deeper with each pass. You'll need a vehicle with a trailer hitch. After returning the saw, a 60 pound stand-up electric impact hammer (a.k.a. jackhammer) will make quick work of the break-up and removal of the 2 feet of concrete you're removing. I would think you'd be done with that tool within the yard's "minimum" (2 hour minimum? 3 hour minimum?). You could use one of those hand-held demolition hammers, but it will take longer, and you don't need that level of precision. Using the 60 pound impact hammer is a LOT less intimidating than it seems! I actually found it kind of fun (and I'm a scardy-cat usually). Once the area is cleared, you'll want to dig down with a shovel. It looks in the images like you need 16" of footing. I would suggest you dig down about 20" to 22", then dig out from under the cut slab a few inches, then set your forms on the outside edge. Use steel stakes -- the're much easier to drive into and pull out of the ground. You'll need to rent a rotohammer and a long bit to drill those horizontal holes into your cut slab so that you can get that rebar in there. Fortunately, Home Depot sells rebar precut into various lengths. In the trench, so that you can lay in 4" to 6" of crushed rock as your base, then lay down plastic sheeting. This will reduce capilary action (wicking) of moisture up from the ground into the footing. Like I mentioned before in your thread, you may as well lay a couple of rebars in there. Use small pieces of wood with holes drilled in them to position your J-bolts -- you should use 12" bolts, submerging them 8". This gives you plenty of penetration and leaves you plenty of exposed bolt for you to do either a single or a double soleplate. As far as the the edge of the existing concrete is concerned, I recall you wrote that your architect specified roofing felt as your cold joint. That's strange. I would have thought the closed cell foam stuff I used would be better. Nevertheless, follow their advice -- it may help for you to call them for specifics. Here are a couple of photos of what I did.
