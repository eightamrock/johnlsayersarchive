Hey Marc! I have to admit that while the photos are very helpful and your description of your dilemma is very detailed, I'm having a bit of trouble following all of the options you're weighing. That being said, I'll say this. You will be surprised, perhaps shocked, at the nailing schedule of shear wall panels. Along the edges, it's probably one nail every 2 to 3 inches. Of course, don't take my word for it, because my codes aren't your codes and what was done on my house six years ago may not even be in accordance with today's codes. So, yes, definitely consult with your local building department for the best approach. I'm surprised this didn't come up in your dealings with your architect... :? I don't think having a "partial third leaf," especially one that only exists for maybe one foot of an eight foot wall, is anything to worry about, isolationwise. However, my gut tells me you may be better off yanking out that non-structural header so that you can do the "ultimate" wall there. I am, however, having some pretty serious concerns about your concrete "slab" to which you intend to attach this new wall. :roll: Now, granted, not everyone (in fact, very few) would go to the great lengths I have in pouring a new slab. However, one of the factors in my mind was seismic stability, just as hurricane force stability is in yours. That concrete slab outside is surely not a structural grade slab -- it's probably quite inferior to the slab in your garage, which is inferior to the slab inside the house. How thick is that patio floor, anyway? :shock: Structural slabs are engineered to support houses. That patio floor was "engineered" to support... Patio furniture and people walking on it? :roll: Personally, I'd like to see you excavate and pour, at the very least, a structural footing (complete with rebar) on which to attach the new outer wall... And, ideally, remove the "patio slab" and pour a new slab in its place. You're probably shaking your head at this point, which is understandable... But I had a neighbor once who had a sunroom built in his back yard on what was his patio. The contractor had to replace the patio with a structural slab. That was standard operating procedure for that sunroom builder, and for all I know, was/is required by code. I hope Rod is tuned into this. In the event of disputing advice between him and me, definitely listen to him! Bottom line, in my opinion anyway, that wall will only be as strong as the soleplate on the bottom of it, and the soleplate will only be as strong as the footing to which it is attached. Think about it. --Keith :mrgreen:

guitardad72 wrote:
Patio Slab is the same grade as my garage slab, it's about 5" thick (I think, I'll have to dig it up to confirm). I questioned the hell out of my architect about this, when planning for the drawings and he said my patio and garage slabs will take the weight of the walls I am planning on.
Marc, I wouldn't bet on it. The fact that the slab was poured monolitic with your garage isn't indicitive of anything. Frost heave isn't an issue in Forida - and that's the only reason that we (here in frost heave country) avoid doing things like that. So it's no problem (in your state) to pour a simple slab extension like you have at your house. BUT - even if the slab is the same thickness as your garage - the garage isn't being carried by the slab - it's being carried by the foundation and footing constructed to bear the load. This is very evident in your photos. Understand that the load (in your case) goes both ways - up and down. The down load (gravity load) is very apparent - the upload is wind design for hurrican force winds. I would be very VERY surprised to find that this same footing and foundation was poured to carry your patio slab - and the slab itself will not withstand the gravity load - nor will it act as a counterweight when it comes to uplifting windoads (always a concern in your state as noted above). I would not consider any construction on that slab until I excavated the entire perimiter and witnessed the block foundation and footings. As a matter of fact -0 I would be very surprised to find an inspector in Florida who would allow you to construct on that with out first proving to them that this was the case........ so make sure to take plenty of photos for them if (indeed) the footings and foundation do exist. Sincerely, Rod

I'm sorry you're having this rather urgent challenge before you, Marc. You deserve better. You hired and paid for expert advice, got your permit in order, hired a licensed and insured contractor, and proceeded with caution. How you have a gaping hole in your building and you're quite understandably worried about hurricanes and security while you resolve this issue. If I lived near you, I'd take some time off of work and give you a hand with this... Even the gruntwork. I know you've spent a lot of money so far, so you're probably not very enthused about spending more than you need right now. However, I think the best solution is to remove the 9' x 9.5' area of the slab where you room is going to extend out to, dig 18" wide x 12" deep footings around the entire perimiter, tamp everything down really tightly, lay down plastic and #4 (1/2") rebar, and pour a new monolithic slab with 12" long J-bolts embedded in the concrete, pretty much just like I did. Yes, you might be able to get away with doing something less involved than that. However, I think few would argue that the solution I described would be ineffective. Benefits: level slab, excellent protection against hurricane forces, level surface for your new floor, and no worries. Yes, it's a lot of work... :roll: ...which is why I think you shouldn't spend any time stressing out about it -- just dig in (literally! ;-)) and do it. I thought about the fact that you might be able to get away with just cutting and digging the footings. However, when all is said and done, that's still an awful lot of work, and then you'll still have that sloped floor to deal with. :roll: I'll bet if you just pulled out the stops and kicked butt on it, you'd have it wrapped up pretty quickly. I have a nice how-to DVD on building a monolithic slab that I'll be happy to donate to the cause... If you PM me with your address, I'll mail it off to you. Hang in there, Marc. We'll get you through this rough patch. --Keith :mrgreen:
