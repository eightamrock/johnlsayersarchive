I'm aware that the State of Florida has no state income tax and that they tend to make up for that in the form of property taxes... But do you mean to tell me (and everyone else) that adding (for example) a $10,000 improvement to your home will cause the entire home to be reassessed at today's market value?? :shock: Everyone can say what they want about California, but at least we have Proposition 13 to save us from that kind of nonsense. It appears that Florida now has a similar reassessment protection amended to its constitution. According to that page, "The full market value of physical alterations to the property such as additions or improvements (not including normal maintenance) will be added to the property’s assessment after the SAVE OUR HOMES Cap has been applied to the qualifying homestead property." Of course I'm no legal expert, but that information is promising and very much worth your looking into. That being said... Honestly, Marc, your best bet is to work closely with your local building department and/or your tax assessor's office every step of the way. Mine was really helpful. My city's building permit and plan check fees are based on the valuation of the work, and the clerk who helped me initially coached me on keeping the valuation number low while also not making it so low as to cause a red flag to be raised -- basically, working with me to keep my costs down. Hopefully you'll encounter some nice folks down there who can advise you along the same lines. Regardless, you are definitely doing the right thing by doing this project by the book. 8) Sometimes doing the right thing isn't easy, but it's worth it in the long run. --Keith :mrgreen:

Very cool pictures, Marc! :-) I love seeing photos that offer a glimpse into the life of the people we've come to know on this forum. 8) Anyway, I think you and I are in agreement on everything -- what made me think that we weren't on the same page was the use of the term "skyrocketing" to describe what would happen to your property taxes. "Skyrocketing" is what happened to my property taxes as a result from moving two years ago. They went up over 300% because of the difference in purchase prices between our current and previous homes. Having your assessed value go up a few thousand dollars, even if it is over the lifetime of your residency in that home, doesn't constitute "skyrocketing" in my eyes. :) Also, another thing about the value of your improvements... Typically the cost of labor is factored into the value, even if you are doing the work yourself. Using the City of San Jose's policy as an example, "When you apply for your permits, you will be asked for a realistic estimate of your project's value, including the fair market value of all labor and materials. This information is compared to a State approved table of construction values for similar projects. Construction taxes and some permit fees may be calculated based on the valuation of your project." If your building department is anything like mine, though, they'll help you come up with a number that is low enough to save you some money, yet not so low that would be subject to some kind of override. In my case, with their guidance, I used a value that is representative of a more typical partial garage conversion, rather than the beefy soundproofed structure I'm actually building. The main thing they want to avoid is people buying a tiny home on a big lot, being assessed for the value of the tiny home, and then having those people DIY themselves into a mini-mansion of sorts, and paying a fraction of the taxes than that of buying a home of equal size. As far as the $2,500 figure goes, I think they're basically saying that anything less than that is insignificant, such that the assessor's office probably doesn't even want to bother with reassessments resulting from projects that small.
