knightfly4 March 2006#81
1 - Best bet is to ask the manufacturer of your flooring, being sure to explain your climate. Any vapor barriers in that climate need to be at the OUTER shell, either sealant on concrete walls/floors or for frame walls, just outside the outermost studs. 2 - KISS method usually works best; normal, absorbent corners. As thick as you can afford, no less than 4", 8's better, complete fill a bit better yet. 3 to 6 PCF for corner traps, if more than 3 PCF for side absorbers (first reflection points) I'd face it with fluffier stuff. 3 - Any weakening of the isolation due to resonant traps will always be a 2-way street. For a classic example of this effect, put a loud boombox in another room, separated from you by concrete block (hollow) - Hopefully this room has a door for more isolation from the room YOU are in - pick up a 4x4 foot section of plywood or gypsum, hold it in front of you like you're gonna hang a picture on the intervening wall, and slowly walk toward this wall with the board in front of you - note which frequencies change in loudness as you get closer, right up til you actually TOUCH the block wall with the 4x4 board. Note that the changes did NOT need a SEALED trap, only the air between the board and the wall... Steve

Dan Fitzpatrick15 March 2006#85
Marc, it's not too difficult to run a new duct out through a composition shingle roof, i don't know if you have done this before. it can be a little scary to cut a hole in your roof. :D make sure you are not to close to the peak of the roof, the flashing can be quite large and needs to stay below the peak obviously. it's a good idea to make a cardboard template of the bottom of your vent with the proper hole, and then spray paint works well to mark where you want to cut the hole. it's good to do this on a relatively hot day if possible, what i have done several times in the past is run a very long screwdriver or similar tool under the asphalt shingle rows where the flashing for the vent needs to go and simply lift until the roof nail is pulled through the warm/hot shingle. then i use a block of wood and hammer the nail head down through the upper layers, since it now will be protruding a bit, and get in the way. in manner way you can create a nail free zone for the flashing of your vent to slide up in. then i just nail the bottom corners down and put a little roof cement around the nails. i've done this at least 4 or 5 times for various projects, without any leaks. but that's just me "making it up," i'm not sure if there might be a better way.

Sandersd29 March 2006#97
Marc, I did a two year stint as an estimator for an insulation and fireproofing contractor and learned a lot about thermal and acoustic insulation. There are three kinds of heat transfer: radiant, conductive, and convective. Each requires a different method of control. The most cost effective suggestions are: install a radiant barrier to the underside of your roof joists and add R-30 insulation batts to what is already there. The radiant barrier will reflect a huge percentage of heat and prevent your attic from heating up as much and the R-30 will isolate the structure below from convective heat transfer. Conductive heat is not an issue because the framing is wood and not metal. I used a spray-on radiant barrier, but HD and Lowes sell a staple-up metalized foil or bubble wrap looking stuff that does the same thing. Install it with the reflective surface facing down. HUH :shock: If you don't, it'll eventually be covered with a layer of dust that will render it ineffective. It will work facing either way. I've also installed unfaced R-30 batts perpendicular to my joists and on top of the existing pitiful excuse for insulation (3" blown in) that was installed 35 years ago. The attic is noticably cooler and no heat radiated into the house. :idea:
