knightfly27 February 2006#57
Spans - 2x6 doug fir #2, 24" centers will span 10'8" at 10 # dead load for ceiling joists, other species are all over 10 feet. Framing weight adds approx. 1.2 PSF, leaving 8.8 PSF capacity; 5/8 gypsum runs just under 2.4 PSF per layer, 3 layers would be 7.2 PSF, for a total of 8.4 PSF. No other coverings considered, insulation will add less than 1 PSF if you use standard fluffy fiberglass even 12" thick. 2x4's on 24" centers, same conditions, will only span 7'3" (doug fir) - on 16" centers (worse LF isolation than 24" centers) - 8'11" - on 12" centers (even WORSE isolation) 2x4's would span 9'10", but my own personal thought there is DONT - besides the 2-3 dB hit in isolation, you're trying to cram 3 times as many screws as normal into 2x4's, there's GOTTA be a chance of WEAKENING a couple of "questionable" joists there... Drywall - I've clarified the "different thickness" concept in a few places, but they're scattered and NOT in the reference section where you've been looking. I'll reiterate here, and if someone nags enough :? I'll amend the two main places in the reference section (why hasn't anyone invented 48-hour days (that don't wear you out any more but you get twice as much done ) yet??!? Pluses for differing thicknesses in successive layers in a wall - (NOT GLUED TO EACH OTHER) Differing coincidence frequencies, flattens out the Coincidence dip at around 2-3 kHz by a couple of dB. Minuses for differing thicknesses - confusion on job site, probable wrong thickness applied somewhere, causing tearout Loss of mass, causing worse TL on low end by a couple dB Bottom line is this - if you use all 5/8 you improve bottom end performance, at a 1-2 dB penalty around 2.5 kHz - but since bass is the hard part of isolation, it's better to build for low frequency and the rest will take care of itself. You also remove the chance of screw-ups, cut down on the open floor space you need to store two stacks of drywall instead of one, and improve on your chances for bulk discounts by ordering all the same stuff. BTW, "X" code drywall is about 4 pounds lighter per 4x8 foot sheet (and usually more expensive)so won't perform quite as good on low end as standard stuff - Keith SHERWOOD :twisted: and I went over this sometime back, it's probably on his thread on about page 5, or 8, or 11, or... - bottom line is, ANY gypsum wallboard as thick as WE would build is AUTOMAgICALLY at LEAST 1-hour fire rated when built correctly. "Inside out" walls - originally invented by John, great when space is at an ABSOLUTE premium. Their shortcoming is that they do NOT offer as good isolation as "normal" walls, there is NO WAY they can do this. For one thing, if you put 3 layers of 5/8 rock on a 2x4 stud frame, a 12 foot section of this wall 8 feet tall will weigh 787 pounds; even HALF of that (when tipping up AFTER drywall is in place) is nearly 400 pounds. All that weight will be supported by the EDGE of the bottom plate until the wall is fully upright, this is the same idea as a woman in spike heels - SHE may only weigh 110 pounds, but (assuming 3/8" square heel size) she's putting almost 900 pounds per square INCH on a floor... Add to this the problem of getting the innermost (acoustic treatment) insulation to press firmly enough against the wall panel to damp it properly, and you'll likely lose ANOTHER couple dB (up to 10, actually, but typically around 5) compared to a "regular" wall. Also - air gap drops from maybe 8" minimum, down to (assuming masonry in one leaf) less than HALF that; a serious "hit" in m-a-m and LF resonance. Bottom line - inside out walls are a great idea (if you are REALLY cramped for space) but NOT if you're looking for best isolation. Green glue - costs approximately as much as TWO MORE LAYERS of 5/8 drywall - I've been too swamped to keep up with Brian (probably couldn't even if I had NOTHING else to do :roll: ) - but I'm wondering if he's done a test wall comparing 2 layers of drywall WITH GG to FOUR layers of drywall WITHOUT... Still, for a CEILING where weight is more critical (and if isolation is also critical), GG makes a LOT of "cents". ANY acoustic treatment that "traps" sound will negatively affect isolation, but ESPECIALLY RESONANT traps like panels, helmholtz - only way around this is to OVER-build the containment shell(s) to compensate. Isolation and "sweet" sound are nearly ALWAYS at odds with each other, don't know another way around this. Filling CB (concrete block) with sand AFTER construction - not fun, never done it. Don't know of any reasonable way. Some thoughts - If possible, drill 1" holes down through the upstairs wall plates and the sill plates on top of the block, get one of those long funnels (used for adding transmission fluid to cars) and pour the sand in from above - then fill the holes with Fixall or similar patching compound. This could ALSO be tricky, depending on accessibility. Drill 1" holes in the SIDE of each top block, in each cavity, same funnel, constant vibration to work the sand into the holes - this is MADNESS. Also, every few blocks will be ALREADY FILLED with CONCRETE but ONLY AT THE TOP, for "J" bolts - finding out which ones, and how far down the concrete goes, would be my idea of purgatory if not Hell itself. Also, these holes would need to be filled afterward with the CONCRETE version of Fixall or another concrete patch product. Third, most expensive way - a high output air compressor and sand blast system, build a curved nozzle out of steel brake line (tubing bender required), right angle drill, drill the sill plates, insert curved nozzle, and let the sand blast down into the cavity til full. You would need to keep the nozzle aimed straight down into the wall so you don't blast a hole in the side of the block, it's gonna be messy, if you BUY the compressor it'll cost you about $1700 (that's what mine cost a year ago, and it's about the smallest that would keep up - a TRUE 5 horse, 80-gallon INDUSTRIAL version [draws 25 amps @ 240 volts] that will sustain 22 CFM @ 175 PSI indefinitely) - also, the sand will erode your curved "nozzle" fairly quickly so you'd probably need to make about 30 of these before you're done... A lower mass, but more hole version of this (Rod once suggested) is to drill holes in each cavity maybe 2 feet apart (that's about 72 holes in our hypothetical 8' x 12' wall) and fill with spray foam, then patch holes with concrete patch - no extra mass, but will alleviate the 3-leaf effect of hollow block AND a gypsum wall... 'Nuff rambling (note, NOT "ragging" :wink: ), gotta go play Dad/Gramps, haven't seen me perfect new grandson yet... Steve
