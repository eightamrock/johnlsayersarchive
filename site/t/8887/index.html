Cram wrote:
Double Wall assembly . . .I plan on having 2 layers of 5/8 drywall on the outside facing wall and the same on the inside along with r13 insulation in between. . . what’s a guide or recommended amount of spacing between these wall assemblies? Will one inch between walls suffice?
One inch of separation between double framed 2x4 walls translates to an air gap of 8". That's really good, and in fact is the main benefit of double wall framing because it's impossible to get an air gap much smaller than that. If you have the space and can easily afford another four inches, then that's even better, especially for low frequencies. I remember reading a couple of years ago when I started my build that 12" is kind of a "sweet spot" where more is still better but the improvement is more negligible and requires even greater gaps in order to have noticable improvement.

Cram wrote:
Next layer of Drywall in between studs? 5/8 or 2 layers of ½?
You can possibly get away with no more than the one layer you have. Strictly speaking, 5/8" is better because it's more mass. There's consensus among the experts here that the concept of using varying thicknesses of material is inferior to using all thicker material -- the slight benefit of the variance is beat by the sheer mass of the thicker material.
Cram wrote:
Walls as they are attached to the slab - Should I use pressure treated wood on the bottom of my frames? Flood is not a concern, but moisture is. Anyone have experience with this or is it not much of a concern?
Pressure treated soleplates are typically required by modern building codes. There are also different levels of pressure treatment -- some are for indoor/outdoor use, and some is for outdoor use only. Bear in mind that the moisure tends to wick up from the floor, even if it is not visible. Sillseal is also a good product that can be used in conjunction with the pressure treated soleplates. I recommend anchor bolts for securing your soleplates to the floor, rather than concrete nails. It's more time consuming, but the result is far superior. 8) I realize you asked more questions than I answered. I only touched on things on which I have some familiarity. Hopefully others will chime in with their thoughts as well. --Keith :mrgreen:

BradJacob8 December 2007#22
If that is a vapor barrier between the concrete wall and new framing (picture: 2007_12_7 018.jpg) I believe this is on the wrong side. Once you insulate, the plastic will be between the insulation and concrete. I did the same thing and Rod pointed out that is needs to be on the "warm" side of the wall because otherwise, condensation will happen on both sides - and this can damage your rock. I ripped it out and had to insulate - THEN installed the plastic against the studs. So to simplify, you frame, insulate, then put up the plastic, and then rock. Hope this helps.
