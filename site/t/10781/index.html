gullfo30 June 2008#2
besides conduction through the floors and walls - concrete and brick are good isolators and good conductors... the windows also need attention. with the ceiling having multiple support beams which look like they drop pretty low, getting an even surface will be tricky. you should do some tests to determine how much sound is escaping through the concrete to determine if its possible to leave some of those beams exposed. leaving them exposed would then make your design considerations (trapping) easier. another option is a acoustic tiled ceiling just below the beams. this way you can hide a multitude of ugly bits into the space, add recessed lights, have a decent depth for trapping etc. i'd also consider the positioning of the desk and speakers - move them more to the front of the room and move that chair out of there. your most important consideration should be to make the mix position as neutral as possible - and since you look like you've drawn in mostly electric equipment, your "live space" doesn't have to be too live either.

gullfo2 July 2008#4
i think the stomping test is good, the bass amp test is also good. testing with a metal and rubber mallet is also good - what kind of impact noises and bass levels are making it through? if its not much that helps in terms of isolation because you'll need less work there. I'd add second layers of doors and windows. keep as much light as you can, plus resale later. worst case make plugs or hinged plugs. my comment on the beams - if you can leave them most exposed because of low isolation requirements, you can then use that space for trapping - you don't necessarily treat them as corners, you can use panels of porous absorption hanging down a bit. the option of hanging ceiling tiles with the additional absorption above would give a more regular ceiling, allow for recessed lights, air ducting etc. plus it might offset some irregularities that the lengthwise beam may give you (its not symmetrical and some diffraction will occur so a more even ceiling should help some). you could set the ceiling to be level with the lengthwise beam and possibly leave the width-wise beam exposed. depends on its proximity to your desk - diffraction off the beam would not be good so maybe consider you'll need to add some additional absorption there later. the desk position of 38% is a very good one but its a start point not a finish line... if that spot works and the chair on the one side doesn't make things behave oddly, then its all good. if not, plan on moving the desk and speakers around a bit (small movements) to get things just right.

marz31 July 2008#8
Ni Hao Lunatique! Looks like you are off to a good start with nicely documented photos and diagrams of your situation. It is even better that you can start from the bare shell. Another good thing is I believe you said you would use the upper storey of your 2 storey apartment, so direct Sound Flanking below is only going to affect your family and not the neighbours! Of course this may still be an issue for your family, but it is generally very expensive to overcome satisfactorily. IE - Your lower floor acts as a kind of Isolater to the people below on the 6th floor. I think that it would still definitely be worth it to make a room within a room inside your apartment building, even without the floating floors: Many argue against buidling a floating floor and in your case, you probabaly could not do it anyway - it needs to be a really heavy concrete slab and well engineered to be effective. Most suggest building your new timber walls directly on the existing concrete floor of your upper storey. You could then put Timber boards - plywood or OSB directly on top of the concrete and finish with a thin layer of laminate flooring. (You will probably start to run out of room height at this point! Given that you are building on a pre-engineered floor, I highly recommend that you build a timber-frame inner wall as your 2nd leaf. You can build this wall (100 to 150 mm thick timber wall) from 50 to 150 mm away from the existing brick and mortar walls and fill with Fluffy Fibreglass or Mineral wool type Insulation. Then fasten a double skin layer of plasterboard on the inside of these walls as per Rod's book's designs. The bigger the gap between the leafs, the better the isolation to a point, but obviously a trade off with room size. I believe this will be much lighter than another brick wall and less of an engineering issue. It also lends itself to doing much of it yourself if you have a practical hand or friends who could help you. This also allows you to build a 2nd leaf of the ceiling on a timber framework sitting on top of these new timber walls, forming a 2 leaf structure, well isolated from the people on the 9th floor above you. You will need to be creative around the Concrete beams below the ceiling (Your soffits) and the 2nd Ceiling Leaf will have to follow the existing ceiling shape without touching the concrete beams to be effective. It does not look like you could use this as your final ceiling height which could have worked, but it looks lower than the windows. IE you will have to build some framework around the beams to suit, and the walls which also will need to be built under the Beams. Do-able, just needs some thinking about it and some way to hide in your final studio treatment and Air Conditioning. Many also argue against even using the Neoprene pads, though I did place Neoprene pads underneath my wall frames, but not the under the floor. My studio is not yet finished, so I cant give any feedback on the success. I think the general consensus is that if you can build the 2nd Timber frame walls with at least 2 layers of 12mm or even 15mm Plasterboard, you are already off to a good start. If you are going to use the RC, you may not need the Celotex as well - I am not sure what it is? Even the RC may be overkill, and you will need to use it on the ceiling as well. If you build the ceiling timber frames sitting on top of 2nd leaf Timber frame walls, you probably dont need to put Neoprene pads there as well. But do ensure that the Ceiling framework is separated from the Concrete floor above 100% and treat it exactly like the walls. Generally for Timber frame ceiling of 3.6m span, you would need 150 mm deep timbers for the ceiling framework, again eating up room height. If you cannot 100% isolate the 2nd leaf of the ceiliing from the concrete floor above, then you may want to re-evaluate the whole room within a room idea. Possibly the hangers may work, if you can find some good quality ones, designed to carry a 2nd ceiling equivalent to your walls, IE 2 layers of 12mm or 15mm plasterboard with insulation. This is an interesting engineering challenge. If there is any shorting out of the isolation and weak joins to the walls, then the whole exercise may be a waste of money. Wood floors seem to get the most votes, especially for a guitarist and you have the flexibility to add carpet if you need a less "bright" sound later on. Building in this way also allows you to make really Massive doors and covers for the windows in a way that is most efficient with maximum Isolation. See again Rod's suggested designs for Doors and Windows. This means that you could probably leave the existing windows and doors as they are and save some money there. You could make the frames and doors any size you want within reason and building code, but probably allow the outer door to pass through the inner door. I have no idea of material costs in China, but be ready to see your studio eat up lots of money!!!! You especially want to take care about all openings in the wall for wiring, both audio and electrical. These need to be well sealed and air tight. This applies especially to all the plasterboard joints as well. You will need to be a good supervisor or do it yourself to ensure it is properly done as the specs are not the same for standard construction. Good luck with your build. regards Marz
