David French19 April 2006#2
If you are attempting to determine the modal frequencies of your room, place a speaker, a sub if you've got one, in a corner where the floor meets two walls. You don't need a dodec becuase in the freq range we're interested in all speakers are pretty much omni. Put the mic in the exact opposite corner: diagonally across the room right in the corner where the ceiling meets two walls. Get the mic's diaphragm as close as possible to the walls and ceiling without touching them. For this work, what type of excitation signal you use is of little consequence; both pink and sweeps will get the job done. If you need help I'd be happy to send you a test tone and process it for you to show you the modes. I love doing this kind of thing, and every new room is a new chance to learn.

David French19 April 2006#4
I would do two tests. To find the modal frequencies, do what I first told you. To study the effects of the traps, put a full range speaker at 38% of the distance between parallel walls in all dimensions and put the mic in the opposite spot in all dimensions. This idea is difficult to elucidate, so an exanple is probably a good idea. If the room is, say, 20 x 14 x 9, put a speaker at 20 x .38 = 7.6 from a lenght dimension wall, 14 x .38 = 5.32 from a width dimension wall, and so on. You really woudn't have to do this; so long as you had the mic and speaker in the same spot for the before and after it would be ok, but the 38% mark should give most of the modes a fair chance at developing.

David French27 April 2006#13
I just re-read what you posted about the two tests you'd suggest doing. The one where you're placing the speaker (sub) and mic at opposite corners of the room, that's to find the modes of your room? And a frequency sweep from, say, 40 hz up to 300 hz would be ok for that? Would a pencil style condenser be ok for that? If not, what mic would you suggest?
Yes, it's to identify the modes of your room. This kind of thing isn't usually necessary because knowledge of the exact frequencies at which your room resonates doesn't change the treatment strategy. If you're testing, you may as well just do a full 20 Hz - 20 kHz sweep. As far as mics go, you will need an unequalized (flat) omni with a very small diaphragm. The Behringer ECM8000 is a popular cheap choice, but I think the Nady CM-100 may be an even cheaper alternative, though I haven't tried it. It's not a big deal to build a flat omni, so don't worry about quality too much if you just want the mic for testing. If you want a mic that will have value as a recording mic as well, look at the Earthworks mics and maybe the Audix TR-40.
After the room is treated with traps do you suggest doing that same test again? With the mic and speaker as close as possible to the same spot as the before test? Or the second test you posted about? The full range speaker, that would be one with a tweeter, mid, and 15", right? I guess I don't quite understand the second test you suggested.
I wouldn't bother with the modal test since first it's mostly academia and second you will probably need to put sonething in the corners anyway. Try the second test. For the test to be perfectly valid, you shouldn't move the speaker or the mic until both the before and after tests are complete. Also leave the gains and volumes alone. This test is designed so that the major resonances of the room are somwhat evenly excited so that you can see the effect the treatment has on all of them. It's just a good general source/reciever position. By full range speaker, I just mean one that can produce the full frequency spectrum, or as much of it as possible.
