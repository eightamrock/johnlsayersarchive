I guess I don't understand what you mean there. Is that sequence starting from the outer leaf going in, or from the inner leaf going out? If it is the former, then it seems you are building your inner lead "inside-out"? Is that what you mean by saying that the final layer is "rockwool" followed by "airspace"? If so, then what is the purpose of those two sheets of drywall that are tight up against the existing concrete walls? I can't see what purpose they serve. Or if it is the other sequence (inner leaf going out) then it seems you are building a three-leaf wall! If I'm reading that correctly, then starting from the existing concrete walls as your outer leaf, you have the following sequence: Concrete | airgap-with-rockwool | 2-sheets-drywall | airgap-with-rockwool | 2-sheets-drywall Is that correct? If so, are you aware of the reduced low frequency isolation that you get from building a 3-leaf wall, instead of a 2-leaf wall? Also, I'm curious why you are specifying 100 kg/m3 density for the rockwool in the walls. There isn't much extra benefit once the density gets beyond 48 kg/m3. It will cost you more, but it won't help you much extra. Optimum density is 48 kg/m3. The air gap inside your wall doesn't seem to be enough, either, at only 50 mm. The normal recommendation is at least 100mm, for several reasons: more depth is greater isolation, more depth reduces the resonant frequency of the MSM system, and more depth gives you more space to put in more rockwool. The other comment I had is on the rather thin drywall you are specifying: 9mm plus 12.5mm. Most people here use 16mm, for two main reasons: extra mass, and lower resonant frequency. Two sheets of 16mm will give better isolation than what you mentioned. Based on that design, it seems like you don't need a lot of isolation, and especially not in the low frequencies. Is that correct? Because if you do need very good low frequency isolation, that wall design doesn't seem to be what you need. It's a 3-leaf wall with low mass and two thin air gaps, but what you need for good low frequency isolation is a 2-leaf wall with high mass and one single thick air gap.

Then Stuart in order to see if we need to float or not the rooms is by the hummer test?
I'm not sure what you mean by the "hummer test", but the bets way to see how much isolation you already have is simply to make lots of noise in the existing room, and measure it in other places around the building, inside and out. The easiest way that I know of to do that is to pump out pink noise from a full-range speaker, at the same level that you will normally have inside the room when you are recording. then take a sound level meter (or spectrum analyzer) to the places around the building that you are concerned about, and measure the levels there. You need to do two measurements, of course one with your speaker running full blast, and the other with your speaker turned off. So for example you'd set up a full-range speaker to produce 120 dB(C) in the room, then shut all the doors and windows and walk around the rest of the building noting the measurements on paper. Then repeat the same "walk around the building" with the speaker off, to compare the levels. Wear ear protection in the room, of course: 120 dB will leave you with hearing damage after a few minutes. And warn the people in the building tat you are going to do the test beforehand! - Stuart -
