Finally got a chance to do some looking at your project; without that center beam, you'd be lucky to get 10 PSF dead load. With it, I think 15 is conservative unless it's the BEAM itself that's the limiting factor - 2x12 SP spans up to 12'2" with 60/20 loading (highest 20 lb dead load my calculator shows) Keep in mind that your plywood, and the weight of the joists themselves, adds up to about 6 PSF (both of which are counted in these calculations) - so if you really ARE limited to 15 PSF, you could only add about 9 PSF more weight - not NEARLY enough for a "real" floated floor. In addtion to that, if you already have wallboard on the ceiling of the garage AND plywood on the TOP side of your joists, that's your two leaves of mass. knightfly25 June 2006#34
I get the same, 12'2", when using #2 southern pine - which means that, as long as that beam is strong enough, you could put MORE weight up there since your effective span is only 7'6" - what screwed up your calculations is using "stud" grade - use @2, plain instead, and you'll get 100/20 load for span up to 9'11" - this means you can put even MORE dead load than 20# (not sure how MUCH more, tables don't cover it) and still be OK. Dead load includes weight of framing, plus any other materials mounted to that framing, either on top or bottom of the frame - so in your case, figure the weight per square foot of your upper plywood plus the weight of your framing plus the weight of the ceiling gypsum that's attached to the bottom of your joists - 1-1/8" T&G floor ply weighs about 3.6 PSF, 5/8" gypsum weighs about 2.35 PSF, and 2x12's on 24" centers contribute about 2.4 PSF to dead load (s.pine is close to doug fir, where I got this figure); add these up, and you get about 8.4 PSF dead load - figuring that you can probably get close to 25 PSF with your spans (assuming that beam isn't the limiting factor) that leaves about 16 PSF you could add. A layer of 3/4" MDF plus a layer of 5/8" gypsum on top would add 3.2 (MDF) + 2.35 (5/8 gyp) for a total of around 14 PSF dead load; still well within your total of approx. 25 PSF dead load (we still need info on that beam )... Steve

knightfly26 June 2006#36
Yeah; if your floor's total area is (say) 300 square feet, and you're loading it (dead load) with 20 PSF, then the total weight would be 300 x 20, or 6000 pounds; divide that by 3 (2 outside walls and the beam) assuming even loading, and you get 2000 pounds per support - there is calculation software that will figure the deflection limits of a beam based on total length, type of material, distributed load, etc - this is what would need to be figured for your beam to be sure it isn't the weak link in your floor support chain. Live load (people, piano's, pets, etc) would also need to be calculated for both the beam and your floor joists, so it would be a good idea for you to make a list of all the gear you intend to have in the studio and the maximum # of people you'd have up there at any given time. If your engineer can't figure this out, I hope you're not paying him much :wink: Steve

sharward28 June 2006#41
If you're going to use that "initial" door as your outer leaf door, then you have to beef up everything leading up to that door (and probably the door itself too), then hang an inner door on your inner leaf walls. Your drawing shows your inner door on your outer leaf. Two outer leaf doors with a gaping hole in your inner leaf would perform horribly. However, having a good distance between inner leaf and outer leaf doors (as I described it above) could perform really well. The space between the doors would be what is often referred to as a "sound lock." I think we're not totally communicating on the height thing. 7' is your minimum height, but most of your room, even right now without any modifications, has less than 7' of clearance. I think you're focused on the highest point of the room. Obviously if the peak of the room is 6', 11", that's not going to fly. But even if you can manage getting having the peak be 7' 4" or 7' 5", it may still not fly if it the majority of the room is progressivelly lower than that. There are accommodations in code for this -- I think it's based on a percentage of floor area having a certain amount of headroom. Explore this with your building department. You may find that the only way you can do it legally is to have drastically less floor space, which may make the room too small to be practical. As an example, Portland, Oregon, says this on their Web site: "In attics and half stories, the ceiling height may be a minimum of 6'8" if it is over an area no smaller than 6' x 10', and if it is at least 5 feet high over an area no smaller than 10' x 10'." --Keith :mrgreen:
