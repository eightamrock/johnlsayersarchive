Hi Jake, and Welcome! :)
so I expect the noise to get anywhere near 110dB at times
Drums can actually go quite a bit louder than that: Count on 115, perhaps even heading for 120.
The garage is a separate structure on a single concrete slab
That's good news!
of a wooden frame with a weatherboard exterior and a corrugated steel roof.
That's not so good news. That roof will need "beefing up" with mass to create your outer leaf, or alternatively you'll need to go with a 3-leaf system, blocking off the attic space entirely.
I tried to account for standing waves, making my opposite walls at least 12 degrees offset in both rooms.
That won't do much for room modes (standing waves). It will help for flutter echo, but not modes. That's a common myth, actually: Angling (or "splaying") your walls does not eliminate room modes: it just moves them to a different frequency and location, and makes them harder to calculate.
I wanted to angle the studio ceiling but I don't think it is possible with the limited height.
Right. Just keep it flat, then hang a hard-backed, angled cloud.
is if I am putting drywall on both sides of my inner structure,
:shock: :cop: :roll: You don't do that! :!: You only put drywall on ONE side of your inner-leaf. Never on both sides.
, having gotten confirmation that I can put the outer layers of drywall on the frame before standing it up.
Once again, you do NOT need to put drywall on both sides of your inner-leaf framing. It only goes on ONE side. That can be either the side facing the air gap, or it can be the side facing the room. But only one, not both.
I have since learned that I only need resilient channel on the inner layer. From what I can read decoupling the outer layer would be a waste of money if I have already decoupled on the inner layer.
You arelay HAVE the outer leaf! It is the existing garage. That is already there. It might need beefing up, but you don't need another one. All you need now is your INNER leaf, which is just stud framing with drywall on only ONE side of the studs.
I am actually probably going to go with resilient mounts for furring channel as it is available from my local hardware store.
... and increase your costs considerably! :) You do not need RSIC clips, or furring channel, or RC, or anything similar. As you already mentioned you are going to put up your inner leaf as studs with drywall on them: that's all the decoupling you need. As long as you make sure that your inner-leaf walls do not touch the existing garage structure at any point, then you are fine.
It is slightly less though as the garage doors take off 430mm when open.
Does it need to open, ever again, after your studio is built? Most people just fis the garage doors in place permanently, then remove the mechanism and build a wall just a few cm away from the now-fixed door, to complete the outer leaf. Then build the inner-leaf.
I am still open to suggestions on layout and ceiling
Your ceiling should be done the same as the walls: outer leaf (roof), and inner-leaf (which rests ONLY on the inner-leaf walls, not touching the outer leaf anywhere).
I am struggling to find much information about anchoring my frame to the existing concrete slab. I would really appreciate any recommendations in this area.
Just use anchor bolts. After the walls are up, drill down through the sole plate and into the concrete, then drop in an expanding anchor bolt and tighten it up. Do that every few feet, as required by your local building code.
Will I be losing much isolation if I use the standard construction procedure of anchoring the frame,
You still need to create the seal: Before you put the sole plate in position, run three beads of caulk along it, or along the floor where it will be. One bead goes down the center-line of the sole plate, then the other two go about 2cm each side of that, so you have three full-length seals. Then after it is bolted down, caulk along the edges to create two more seals. Then caulk under each layer of drywall as you put it up, for additional seals. - Stuart -

Soundman20209 September 2014#6
I think I will be able to achieve the isolation I need with a well built inner leaf.
If you don't seal the outer leaf, and put enough mass on it, then the inner leaf will basically act like a single leaf, subject to mass law, and not so much like a 2-leaf MSM system. So you'd probably get about 6 dB to 10 dB of additional isolation, beyond what you have now. Leaving the garage door in place and operable also severely limits the maximum height for the room.
At the moment I am getting about 30dB reduction at 6 foot from existing structure with a sound source of 100dB.
The correct place to measure is at three feet from the wall, so likely you are getting around about 25 dB reduction. Adding the inner leaf will increase that to something like 30 to 35 dB of isolation. So your 100 dB level inside will be around 65 to 70 dB outside.
I plan to be doing a lot of treatment in the rooms to deal with reflections but that will be another topic altogether once I have this built!
It is better to design the treatment at the same time as you design the rest of the room. Otherwise you normally end up with conflicts that cannot be resolved, except be taking things apart and re-building them. Issues such as needing to move doors or windows so that the treatment can go where it is needed, for example, or HVAC ducts, registers, or silencer boxes being in places where treatment needs to go, or where HVAC airflow will be interrupted by treatment. The entire design should be absolutely complete in all aspects before you start building anything. if not, you are pretty much guaranteed to run into problems that will prevent you from doing things optimally.
Unfortunately I don't have the option of inside out walls, as I will need the exposed joists on the ceiling
I don't see the connection there at all! Why would ceiling joists interfere with whether or not you can have inside out walls? There's no relationship that I can see.
as I will need the exposed joists on the ceiling to be on the outside to fit between the existing joists of the shed, allowing me a lot more ceiling room.
If you do it that way, you will be LOSING height in your final finished ceiling surface! :wink: Think it through... :) with an inside out ceiling, the drywall goes just below the outer-leaf joists, then come the inner-leaf joists which have the final treatment in between them, so the bottom surface of the inner-leaf joists IS the lowest point in the room. If you do it conventionally, then your ceiling drywall might be a little higher than what the bottom surface of the joists would have been for the inside-out ceiling, but then you still need to hang your ceiling treatment below that, so the final surface is a lot lower... Try out both alternatives in your SketchUp model, so you can see the difference....
I am going to head out to some local air con places and see what they recommend.
Careful with that approach! Very, very few air conditioning stores have even the vaguest idea of what a recording studio needs, or how it is built, or how to install a system for a studio. It is far better to work through the math yourself, then go to the store and tell them what you need.
it seems as though I am possibly looking for two quiet mini-splits that have the ability to supply fresh air.
Those are really expensive, and likely don't supply enough air anyway. A studio needs about 8 air changes per hour, so figure out how much that is for your studio, check the specs for the unit you are thinking of buying, and see how close they are....
Am I missing anything?
yep.... :) While a mini-split with a fresh air supply might be able to bring some air into the room, where does it go after that? :shock: You also need ducting to exhaust the stale air from the room. In a normal house installation for those things, that isn't a problem since houses are very "leaky", so the small amount of exhaust air will find its own path out: But studios are sealed air-tight. there is path out. So you have to create one, with ducts, silencer boxes and fans. And since you need to go to all that trouble anyway, you might just as well buy a cheaper mini-split without that feature, and do the ducting properly... :)
two layers of drywall, different thickness,
That's a myth: you do not need different thicknesses of drywall. Yes, there is a slight advantage in impedance mismatch from doing that, but you would more than make up for that from the increased mass and reduced flexibility by just making both of the layers as thick as the thicker one. So 2 layers of 5/8" will give you better isolation than one layer of 5/8 plus one layer of 1/2.
and green glue(or equivalent).
There is no "equivalent" :) :!: 8) Or at least, there are others that claim to be equivalent, but their supporting documentation is rather wanting... Always be aware of any manufacturer that makes wonderful claims for their acoustic products, but then does not back it up by supplying the results of testing conducting in an independent, reputable acoustical laboratory... :)
I understand this is quite a vague guide to materials at the moment but would it be possible to get an estimate on what sort of isolation you think I will be able to achieve without beefing up the outer layer?
At a rough guess, I would estimate something in the region of 35 dB to 40 dB of isolation, with luck. - Stuart -
