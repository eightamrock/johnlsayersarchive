Soundman20201 April 2010#28
regarding the inside gypsum boards, i thought that as well as two on the outside of theframe you also had to put some on the inside of the frame when building an inside out wall.
NOPE! That would make a three-leaf wall, which is something you do not want. (And isn't necessary in any case, for your situation). You already have concrete blocks for one leaf. You only need one other leaf, which will be either the fiber-cement board or the drywall, but NOT both. Two-leaves, only, ever. Never one, never three. Just two. (Unless you have some really unusual issues!). The purpose of building inside-out is so that you can use the space between the studs for room TREATMENT. Acoustic treatment is deep, and has to be hung on the walls anyway. But if you build inside out, then it can go in the space BETWEEN the studs, instead of on top of the drywall. But you still have to fill the wall cavity with insulation. The cavity is the 4" gap between your concrete blocks and your drywall. Regardless of what you do inside the room itself for treatment, you need that insulation in the cavity as part of the MSM system. Also, don't forget to seal those concrete blocks before you start building! They are porous, which isn't good. You need to seal that. Stuck, plaster, paint... anything that seals well will work fine.
the framing is 2x3 reason being that the rockwool that most seem to use is 3" thick.
Yeah, but that framing has to support a LOT of weight! Add up the weight of all of that fiber-cement board, and all of the ceiling drywall, and all of the framing, and all of the caulking, and all of the insulation, and all of the screws / nails / Green Glue, and all of the electrics, and all of the lights, and all of the acoustic treatment, and all of the... OK, I think you get the picture. Those walls have to support thousands of pounds of mass. I'm not sure that 2x3 studs is enough to do that, especially if you are going to use 2x4's on the ceiling. Code probably doesn't even allow that. You might want to check with a builder or engineer.
with the inner walls covered in plasterboard i can get them skimmed prior to painting them.
With inside-out construction, there is no plasterboard! Nothing to paint. either build slot resonators over the walls, or just cover them with cloth if you only plan to use absorption. Or use the stud space for panel traps, or whatever else your room needs. But no plasterboard, and no paint. - Stuart -

Soundman20201 April 2010#31
well saying i did put the other layers of gypsum board on the inside ... will the extra internal boards help with sound isolation ?
Actually, they will REDUCE your isolation! Stop looking at me like I'm nuts! That might sound illogical and counter-intuitive, and it is, but nevertheless, it is true: Adding a third leaf to a two-leaf wall really can reduce isolation in the low end of the spectrum. "Low end of the spectrum" is the place where kick drums, toms, bass guitars, the lowest octaves on keyboards, some percussion, and other Pink Floyd favorite tones live. The reason for this is kind of complex, but basically your two-leaf wall is a tuned filter. It pass sound through at the frequency it is tuned to, and blocks sound at all other frequencies (with various degrees of success). If you design and build it right, your wall will be tuned to have a resonant frequency that is LOWER than the bottom end of the audible spectrum. At its resonant frequency that wall will be a really lousy isolator, and will in fact amplify and assist sound through it, at that frequency. At all higher frequencies, it gets progressively better at filtering (isolating). At twice the resonant frequency, it already does a reasonable job of isolating, and it just gets better and better as you move up the scale from there. All well and good so far, right? The wall is tuned low enough that it isolates all the frequencies that we humans can hear and are concerned about. BUT! If you add a THIRD leaf to it (where a leaf is just a massive surface separated by an air gap), then what you ave done is to RE-TUNE the wall, such that the resonant frequency is now much higher than it was before! So now, the frequency band where the wall is a really lousy isolator has moved up into the range where we humans CAN hear them, and thus your wall becomes a bad isolator at low frequencies. Pink Floyd is now thumping in your neighbor's living room, as well as in your studio... You can compensate for this "three-leaf effect" by adding even more mass to the leaves of your wall, and also by increasing the air gaps. That will re-tune the wall to a lower frequency once again. But at the expense of more mass (=more money) and reducing the usable floor space inside your room. You can do that if you want, but why would you??? :) If you really are concerned about being "too loud", then it would be much, MUCH better to put that same drywall on the other side of the studs, right along with the fiber-cement board, so that you get back to a two-leaf wall again: The extra mass on the second leaf would be very beneficial, and would greatly improve your isolation. But if you make it into a third leaf, it will do the opposite. Maybe this graph might give you a better idea of the issue:
Image not preserved: 2-leaf-vs-3-leaf-700pix.jpg
The line marked "mass law" is the theoretical best isolation that you can get from a single-leaf wall, which is basically just a solid lump of mass (such as your existing concrete brick wall. The line marked "double panel" is what you get from a 2-leaf wall, and the line marked "triple panel" is what you get from a 3-leaf wall. What you will notice on that graph is that it is really hard to isolate low frequencies (left hand side of the graph) no matter what you do! But a 2-leaf does a better job than a single-leaf provided that you tune the resonant frequency low enough, and it also does a better job than a 3-leaf. This is not intuitive, but is obvious when you see the graph. At high frequencies a 3-leaf is better than a 2-leaf, but it doesn't matter since a 2-leaf is already pretty damn good by the time you get up to the frequencies where it makes a difference. However at the lower end of the spectrum, a 3-leaf is always worse than a 2-leaf, all other factors being equal. So you really do not want to put that drywall on the outside of the studs if you plan on playing Pink Floyd in there! It would be good if all you ever plan to do is have piccolo solos in there, but any music with considerable low frequency content is going to be worse off with a 3-leaf. Having said all that, what you really should do is see just how good your existing isolation is, by measuring real sound in your real room with a real sound level meter. Play your favorite Pink Floyd at the loudest level you ever expect to produce, then measure that inside the room, then measure it outside the room in many locations, both inside and outside the house. Note down all the readings on the meter at all of those locations, and circle the HIGHEST reading. That is your worse-case. Now progressively turn down the volume inside the room until you can no longer detect any sound at all, at ALL of those locations. In other words, turn it down a bit and measure again, then turn it down some more and measure again. Note down the reading on the meter at all of the locations, at the level where you cannot hear the music any more, and circle the LOWEST reading. (Do this test late at night when everyone is asleep, and the ambient level is at its lowest.) OK, so now you have three numbers: 1) The level inside the room with Floyd belting out full blast, 2) The highest level outside your room with Floyd belting out full blast 3) The target level for perfect isolation, with Floyd whimpering along vaguely. Subtract 2 from 1: This is how much isolation you have right now. Subtract 3 from 2: This is how much additional isolation you need, in order to keep the cops away from your front door. :) With those numbers in hand, you can figure out the best design and construction method to attain the target level of isolation. - Stuart -

Soundman20201 April 2010#35
the lights would be ceiling mount lights ... that recess into the ceiling,
Careful!!!! If you do that, you will have to cut holes in your inner-leaf. In order for the MSM system to work properly, each leaf should be one solid sheet of mass, with no holes in it. Cutting a hole, or even a small crack under the wall, is enough to damage the isolation. It will cost you many decibels of isolation. You can still do recessed lighting, of course, but you have to make up for the hole by building mass around the back of the light fixture (to the same level as the drywall was giving you), and sealing it air tight. Both leaves must be sealed hermetically airtight, for optimum performance. So any place you are planning to cut any kind of hole, you need to think about that carefully to plan how you are going to seal it up again, and make up for the lost mass. The same applies to things like electrical sockets, light switches, microphone patch boxes, etc. The best way to avoid those issues is to surface mount all of these, on top of the drywall surface, without penetrating at all. Use surface mount cable ducts, too, so even the wires don't penetrate the drywall.
it would have been good to have put the boards on the inside also simply because i wanted to skim and paint in there.
Well, you don't HAVE to build the wall inside out! Instead of putting all the mass on the cavity side of the studs, you can put it all on the room-side of the studs instead. In that case, you can paint and decorate to your heart's content. And then you can cover most of it with the acoustic treatment that you will undoubtedly need.
on this build the owner a commercial architect had his building skimmed in a special facade paint, this blocks water and dirt and basically keeps itself clean.

That stuff you linked to sounds like it will do the trick for sure, but you can probably get something less fancy and a bit cheaper for the wall face inside the cavity: nobody will ever see it in there once the wall is up, so it can be whatever you want that does the job. - Stuart -
