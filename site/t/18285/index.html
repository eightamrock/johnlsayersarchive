That will help to damp the vibration a bit, but it won't fully eliminate the noise: That is basically impact noise, and will be transmitted to the building structure itself. Since it isn't realistic to think about beefing up such a large area of metal with extra mass as well, you might want to think about doing your studio as a three-leaf system. This might be one of those cases where it makes sense to do that.

100 = 38 °C, 110 = 43 °C, 120 = 49 °C. That's hot, no matter which way you look at it! :shock:
Bottom line is I need some serious A/C or I will fry in there.
Yup!
Hopefully 125 amps will handle that plus plugs, lights; it would be a major expense to upgrade the service.
That's nearly 14 kVA. That should be good for about 400,000 btu/hr of cooling, assuming an extremely inefficient 10:1 system. You should be fine, unless you plan to record an entire symphony orchestra in there with 120° outside! But that's just very rough, "back of the envelope" calculation, so it would be sensible to do all the HVAC math properly, and if necessary size the actual studio interior small enough that you won't need excessive cooling capacity. Also consider low consumption lighting, that not only draws less power but also produces less heat. Etc.
I do a few things musically. Score movies. Produce my own rock/pop music for my band which has become a virtual recording band because we are spread all over the place. Mix and master tracks for other people (online mostly).
So for the control room, you will need a large video screen at the front, a 5.1 system (or 7.1?), and accurate acoustics. In other words, the room needs to be designed as a multi-channel room, based on the ITU, EBU and AES specs. Would that be about right?
Probably maximum levels will be 85 Db.
That's the "standard" calibration level, and it is smart to use 80 to 85 as a reference mixing level, especially for film work.
Since I have the space, I would like to make this room an optimum size, I have read that somewhere around 16' x 30' works well?
For a multichannel "critical listening" room, the ITU-R BS.1116-1 specs call for a minimum floor area of 30 m2, and no more than 70m2 (roughly 320 ft2 to 750 ft2). 16x30 fits in there nicely. But it is not just floor area: you also need to consider room ratio and total room volume. The standard "reference" room has a volume of 100m3 (3530 ft3), so that's a good one to shoot for. So look for a good ratio in that region.
I also want to have a small isolation room, maybe 10' x 12' for vocals and acoustic guitar and another room where I can set up a kit of drums and some amps.
So this is basically a three-room facility? Control room plus iso booth plus live room? You have plenty of space to do that, so this should work out very well.
I also need an office, a restroom and a small storage room.
OK, but those are not really considered to be in the acoustic part of the studio: they don't need any isolation or special treatment. One thing that would need to be taken into account is the HVAC system, which will need to cool both the studio and these "utility" rooms: that needs to be separate into two sections, independent. You don't want bathroom noises (and odors!) getting into your control room, live room and vocal booth...
I am thinking about maybe using the back of my main room as the office.
If you are doing serious mastering, I would put the office in a separate room. Mastering studios need to be very accurate, acoustically, and typical office stuff might not fit in well with that. You could maybe have an office just off to one side of the control room, through sliding glass doors that can be left open to give you easy access to the office area, but closed when you need to do critical listening.
If my listening position is say a third back
The theoretical best point is 38% back from the front wall. But that's just theory, and in reality most engineers do prefer to be a bit forward of that point.

Your place has huge potential! I look forward to watching the build unfold. In terms of insulating / quietening the steel roof, I have used SPU -spray polyurethane foam. It's expensive but can work well for this kind of structure. It does not have a mass advantage for isolation but does tend to seal leaks extremely well. You have so many decisions like this to make, so give yourself time to think it all over. Another expensive option to consider is a balanced power isolator. You might be surprised at the electrical noise reduction you get and in a well-designed, quiet room, it can make a real difference, especially when you run A/C, heat, etc., on the same mains. Best of luck!
