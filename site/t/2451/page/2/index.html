knightfly26 October 2004#28
Yeah, I've mentioned this here and there but it's not in the REFERENCE section as far as I know; A lot of times, using enough fberglass to take care of bass trapping, mids, flutter, etc, causes too much high freq absorption; a thin layer of plastic over parts of the absorbent can keep the highs from being over-absorbed. John's had good luck with this in more than one studio. Also, sometimes it's a real bitch getting rockwool into cavities over RC without major "shedding" - I've suggested to a few members with this problem that they first put the batts into plastic garbage bags (one over each end, taped together) and it's helped solve that problem. If, for some reason, that made the room TOO bright, you could always cut away the front of the plastic AFTER the rockwool is installed - My thoughts on this are that you can get all your calculated absorbent in place WITHOUT the cloth facing, then listen to the room for a while (with music playing :? ) and if it's already too dull, start adding plastic (NOT at first reflection points, if it's a CR) until the room sounds just a LITTLE too bright; If you then add a cloth cover, the cloth will take the room back to more absorbent at highs, and the end result should be just right. Hope that helped... Steve

Hi PhiloBeddoe, Yea, the HVAC stuff took a little to figure out, rather it took a little time to get my service guy to figure out. Luckily he was a friend so I didn't have to pay him for all the research time, plus I think it was a challenge since he'd never done anything like this before. Since my room is totally underground and didn't think we'd have too much trouble heating and cooling it, he thought it OK to tie into my main system, plus it was big enough to handle it. My main concerns were to keep the air transfer noise to a minimum and keep as little sound transfer from my studio back through the rest of the duckwork. Here's how we attempted that and I'll let you know how successful we were when I get the music up and running. First we just used regular insulated flexible ducking for the pipes, we just made a few extra turns than would have been normally. Once he figured out how much air flow we needed for each room, we used large enough pipe for the feed and return. Then we built a load box for both sides out of duckboard, the load box or catch box serves a couple purposes, to slow down the air flow and kill most of the sound that enters it. Since duckboard is compressed fiberglass it seems to work well. Then we just feed each room out of the load box by using extra long feed lines. My returns I did the same way except something extra I added, we built a filter cage on the end of the return box and located it outside the studio in the garage, that way when I change the filter all the dust is left outside the studio. So far everything seems to be working like I wanted it to, I have plenty of air movement but it's at a volocity that doesn't give that 'whoosing' sound, plus I have send and return in both rooms, which should keep the vocal room comfortable, I also made another sound catch out of the duckboard where they enter the rooms. I hope all this make sense, if not let me know and I'll try to clarify. Hope you have good luck with it. Arthur

knightfly18 November 2004#39
"Why do you think it might resonate? Am I missing something? It seems to be a lot more dence than plain 3/4 laminate plywood" - Density isn't the only property that affects what a material does acoustically - for example, 1" plate steel weighs 40.82 lbs/sq.ft., or just over 1300 pounds for a 4x8 foot sheet; if you hang it from a cable and hit it with a soft mallet, you still get an audible response from it. So even the phenolic, which apparently weighs twice what a 5/8 gypsum panel weighs, and a bit over twice what 3/4 plywood weighs, will likely still have a resonance that's within the audible range. Baffle extensions should NOT resonate - so these will need to be damped no matter what material you use, unless that material is 12" thick depleted Uranium or lead... Steve

knightfly19 November 2004#41
Arthur, you're right - density IS your friend; but so is low resonance - all I'm saying is that your baffle extension should NOT also be a "passive radiator" its only function should be to extend the baffle so it forces the woofers into "half space" mode. Beyond that, it should be dead enough NOT to sympathetically vibrate, which means that it needs to be damped. This generally takes mass, as well as some degree of limpness - I'm not positive, but I think this is one reason using heavy plywood seems to work so well - it's just my gut feel, but I think the separate layers and glue of the 1-1/8" flooring plywood help minimize quite a bit of ringing. The ringing is what I'm a bit worried about - what little I've handled phenolic (much smaller pieces than yours) it just seemed like it would ring quite a bit. You can find this out fairly simple - take a piece of 2x2 about 6" long, rip it diagonally to make two triangular pieces 6" long, set them on the floor about a foot closer together than the side of one of your panels, have a helper help you set the panel on these so there are only two "knife edge" support points, then balance the panel so it only takes a light touch to keep it standing verticall, and tap the panel in different locations with a rubber mallet - if you hear ringing at any frequency, it needs to be damped. Bolting it to a frame would lower the resonant frequency; how much I don't now. Gluing heavy rockwool batts to the rear would also help. I'm really not trying to be an alarmist here; just pointing out things I would look at before committing to anything new and different... Steve

Paul Woodlock20 November 2004#43
Greetings Arthur In addition to Steve's cool advice..... Soffit walls need to be STIFF!!! To avoid the soffit wall becoming a passive radiator, you should decouple the speakers from the soffits/soffit wall with a resonant freqeuncy at least 2 to 4 octaves BELOW the lowest freqeuncy your speakers can emit. Genelec recommend a resonant freq of 2 to 8Hz. This is hard to do, but 10Hz is easily achievable. The speakers should be EXACTLY flush with the soffit wall. to prevent diffraction. Soffit mounted speakers not only give you an incease in bass levels ( compensated for by EQ ), but they also excite standing waves ( room modes ) more than free standing speakers, so you should heavily absorb the rear wall. I thoroughly recommend reading the Genelec papers on soffit mounting. here's the links http://www.genelec.com/support/soffit.php http://www.genelec.com/support/faq/faq20.php http://www.genelec.com/support/faq/faq19.php hth ;) Paul p.s. I've just finished building my soffits/soffit wall. See Paul's Studio Build Diary here....http://forum.studiotips.com/viewforum.php?f=1

knightfly22 November 2004#45
Arthur, not only should you cut to size for the test, but keep in mind that when you mount to your frame, you will LOWER the frequency of resonance by further increasing the overall mass - What I'm concerned with, (with nothing to back it up beyond gut feel and natural paranoia) is that the phenolic might have (for some reason) a really HIGH resonant frequency, so that (when attached to your frame) it will be at a REALLY audible frequency range. I'm not telling you that this won't work, I'm just suggesting caution when "blazing new trails" - that "limb" you whack off with your machete just might be the tail of a very large, pissed-off snake, or you could discover that the sap of the plant is a cure for cancer (sorry about the obscure crap, it's 5 AM and I'm not quite awake) Steve
