knightfly10 October 2004#13
Yeah, flanking noise can get you for sure - if you allow the sound to directly affect the concrete, the sound will travel along the concrete to other areas and vibrate whatever it's in contact with, eventually moving the air in that new location (IOW, now it's back to being AIRBORNE sound) The way to avoid this is isolated frames and inner leaves - if you have an inner mass leaf on a separate frame, then your ceiling is either resiliently hung from upper joists or resting on the inner wall frame, you've just broken the flanking path (other than the vibes getting into the concrete floor >concrete walls > upper walls > radiated into living space ) Here's an article on flanking that may be of interest - http://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/publications ... /96228.htm Although I think that covers more flanking from room to room due to framing if I'm not mistaken. Still, the concept is the same - you need to not allow sound to impinge on any surface that leaves the room. This is the idea behind room-in-a-room construction... Steve

Ptownkid13 October 2004#19
you wrote "broadband absorbers OR slat absorbers for larger room". What is the diff between the two, and what size rooms do they apply to?
knightfly13 October 2004#20
Differences - slat absorber, slot absorber, and perforated panel absorber are all names for various types of Helmholtz absorbers, named for their supposed inventor - they are all resonant traps, and as such have a fairly narrow bandwidth for absorption - there is a spreadsheet for their design located at the top of the Acoustics forum; it's corrected by me with the right formula (most on the net are WRONG) and John enhanced it for use with both metric and inch units. The slats/slots and cloth in front of a sealed, fiberglass absorbed cavity is what makes them tunable; with just the fiberglass, they become a broadband absorber that doesn't absorb to as LOW a frequency, but has a wider range of effectiveness. The smaller a room, the higher in frequency its resonant modes will be; the lowest dozen or so of these are always spaced too far apart to just "blend together", so to speak. Because of this, these lowest modes can be easily percieved as peaks and nulls in frequency response. The larger the room, the lower all these modes are in frequency; when you reach a large enough size, all the wider-spaced nodes are below the audible range and modes cease to be audible. Very few of us on this board will have the $$$ to build rooms large enough that this happens; I'm temporarily out of time for now, but I'll post a wrap-up on this either later tonight or tomorrow; if for some reason I forget I said that, please "bump" the thread and remind me; this is an important topic that's not been covered here much... Steve

knightfly14 October 2004#22
No, the side absorbers are fine; their purpose with the slanted faces is to redirect early reflections away from the mix position WITHOUT deadening the room more than necessary. The only change I'd make is to make EACH one 4 feet wide with ONE continuous angle, and 10-12 inches difference in depth across that 4 foot span - this will work better to kill the less audible effects of flutter echo, and still act as a part of your RFZ for mixing. If you take another look at that drawing, you'll see that I only said that about the rear corner absorbers; the front corners still need broadband absorbers (no slats) in most rooms If your room is 20 feet long, you're getting close to being able to use diffusion at the rear (or V-shaped slat absorbers) if you want the room brighter - generally it's only recommended when you can have more than about 12 feet from the back of your head to the back wall; less than that, and the sound doesn't really get diffuse enough not to still have specular reflections, which make head positioning pretty crucial... Steve

knightfly17 October 2004#25
Drums generally do better in their own space that can be tailored more to their needs; for example, tuning a fairly large slat resonator to 300 hZ in a drum room is almost always a good thing; that's the shell resonance range for 22-24" kick drums, so that 300 hZ trap will help keep the kick drum from exciting room modes at that range. If the room is multipurpose, you need to stay away from narrow band treatments like slats and panel resonators, unless you have enough room for several that are tuned differently. When in doubt, I would stick to more broadband treatments... Steve
