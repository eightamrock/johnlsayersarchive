Husky, What you are suggesting will do the trick only in reference to directly beneath your area. Air borne sound will make it back into your space via the rest of the open ceiling as it searches for the path of least resistance. It might help if you did as you are suggesting, and then make certain that you get this past the framed wall boundaries of your room above and mass up the cavity, floor to attempt to mitigate the sound from moving in between the panels. At the very least use acoustical caulk where your sheetrock ends to seal the perimeter.

Soundman20208 March 2013#8
ok, so your take is that 18" of fiberglass (in a 16" cavity) will not be an improvement over a single layer of 9" insulation?
Exactly. There might be a slight increase, but not much. You would be getting into the area of "diminishing returns". The insulation does nothing to stop the sound getting through: it is merely there to damp the resonance inside the wall, and that will be taken care of by the 9". This is a very important function (damping resonance), but 9" will do the job. Adding the other 9" would only help a little extra, and probably isn't worth it.
i'd imagine 'more is more' in most cases here, right?
Yes, but it gets to the point where adding one extra inch does not have anywhere near the same effect as adding the first inch. With each additional inch, the added effect is lower and lower. I mean, you sure CAN fill the entire space, if fire code allows you to do that, but there isn't a lot of justification, acoustically, for doing so.
not sure i can get away with two layers of drywall with gaps.. might have to go for a dual layer drywall situation with no gaps - since this is not in my space, but below me, and i'm essentially making a deal with the tenant downstairs to let me in there to insulate before he moves his business in there. two layers with airgap might be too time consuming.
I'm not sure what you mean by "with air gaps". The space where you will be adding insulation IS the air gap! That is the one and only air gap that you need. Adding additional air gaps between the layers of drywall below will make things WORSE, not better. I think you misunderstood what Brien was saying. The two layers of drywall go directly on top of each other, no air gaps. And if you will have access to the room below, then do yourself a huge favor (and your neighbor too); Like Brien said, put resilient channel across the joists, and hang the two layers of drywall from that. Or even better, use RSIC clips and hat channel. Either of those options will decouple the drywall from the joists, which greatly increases the isolation. That is probably the single best thing you can do here: decouple with resilient mountings. - Stuart -

Hat channel, but NOT by itself! Hat channel looks very similar to resilient channel, and is probably cheaper, but it is NOT the same, and will not do the job... by itself, You can us hat channel (also called "furring channel") PROVIDED THAT you attach it using RSIC clips. Like this: http://www.acousticalsurfaces.com/rsic_ ... _clips.htm http://www.soundproofingcompany.com/ins ... -overview/ So if you use hat channel, you MUST also use the RSIC clips to get the decoupling. So, you can either use resilient channel by itself, or you can use hat channel plus RSIC clips. - Stuart -
xSpace9 March 2013#11
If you use RC, it has a load requirement of 5 PSF, as best as I can tell and the insulation should not rest on the sheetrock. Also, read the instructions and do some research on RC installation. You can very well short circuit this material with one ill placed screw...screw since nails are not recommended at all. The material runs perpendicular to the joists, that means at a 90 degree right angle and there are spacing specifications based on the existing layout of your joists and the thickness of sheetrock you are wanting to add.
