What will you be recording? Will you be recording any acoustic drums? So far it seems you have a single leaf system, which is definitely going to suck by any test, noisy metal pot with large metal spoon or otherwise. Fortunately, your outer leaf of 5/8" plywood and 7/8" stucco is not a bad start for an outer leaf mass. 8) The closed cell spray on foam isn't doing anything for you in terms of mass, but it may be helping in terms of resonance, which is definitely something you need to control for good isolation. (That's why Green Glue is so great.) The specific advice you'll get about what to do about constructing an effective inner leaf will depend on a number of factors: how loud you intend to be on the inside (i.e., whether or not rock acoustic drums are involved!), how quiet you hope to be on the outside, how much money you have to spend, how much square footage inside the building you're willing/able to sacrifice, etc. On the high end, you're looking at building a separate, isolated inner leaf assembly and installing multiple layers of drywall onto that, possibly with Green Glue at which you have already hinted. So, what is this building, anyway? Is it a detached garage or something? Are there windows or any other types of openings/penetrations/utilities that need to be addressed? The cross-section drawing looks great -- one of the cleanest hand-drawn ones I've seen here. 8) Now, a top-view layout of the whole building and what's around it would very helpful. sharward19 October 2006#4
Wow, Dublin -- you're in Dan's neck of the woods. 8) You are correct to question the wisdom of staggered stud construction in your scenario. Years ago I heard about staggered studs and thought it was the ultimate construction method to achieve great isolation and would be plenty sufficient for isolating drums. Since then I've learned a lot, mostly here, and the fact is, when you need to isolate low end frequencies, the mass on each leaf of a two leaf system is just as important as the width of the air gap between them. It seems that in your build, you are at a pivotal point. You have an opportunity to make improvements to your plan without changing too much of what you've already got in place. How open are you to sacrificing those inner staggered studs, and instead, building new inner leaf wall frames? Is this residential or commercial? Codes and noise regulations tend to be very different between them. Have you pulled permits? Is your roof "just as beefy" as your stucco walls? I somehow doubt it... :roll: ...If it's just one layer of sheathing and asphault composition shingles, you've got yourself a roof that won't keep up with your walls -- the "weakest link" effect. Will your attic be vented? If so, those holes, while necessary for venting and prevention of heat and moisture build-up, will wreak havoc on your isolation. One way out of this pickle might be to have a three leaves overhead -- a relatively wimpy roof atop a liberally vented attic (which, as I understand it, offers some mitigation of the third leaf effect), then two leaves below that -- middle leaf firmly attached to outer structure, and lower leaf attached to separately framed inner leaf wall frames, making a true "box in a box" design. Budget...? Have you bought Rod's book yet? (Hint: Only socially and intelligently acceptable answers are "Yes, I have it," "Yes, I ordered it and I'm awaiting shipment," or "No, but I'm buying it within the next sixty seconds." ;-)) --Keith :mrgreen:

Only 80% over? ;-) Aw, man -- you're going great! :P According to my trusty calculator, that means you're running about 30% under! :lol: But seriously... I realize money's tight. I kind of have a reputation around here for my propensity towards overkill. In your case, I'm genuinely concerned for you. The spray foam will do very little for you isolationwise, except maybe sealing everything up and possibly absorbing some resonance. Unfortunately, you've shot yourself in the foot somewhat by making it impossible to add mass against the underside of the roof sheathing between the joists (what we usually call "beef-up")... :roll: ...And your shingles are already installed, so adding a second layer of sheathing isn't very practical at this point. :? That's why I'm continuing to lean towards recommending that you have three leaves overhead -- one wimpy roof leaf that is liberally vented (for heat reduction, moisture build-up prevention, and mitigation of the dreaded "third leaf effect"), a beefy middle leaf firmly attached to the outer framing structure (provided you can afford the extra weight on the structure -- make no assumptions here!! :!:), and then an inner leaf ceiling that is firmly attached to new joists atop a new inner leaf frame assembly. If you do this, you won't need RC anywhere, because you will have already decoupled inner and outer(/middle) leaves, which is what RC is meant to simulate. You'd also be increasing the air gap between your leaves in your mass-air-mass equation, which is critical for low end transmission loss (TL). I'm making this recommendation based on what I presume are your needs for major isolation as you'll be tracking acoustic drums in a residential area with neighbors only 20 feet away. (By the way, noise ordinances typically call for measuring at the property line, so perhaps your neighboring property is closer than 20 feet...? :roll:)

sharward22 October 2006#9
OK... So, no venting is required for your original plan for a cathedral-type ceiling. However, at this point I'm sticking with my recommendation to vent that space liberally, attach a multi-layer sealed drywall "middle leaf" firmly to your existing overhead framing, and then framing a new inner leaf thats not connected to the middle leaf at all. I realize your existing rough electrical will be impacted by that. How many lines are affected? Worst case -- you rerun them. To answer your "what insulation should I get?" question -- it's proably premature at this point... But I'm a big believer of Thermafiber Sound Attenuation Fire Blankets (SAFB) - Nominal Density (2.5 pcf) and Roxul Acoustical Fire Batt (AFB) - ≥2" (2.5 pcf). They perform a bit better for sound isolation than fluffy pink fiberglass, and their fire resistance properties are stellar (withstanding about 2,000 degrees Fahrehneit, vs. about 1,200 for fiberglass. I just paid 55 cents per square foot for 3" batts of Thermafiber myself. That being said, many folks here are satisfied with the pink stuff. Back to the ceiling... One thing that occurred to me is that if you proceed with your original plan, I really think your roof will be your weakest link, and the only way to address it at that point would probably be to rip all the shingles off, add more plywood sheathing, and then reshingle it. :( Even that would perform worse than the changes I have proposed. :roll: So far nobody else has weighed in. I kind of wish someone would, because I acknowledge my ideas are costly and I want to make sure I'm not overreacting or missing an easier method to improve your outcome... :roll: --Keith :mrgreen:
knightfly23 October 2006#10
From the last pic, I'm sorry you came here AFTER the budget overruns, likely (from my few inquiries into spray foam) caused by the very foam that's now causing you to have to make difficult and expensive decisions :cry: ) At this point, if you want more than mediochre sound isolation the only thing I could recommend (loading being acceptable) is, as Keith mentioned, remove roofing and add another layer of 3/4" OSB or similar to the outside, put the roofing back on (couple layers of 30# felt under it wouldn't hurt), then get some RSIC clips and hat channel and float your inner ceiling leaf, using either Keith's favorite thermafiber or plain, UN-faced fiberglass batts to damp the inner drywall layers - 2 layers of 5/8 with Green Glue would be the lightest choice that would get you decent results. The closed cell foam is already gonna be your vapor barrier, and it's NOT in the right place if you heat more than you cool the space; should be toward the INSIDE of the space in that case. However, even worse is to have TWO vapor barriers (kraft paper facing on insulation is a vapor barrier) so you're kinda stuck. Otherwise you could end up trapping moisture between the ceiling/roof layers, and that would REALLY suck. If you follow the above plan, your inner walls should extend almost to the ceiling joists but only be connected by sway braces where necessary, and then your inner ceiling should be suspended INSIDE these walls so NOWHERE do you have inner and outer leaves with hard contact. One other alternative - if you were to be able to use THREE layers of wallboard on the inside with GG between each, the net result between that heavy leaf and your (presently) lighter outer leaf (the roof) should be very similar WITHOUT the need for demo-ing a perfectly good roof... Steve
