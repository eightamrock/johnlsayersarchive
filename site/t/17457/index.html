You could start with John's SAE recording manual, here: http://johnlsayers.com/Recmanual/index.htm That's a good start, but it's oldish and there is a LOT more updated stuff here on the forum, so you need both.
Some things I need to know are what types of insulation to use for sound proofing,
Well, that's actually a myth! You cannot soundproof a room with insulation. Insulation is for treatment, for damping, for absorption, but it is impossible to soundproof a room with any reasonable amount of insulation. Even the term "soundproof" is not really accurate: there is no such thing as "soundproofing". The more correct technical term is "isolation". Insulation does not isolate. Insulation treats and damps. Insulation can be used as part of an isolation system, yes, but by itself does not isolate. What you need for isolation is mass. Lots of it. The only way to stop sound is to put something in the way that the sound waves cannot move. Something rigid, hard, dense and massive. Insulation is not rigid, not hard, nor dense, and not massive. Myth #1 rolls over and dies.
what kind of paint for the interior,
Whatever kind you like! :) There's not much acoustics to paint, fortunately. Paint can used to help seal isolation systems, but there's nothing special about it. Just any good quality paint that seals porous surfaces, and looks good.
carpeting,
None at all! That's another myth. Carpet has practically no uses, acoustically. Take a look around the great studios of this world, and so how many you can find that are carpeted.... Hmmmm... none. There's a reason for that. So forget carpet: the acoustic properties of carpet are not only no use for studios, they are in fact the exact opposite of what small rooms need. So putting ordinary carpet in your room will make it sound worse, not better. Myth #2 shot down in flames.
tiling,
You mean floor tiling? That's fine. Ceramic tiles actually make pretty good floors, acoustically, provided that the rest of the room is treated corrrectly.
ceiling height,
As high as possible! Sound likes space to "breath" in, so increasing room volume is always good, and since most rooms have low ceilings, a really good way of making a small room sound good is to move the ceiling higher.
right angles,
As preferred. You can build a great rehearsal room as a pure rectangle, or you can build a great rehearsal room with no two surfaces even close to parallel. Angled walls can help acoustics, if used correctly, but you can also build a room with parallel walls, if you treat it correctly. So it's more personal preference than anything else.
building rooms next to each other (sharing a wall),
Build each room as a stand-alone structure inside the outer shell of the building (each room has its own 4 walls and ceiling. They can share the floor, if it is a good concrete slab). That gives you correct 2-leaf isolation all around, in all directions.
the basic design idea and what generally is best overall (for example a double wall construction or not )
If by "double wall" you mean fully decoupled 2-leaf MSM construction, then yes, that is the best possible way to get good isolation at low cost.

I plan to build two rehearsal rooms the first being 16' x 16' within the interior, and another 22' x 22'.
That would be two big mistakes! Rooms should NEVER be square. No two dimensions should be the same. If you have two dimension of a room that are the same, or even close (within about 5% to 10% of each other) then the room modes (standing waves) associated with those dimensions will line up perfectly, and you'll have major problems with the room. It will seem to powerfully amplify certain notes, while sucking the life out of other notes, and that will change depending on where you stand in the room! So do not, ever, build a room where two of the dimensions are the same, or close. The only room worse than a square, is a cube. The dimensions should also not be multiples of each pother, for the same reason. So if you have an 8 foot ceiling, do not make the room 16 feet wide by 24 long! So change the dimensions of your rooms to get away from those kinds of ratios.
The space I'm using is an open floorplan with concrete floor.
Great! Cncrete floor is excellent, for both isolation between rooms, and also for acoustics. If the concrete is in good shape, then you could even use it as-is for your flooring. Nothing else required. But you can lay ceramic tiles if you want, as you already mentioned. Or laminate floor. Or hardwood flooring. Or anything solid and reflective like that. so what about the rest of the building? Walls, ceiling, roof, doors, windows, HVAC. All of that is extremely important for your isolation plan.
I want to learn more about the things mentioned above before moving onto that point.
If you really do want to learn about this stuff in greater detail than Rod's book, then the other book we always recommend is "Master Handbook of Acoustics" (abbreviated "MHoA") by F. Alton Everest. That's kind of the "bible" of acoustics and studio building. Rod's book is more practically oriented, MHoA is more theoretical. Rod's book is "how", MHoA is "why". Together, they'll teach you all you need to know. Or you can read the entire forum, and find the same information here, for free! :)
Or maybe an already made design might be okay too.
That would be a bad idea. All rooms are different, all needs are different. There's no "cookie cutter" approach to building studios. each one should be designed and built for the specific purpose. But you are in the right place to learn how to do that! - Stuart -
