Soundman20204 December 2013#2
What we are wondering is whether to build the wall straight, and add acoustic treatment later, or to angle the wall to improve the acoustics of the room?
That depends somewhat on which design philosophy you are going to follow for your control room. If you want to use a true RFZ design, then angling your side walls is a must. Or at least angling the front half of the side walls. It can be advantageous for CID and NER designs too, and even for plain old LEDE, to a certain extent. But if you don't want to go with any of those, and just want a basic low-end control room without the benefits of those more advanced acoustic concepts, then you can just leave it rectangular, and treat accordingly. However, regardless of which design philosophy you decide to follow, you should always start out with room dimensions that are close to one of the good ratios, such as Sepmeyer, Louden, or Bolt, and stay far away from the ratios that are known to be terrible for acoustics. Such as a square room.... :) Right now yours is practically square, meaning that all your room modes in the width axis will line up with all the modes in the length axis, making them twice as loud and also leaving large gaps between modal frequencies. So I'd suggest that you should adjust the position of that wall until you get a good ratio, independent of which design philosophy you decide to use. Right now you show one wall at 4.4m, and another at 4.5. That's practically square! If the height also happens to be close to 2.25m, then you have pretty much the worst possible scenario....
As we are building the wall from scratch I don't want to miss a trick and create more work for ourselves fixing acoustic issues we created ourselves later on.
The room is small, so it will need a lot of acoustic treatment anyway, mostly in the form of bass trapping, regardless of how you angle the walls. Angling helps (considerably!) for different reasons, depending on the design philosophy, but it won't make a lot of difference in terms of total room treatment.
I have read differing views on this.
Right! It all depends on which design concept you follow. There are pros and cons with all of them. That's just part of the entire studio design thing: since "small room" and "great acoustics" don't go together, designing a studio is really all about compromises. There are many, many things that can be adjusted in a studio to improve one aspect, but doing so practically always has a detrimental effect on another aspect. So design is all about finding the best compromise between all of the factors. - Stuart -
