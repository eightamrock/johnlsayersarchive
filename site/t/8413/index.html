This system hasn't taken on and only a few studios following that design these days. drfrankencopter22 March 2007#10
Wall angles will not prevent standing waves...they will prevent flutter echo. Standing waves, are just teh natural frequencies at which anything resonates/vibrates, and all rooms will have them no matter what. Non-rectangular rooms will have less predictable standing waves than rectangular ones, but they will still have them. Short story...non-parallel walls do not reduce the requirement for bass trapping, they jsut reduce the requirement to handle the high frequency flutter issues. Cheers. Kris

You're welcome to beleive whatever you wish...however I'd suggest doing a little more research on standing waves. You'll see that there is no possible way that a change in room shape can prevent standing waves. Please don't take my word for it...maybe read some books by Alton Everest, he usually covers it. As analogy, take a thin peice of plywood cut into a rectangle, cantelever it and strike it. Notice it's resonate vibration mode. Now cut some material out of it...change it's shape. Strike it, and notice that it still vibrates in specific modes (ie. standing waves)...but they're just different than the rectangulat peice. Wall angles affect high frequency flutter...which is also easy to control with diffusion and absorbtion. Thinking that wall angles will reduce requirements to manage bass buildup (i.e trapping) is false economy. Cheers, Kris
John Sayers26 March 2007#14
Kris is correct - it stops flutter echoes between two parallel surfaces - As far as standing waves are concerned they do force the standing wave to form via a different path, but it still forms. yes - diffusors and absorbers can stop the flutter echoes but only from around 250hz up. You can easily check if your room has standing waves by listening to the bass while moving around the room. If you have standing waves the frequency response will change from strong bass to weak bass in different positions in the room. You are then standing in the nodes (boost) and antinodes ( weak ). If you have low end standing waves due to parallel walls covered with absorbers or diffusors you won't hear them like the high end flutter echoes, but they will be there. So you place a snare or kick mike right in a low end room antinode and wonder why there's no bottom end. In a good control room there should be no change in response and the bass should be even throughout the room. I've found that using around 12 degrees in the sides of my control rooms helps. cheers john
drfrankencopter26 March 2007#15
I think John that you'd agree that in order to get even bass response throughout a room that trapping of some sort will be required, regardless of the shape of the room. Angling the control room walls is a good idea if you can afford the space. It sure makes it easier to set up a RFZ at the main mix position. Bass trapping will still be necessary to prevent holes/peaks in the low freq response. Positioning of these traps will not be as straightforward as it would be in a rectangular room (because you can't determine analytically where the modes will terminate), but the usuall general rules will apply (e.g. corners will need extra trapping). Cheers, Kris
