showed it to a friend who works in sound and has a small studio in his home. He recommended to avoid parallel walls.
That's a common misconception. There are only three basic reasons for having splayed walls: to eliminate flutter (which can also be dealt with in other ways), to create an RFZ style control room (in which case they need to be angled a lot for a small room), or because you like the way they look. There aren't any other acoustic reasons for splaying walls. But people still think that you have to do it, because they see it all the time in photos of high-end studios that are based on RFZ or CID concepts... So, you can angle your walls if you want (it really DOES look cool!), or not, as you prefer. A rectangular room with parallel walls can be a great studio, and so can one with non-parallel walls.
So, I've searched the forum and found that while some people say that you SHOULD avoid them, others say you can a have a rectangular room if you treat the walls accordingly.
Exactly! :)
So I want a space that fits the musicians and gear in a comfortable way, trying to keep the shape of the room as simple as possible.
In that case, I'd go with parallel walls, or just angle a small section of the room at the front, where your desk and speakers are, while leaving the rest parallel. That's the best of both.
The space between the real and fake walls is 100 mm (in the floorplan I omitted this space,
You should show everything in the SketchUp model, as accurately as possible. It helps to make sure that things really will work out as you hope. But I'm not sure what you mean by "fake walls". A studio wall is usually a 2-leaf MSM wall, where there is an "inner-leaf", which is the leaf that surrounds the room itself and is the hard acoustic boundary of the room, and an "outer-leaf", which is usually the inner-leaf of the next room, or the actual outside wall of the building. There are no fake walls.
1- Would there be any significative changes from changing the design on the first image to the second?
Yes! Major, important changes. The control room MUSt be symmetrical, such that the left half is a mirror image of the right half. With your first image, that simply is not true, so that is not a usable setup. Also, the speakers should fire down the longest dimension of the room, never across the shortest dimension (unless the room is very big), so here too the first image is no use. The second one is the way to do it.
2- If there is, or there could be an improvement from image 1 to 2, is the second design even more improvable?
I would suggest trying to make the control room bigger: it seems to be too small for good acoustics. Perhaps if you turned it 90° to the left it would be better. There is also the problem of access: the way it is now, everyone MUST walk through the CR to get to the LR, and all equipment, instruments, people and luggage have to go through the CR to get there, through 2 sets of doors, and interrupting any mixing session. IT would be better to have the main entrance into the LR, with access from there into the CR.
3- In the 3rd image I emulated a possible instrument location. According to this, should the walls have carpeting, acoustic foam, acoustic panels, diffusers? Where?
Never carpet. It has the exact wrong acoustic properties for treating small rooms. Yes, you occasionally do see "carpet on the walls" in some studios and cinemas, but in reality that carpet is NOT the treatment: it is simply aesthetic decoration that is hiding the REAL treatment, behind it. Carpet is lousy for treatment. You could use absorption panels in there, or perhaps a slot wall, but most likely not diffusion: that only works in large rooms. It might be possible for the LR; but certainly not in the CR.
4- I have no clue of what is the best option on how to do the ceiling, or what to do on it.
Basic rule for studios: Hard floor, soft ceiling. Make the floor hard and reflective, such as concrete, laminate, ceramic tiles, linoleum, solid wood, etc. Those are all excellent acoustic floors. Then make the ceiling absorptive mostly, as needed to reach the design goals.
