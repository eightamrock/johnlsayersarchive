For that kind of problem, I'd start by doing a proper analysis of the room as it is right now, with REW or something similar, to find out where your real issues are, before you start treating things that might not need it, and not treating things that do. That said, just putting panels on the wall wont necessarily make you guys any quieter! It will only help if there is an acoustic issue with the room itself, but not if the underlying problem is that you are too loud. The only thing that will tell you that for sure, is to measure yourselves with a real sound level meter, to see how loud you are. There might also be structural issues going on, in the sense that the drums and bass are pumping vibration directly into the building itself. It's hard to see from the video, but it looks like the drums are on a riser of some sort? If so, is it an properly designed fully decoupled acoustic riser? Or just some bits of stuff thrown together to lift the drums a bit higher? A good drum riser and a pad for the bass cab could do wonders to keep that kind of flanking issue out of the picture. But I'd start by measuring: both yourselves, to see how loud you are, and the room, to see where the problems lie. - Stuart -

what I can tell just from listening in the room is that there are plenty of noticeable reflections and I'm sure that has something to do with the owners wife saying how loud it sounds to her.
OK, that makes sense: If you can hear echoes, reverb and resonance, then you do have acoustic issues in there, and even though adding people will help to some extent, you'd need to put an awful lot of people in there to make a big difference! (Most of them would have to be standing on the stage, too... ) I'm gonna go out on a limb here, and suggest stuff without having seen any analysis of what the room is actually doing, but what the hell, I'm feeling lucky tonight! :) (And because I like your music...) So lets look at your options: The drum riser and base cab pad: Easy and cheap. A basic, very simple drum riser can just be something as easy as a 4 inch layer of semi-rigid fiberglass insulation laid on the floor, with a sheet of plywood on top. OK that's not too safe, but you get the idea. Use the "search" feature on the forum for "drum riser" and you should find a few simple, cheap designs. Ditto for the bass cab: some manufacturers of acoustic products sell read-made pads for that (some also sell drum riser kits), but once again you can make your own, exactly like a small drum riser. Where is the bass cab, by the way? I but don't see it in there... Do that, and you will at least be sure that you are not pouring energy into the building structure itself. That takes care of structure-borne sound, leaving only air-borne sound. From the video, it looks like you guys are up close to a wall: What is that wall made of? I'm betting it is hard and reflective. If so, I'd start by putting some thick absorption over that. Maybe 6" of the same semi-rigid fiberglass panels (commonly called "Owens Corning 703", available at most good construction supply stores)., and space it away from the wall by several inches. Put it in wooden frames, covered with pretty much any kind of thin cloth that you happen to like. Speaker grill cloth would be great (but not cheap!). Cover as much of that wall as you can afford to. You could make a few modules and angle them a bit (bigger air gap behind on one side than the other) for aesthetics. Next, you seem to have a window over to the right: hang heavy drapes in front of that. Shine psychedelic lights on it artistically (and also on the modules behind you), to make it look pretty: make a nice backdrop! Also, get that speaker out of the corner! I'm guess that's for the keyboard? Vocals too? The corner is NOT a good place for it: All room modes end in corners, and with the speaker right there, you are probably exciting all of them: Figure out a way to keep the speaker far from corners and walls. That way, you won't be triggering the natural response of the room itself so much. What about the ceiling? What is that made of? If that too is hard and reflective, then follow the same procedure as for the rear wall: thick 703, spaced away from it a bit. The rest of the room isn't visible in that video, so hard to say what to do about it, but at least with that basic treatment you'll be controlling some of the sound at its sources. If you want to convince the owner (and his wife) that this will work, just go "borrow" a dozen or so panels of 703 from your local building supply store, and lean them up against the rear wall and window, at an angle. Bring a sheet of thick plywood too, and do the "el cheapo" drum riser, too. If it doesn't work, take it all back to the store for a refund, and you lost nothing. :) If it DOES work (it will) then you can talk to the boss from a more authoritative position, and get him to splurge to build a proper drum riser, frames for the panels, and also money for the rest of the treatment. This "dozen panels of 703" won't make a huge difference, but it should be enough to be noticeable and make your point. Worth a try, I reckon! - Stuart -

Soundman20202 September 2011#9
Anyway, do you think 4" of 703 (or similar) with a 4" space behind it would be effective behind the band?
Well, it's better than nothing at all! If that's what you can do, then that's what you can do. You might want to build these things as movable "panels", kind of like a gobo. Just a frame with the insulation inside and cloth across the front. Build several of them, and set them up across the back of the stage, but angle some of them just a bit (artistically!) so that the depth of the gap behind them varies. For example, set them up in a kind of "sawtooth" pattern, where one panel angles out slightly, and the next angles back in again- The exact depth behind isn't that important, as long as it is at least 4". So you could angle one panel to get 4" depth behind the left edge, and 8" behind the right edge, and angle the next panel the other way. Or some such. Or even "double-up" in places: put some panels in FRONT of other panels, for even greater depth (especially behind the drums, for example!). If you do build them as movable panels, just make sure that they have firm, steady bases: you don't want them falling over on top of you during the performance! :)
Can we get away with less space behind the panels? Let me put it another way. What would be the most efficient approach to treat the area behind the band, in terms of saving space and maybe money?
As you move the panels closer to the wall they become less effective: Move them away from the wall, and they become MORE effective, and treat down to lower frequencies. Think about what happens when a sound wave hits a wall and bounces back: OK, better: think about how a soccer ball hits a wall and bounces back, in slow-motion: As the first edge of the ball just touches the wall, the ball is still moving at its original speed, but as it "squashes up" to the wall, it both flattens out and slows down very fast. As it flattens out, the air pressure inside the ball rises very fast, and the speed of the ball drops very fast. At some point the speed is zero, the pressure is maximum, and things start going backwards. The pressure gets converted back to speed as the ball starts "unflattening" and accelerating away again. As the ball returns to its original shape, the air pressure drops back down to normal again, and the ball speeds up back to its original speed again. Sound waves kind of do the same thing: as they approach the wall they slow down and their pressure increases. Right at they wall the pressure component is maximum, and the speed is zero. Then as they move away from the wall again, the pressure drops and the speed increases. Insulation works on the speed component of the wave, NOT the pressure component. So since the speed is zero right at the wall, there is nothing for it to work on! So insulation up tight against the wall isn't much use. It has to be spaced away from the wall, where it can work on the speed of the wave, not the pressure of the wave. So the question is: how far away? Well, sound waves are also unlike soccer balls, because they come in different sizes, or rather different "frequencies". Soccer balls only come in one size, but sound waves come in various "sizes". You probably know that there is a direct relationship between frequency and wavelength: high frequencies are short, lows are long. So at one end of the spectrum, you have waves less than an inch long, and at the other end you have waves more than 50 feet long: So you have tiny soccer balls the size or marbles, and huge ones that you couldn't even fit in your whole house. Obviously, for the little tiny (high frequency) waves, they are smaller than the thickness of your insulation, so it doesn't matter for them: even with the insulation right up against the wall, they still feel the full effect, because they are so small in relation to the size of the treatment. But those 50-foot waves? Well, clearly they are HUGE in comparison to the 4" thickness of the treatment, so they won't even see it up against the wall: to them, it just isn't there. Doesn't even exist. It turns out that the important measurement here is 1/4 wavelength: That's where the speed is highest and the pressure lowest, for any given wave so that's the best place to put the treatment. So for a 50 foot wave that makes... umm... about 12 feet! Not gonna happen! that's why it's really hard to treat a room for very low frequencies with absopriton alone... If you put your treatment 4 inches from the wall, you have effective treatment for wavelengths of 16 inches and shorter, which is about 800 Hz. Put it at 6" and you cover down to 500 Hz. 8" takes you down to about 400 Hz: etc. So there you have your answer: move it closer to the wall, and you stop treating low frequencies. Put it right against the wall, and all you are doing is taking the edge off some highs (above 4 kHz.).
I was thinking 2" 703 with no space behind. Good enough?
See above! :) OK, it isn't totally as drastic as it sounds from above: that assumes that all waves hit the panel head-on, at a 90° angle. Most don't. As the angle gets smaller, and they hit it more of a grazing trajectory, the effective distance to the wall is LONGER, which is good. So lower frequencies do get treated, if they hit at a sloping angle, instead of head on. In fact, in realty it turns out that if you space it roughly 10% of the wavelength from the wall then you get decent effect. But a spacing of zero is still not much use! - Stuart -
